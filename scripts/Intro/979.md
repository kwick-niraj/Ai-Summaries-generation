## Introduction

Survival and memory intertwine in the aftermath of unimaginable trauma, where the boundaries between victim and oppressor blur and the struggle to make sense of suffering becomes a lifelong endeavor. The Drowned and the Saved by Primo Levi delves into the haunting legacy of the Holocaust, exploring the psychological and moral complexities faced by those who endured the concentration camps. The weight of memory, the mechanisms of denial, and the challenge of bearing witness all emerge as central themes, raising profound questions about human nature, responsibility, and the limits of understanding in the face of atrocity.

Within this landscape, the voices of both the drowned—those who perished or were spiritually destroyed—and the saved—those who survived, often at great personal cost—echo with urgency. Levi’s reflections move beyond simple narratives of good and evil, instead illuminating the gray zones of complicity, the fragility of dignity, and the enduring scars left by systematic dehumanization. The search for meaning, the burden of testimony, and the ongoing struggle to communicate the reality of the camps to those who did not experience them form a powerful meditation on memory, ethics, and the resilience of the human spirit, inviting a deeper contemplation of history’s darkest chapters.
