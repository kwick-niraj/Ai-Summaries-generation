## Introduction

The Revolution Betrayed by Leon Trotsky plunges into the heart of the Soviet experiment, grappling with the fate of a revolution that once promised liberation and equality. The ideals of 1917—workers’ control, social justice, and the end of oppression—stand in stark contrast to the reality that emerged under Stalin’s rule. The tension between revolutionary hope and bureaucratic consolidation forms a powerful undercurrent, raising urgent questions about the nature of power, the fragility of progress, and the forces that can twist even the most radical dreams into new forms of domination. Trotsky’s perspective, shaped by his own role as a leader and later as an exile, brings a sense of immediacy and personal stake to the analysis of how revolutions can be both made and unmade.

As the narrative unfolds, the transformation of the Soviet Union is examined through the lens of economic policy, social structure, and the shifting dynamics of class and leadership. The rise of bureaucracy, the suppression of dissent, and the erosion of democratic institutions are not just historical events but living warnings about the dangers inherent in any revolutionary project. The Revolution Betrayed invites reflection on the paradoxes of progress, the costs of compromise, and the enduring struggle to align ideals with reality, setting the stage for a deeper exploration of the forces that shape societies in moments of profound change.
