## Introduction

A sense of duty, trust, and the weight of history converge in the world of those tasked with protecting the most powerful individuals. The assassination of President John F. Kennedy remains one of the most scrutinized tragedies in American history, not only for its political and cultural impact but also for the lingering questions about the men charged with his safety. Survivor's Guilt: The Secret Service and the Failure to Protect President Kennedy by Vincent Michael Palamara delves into the complex web of responsibility, regret, and institutional vulnerability that surrounded the Secret Service on that fateful day in Dallas. The narrative explores the human side of security, where split-second decisions and unseen pressures can alter the course of a nation.

Through a meticulous examination of firsthand accounts, official protocols, and the personal reflections of agents, the story unfolds as a study of both individual and collective conscience. The emotional aftermath—marked by guilt, self-doubt, and public scrutiny—reveals how those who survived the event grappled with their roles and the expectations placed upon them. The journey through these perspectives not only illuminates the operational failures and oversights but also invites a deeper understanding of the psychological toll borne by those in the shadows of power. As the layers of secrecy and accountability are peeled back, a portrait emerges of an institution and its people forever changed by a single, devastating moment.
