## Introduction

The enduring presence of prisons often goes unquestioned, woven into the fabric of modern society as a necessary response to crime and disorder. Yet, beneath this apparent inevitability lies a complex history of social control, racial injustice, and shifting political priorities. Angela Y. Davis, in Are Prisons Obsolete?, challenges the deeply held assumption that incarceration is the only or best solution to harm and conflict. The conversation expands beyond the walls and bars, inviting a critical look at how prisons have evolved, whose interests they serve, and what alternatives might exist in a world that aspires to genuine justice.

Tracing the roots of the prison system, the narrative uncovers the intersections of race, gender, and class that have shaped its development. The exploration moves through the rise of the prison-industrial complex, the impact on marginalized communities, and the global spread of punitive models. By questioning the very necessity of prisons, the discussion opens space for imagining new forms of accountability and safety. The journey through these ideas encourages a reexamination of what true justice could look like, setting the stage for a deeper understanding of abolitionist perspectives and the possibilities for transformative change.
