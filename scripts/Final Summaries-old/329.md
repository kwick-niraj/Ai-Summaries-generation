## 1. Arriving in a World Apart: First Impressions and Everyday Life

Stepping off the plane in Pyongyang, the first thing that strikes a newcomer isn’t the silence, but the sense of being watched. The airport is small, almost sleepy, and the air feels heavy with expectation. For most outsiders, North Korea is a place of mystery, a country defined by headlines and grainy images. But for someone arriving to live and work here, the reality is far more complex and human than any news report can capture.

The first days are a blur of unfamiliar routines. There’s the ever-present minders—officials who shadow every move, sometimes with a smile, sometimes with a stern glance. The city itself is a study in contrasts: wide boulevards lined with imposing monuments, but also quiet corners where children play, and families gather for picnics. The streets are clean, almost spotless, but the shops are sparsely stocked, and electricity flickers on and off without warning.

Settling into daily life means learning to read between the lines. A foreigner quickly notices that conversations are careful, words chosen with precision. At first, it’s easy to mistake this for coldness, but over time, it becomes clear that people are simply cautious. Trust is something built slowly, over shared meals and small acts of kindness.

One evening, a local colleague invites a foreign guest to his home. The apartment is modest, but the welcome is warm. The family serves what they have—rice, kimchi, a little meat saved for special occasions. There’s laughter, stories about children’s schoolwork, and even a few jokes about the power cuts. In these moments, the rigid image of North Korea softens, revealing a society where people care deeply for their loved ones and find joy in small pleasures.

Yet, the shadow of the state is never far away. Every home displays portraits of the country’s leaders, and every conversation is shaped by an awareness of what can and cannot be said. Foreigners are reminded to avoid sensitive topics, to steer clear of politics, and to respect the rules—unwritten as much as official.

Navigating the city means adapting to its rhythms. Buses and trams run on their own schedule, and walking is often the best way to get around. The city’s architecture is a mix of grand Soviet-style buildings and utilitarian apartment blocks. At dawn, groups of workers gather for collective exercises, moving in unison as music blares from loudspeakers. It’s a scene that feels both choreographed and deeply ingrained.

For many, the hardest adjustment is the sense of isolation. Internet access is almost nonexistent, international calls are tightly controlled, and news from the outside world arrives in fragments. Letters from home take weeks to arrive, if they come at all. In this environment, friendships with local colleagues become lifelines, offering moments of connection and understanding.

Over time, the initial sense of strangeness fades, replaced by a growing appreciation for the resilience and resourcefulness of the people. Despite the hardships—food shortages, power outages, and constant surveillance—life goes on. Children go to school, couples marry, and neighbors help each other in ways that rarely make headlines.

Living in North Korea means learning to see beyond the surface. It’s a place where appearances matter, but where genuine warmth and humanity can be found in the most unexpected places. The first lesson is simple: nothing is ever quite as it seems, and every day brings new surprises.

## 2. Building a Business in a Closed Economy: Challenges and Surprises

Starting a business anywhere is a leap of faith, but in North Korea, it’s more like stepping into the unknown. The rules are different, the risks are higher, and the rewards—if they come—are hard-won. For a foreign entrepreneur, the first challenge is simply understanding how things work. There are official regulations, but just as important are the unwritten rules, the subtle cues that signal what’s possible and what’s off-limits.

The process begins with endless meetings. Government officials, factory managers, and local partners all have a say, and every decision requires careful negotiation. Patience is essential. Agreements that seem settled one day can unravel the next, and progress is measured in inches, not miles.

One early project involves setting up a joint venture with a local factory. The building is old, the equipment outdated, and the workers wary of change. At first, there’s resistance—why fix what isn’t broken? But as the foreign team spends time on the shop floor, listening to concerns and offering practical solutions, attitudes begin to shift. A technician, skeptical at first, starts experimenting with new methods. A supervisor, once distant, begins to share ideas for improving efficiency. Slowly, trust grows.

Every step forward is matched by new obstacles. Supplies are hard to come by, and even basic materials must be sourced through a maze of approvals. International sanctions make importing equipment a logistical nightmare. Sometimes, a shipment is delayed for weeks because a single form is missing a stamp. Other times, a deal falls through because of a sudden change in government policy.

Yet, amid the frustrations, there are moments of ingenuity. When a key machine breaks down and spare parts are unavailable, the local team improvises a fix using salvaged components. When power cuts threaten production, workers organize shifts around the electricity schedule, making the most of every available hour. These small victories are hard-earned, but they reveal a spirit of determination that runs deep.

Cultural differences add another layer of complexity. In meetings, disagreements are rarely voiced directly. Instead, concerns are hinted at, or raised in private after the fact. A foreign manager learns to read body language, to sense when a “yes” really means “maybe,” and when silence signals a problem. Misunderstandings are common, but over time, both sides adapt, finding ways to bridge the gap.

One afternoon, a local partner invites the foreign team to a picnic by the river. There’s food, music, and even a few games. For a few hours, the pressures of work fade, replaced by laughter and camaraderie. It’s a reminder that, beneath the surface, people everywhere want the same things: respect, opportunity, and a chance to build something meaningful.

Running a business in North Korea is never easy. The challenges are constant, and the risks are real. But for those willing to adapt, to listen, and to learn, it’s possible to make a difference—not just in profits, but in people’s lives. The experience is a lesson in humility, patience, and the power of persistence.

## 3. Navigating Bureaucracy and Sanctions: The Art of Survival

In North Korea, bureaucracy is both a shield and a maze. Every decision, no matter how small, passes through layers of approval. Forms are filled out in triplicate, stamped, and filed away in offices lined with dusty ledgers. For a foreign business, this means that nothing happens quickly, and every step forward requires careful planning.

Sanctions add another layer of difficulty. International restrictions limit what can be imported, exported, or even discussed. Banking is a constant headache—transferring money in or out of the country is fraught with delays and uncertainty. Sometimes, a payment disappears into the system for weeks, only to reappear without explanation. Other times, a deal collapses because a supplier in another country gets cold feet.

The rules are always changing. One day, a product is approved for sale; the next, it’s banned without warning. A shipment of raw materials arrives at the border, only to be held up by customs officials demanding new paperwork. In these moments, patience is tested, and creativity becomes a survival skill.

A local manager, used to these challenges, offers advice: never assume anything is final, and always have a backup plan. When a crucial delivery is delayed, the team scrambles to find alternatives—borrowing supplies from another factory, or reworking the production schedule to keep things moving. It’s a constant juggling act, but over time, the team learns to expect the unexpected.

Relationships matter more than rules. A well-placed phone call, a shared meal, or a small gesture of respect can open doors that paperwork cannot. One evening, after a long day of negotiations, a government official invites the foreign team to a karaoke bar. The atmosphere is relaxed, and for a few hours, the barriers come down. The next morning, a long-stalled project suddenly moves forward. It’s a reminder that, in North Korea, personal connections are often the key to getting things done.

Yet, the risks are real. Every decision carries the potential for misunderstanding, and the consequences can be severe. A foreign manager learns to tread carefully, to double-check every detail, and to keep meticulous records. Mistakes are costly, and trust, once lost, is hard to regain.

Despite the obstacles, there are moments of progress. A new product line launches, creating jobs and bringing in much-needed revenue. A training program helps workers develop new skills, boosting morale and productivity. These successes are hard-won, but they show that, even in the most challenging environment, it’s possible to make a difference.

Living and working under sanctions is a lesson in adaptability. Plans change, setbacks are routine, and victories are measured in small steps. But for those willing to persevere, the rewards go beyond business—they offer a glimpse into the resilience and ingenuity of people determined to survive and thrive, no matter the odds.

## 4. Cultural Adaptation and Everyday Misunderstandings

Adapting to life in North Korea means more than learning a new language or following local customs. It’s about understanding a worldview shaped by decades of isolation, hardship, and collective effort. For a foreigner, the process is often confusing, sometimes frustrating, but always revealing.

The first challenge is communication. Even with a translator, meanings can get lost. A simple request for a meeting can be interpreted as a demand, while a polite suggestion might be seen as criticism. Over time, it becomes clear that words matter less than intent, and that listening is just as important as speaking.

One day, a foreign manager tries to introduce a new workflow at the factory. The idea is simple—rearrange the assembly line to improve efficiency. But the workers are hesitant, unsure if the change is allowed. A supervisor quietly explains that, in North Korea, innovation is often viewed with suspicion. People are taught to follow established procedures, and stepping outside the norm can be risky.

To bridge the gap, the manager spends time on the shop floor, working alongside the team. Gradually, the workers begin to share their own ideas—small improvements that make a big difference. It’s a slow process, but it builds trust and shows that change is possible, even in a rigid system.

Social customs are another source of misunderstanding. In meetings, hierarchy is strictly observed. The most senior person speaks first, and everyone else waits their turn. Disagreements are rarely voiced in public, and criticism is delivered gently, if at all. For a foreigner used to open debate, this can be baffling. But with patience, the logic becomes clear: harmony is valued over confrontation, and relationships are built on mutual respect.

Outside of work, cultural differences are just as pronounced. Invitations to dinner are both an honor and a test. The guest is expected to try every dish, toasts are made in careful order, and every gesture carries meaning. A misplaced comment or a refusal of food can cause offense, even if unintentional.

One evening, a foreign guest is invited to a colleague’s home for a family celebration. The meal is elaborate, with dishes prepared from scarce ingredients. The host beams with pride, eager to share his culture. As the evening unfolds, stories are exchanged, and laughter fills the room. In these moments, the barriers of language and custom fade, replaced by a sense of shared humanity.

Yet, misunderstandings are inevitable. A joke falls flat, a gesture is misread, or a well-meaning suggestion is taken the wrong way. Each misstep is a lesson in humility, a reminder that adaptation is a two-way street.

Over time, the process becomes easier. The foreigner learns to read the unspoken signals, to sense when to speak and when to listen. Friendships deepen, and the initial awkwardness gives way to genuine connection. The experience is a lesson in patience, empathy, and the importance of seeing the world through another’s eyes.

Living in North Korea is a daily exercise in adaptation. The rules are different, the expectations unfamiliar, but the rewards are real. For those willing to learn, the journey offers not just new skills, but a deeper understanding of what it means to be human in a world shaped by history, hardship, and hope.

## 5. Human Stories Behind the Headlines: Everyday Life Beyond Propaganda

Most people outside North Korea know the country through images of military parades, stern-faced leaders, and endless rows of marching soldiers. But behind the headlines, life is made up of small moments—families sharing meals, children playing in the park, neighbors helping each other through hard times.

One morning, a foreigner walks through a local market. The stalls are simple, but the atmosphere is lively. Vendors call out to passersby, offering vegetables, homemade snacks, and secondhand clothes. Money is tight, but people find ways to make do. A mother haggles over the price of rice, while her son tugs at her sleeve, eyeing a toy on a nearby table. The scene could be anywhere, but here, every transaction is shaped by scarcity and ingenuity.

At a nearby school, children gather for morning exercises. Their uniforms are neat, their faces serious. But as the teacher turns away, a group of friends break into giggles, sharing a secret joke. Education is strict, with lessons focused on loyalty and discipline, but children find ways to be children—drawing pictures, playing games, and dreaming of the future.

Workplaces are a mix of routine and improvisation. In a factory, workers gather for a daily briefing, listening as the supervisor outlines the day’s goals. The equipment is old, and supplies are limited, but the team takes pride in their work. When a machine breaks down, they pool their knowledge to fix it, using whatever materials are at hand. It’s a reminder that, even in the most challenging circumstances, people find ways to adapt and succeed.

Holidays are a time for celebration, but also for reflection. On national holidays, families dress in their best clothes and visit monuments to pay their respects. The ceremonies are solemn, but afterwards, the mood lightens. Friends gather for picnics, sharing food and stories. For a few hours, the pressures of daily life fade, replaced by laughter and hope.

Yet, hardship is never far away. Food shortages are common, and many families rely on small gardens to supplement their rations. Power cuts are a fact of life, and winter brings biting cold. But even in the toughest times, people look out for each other. A neighbor shares a bag of rice, a teacher gives extra lessons after school, and a doctor makes house calls despite limited supplies.

For outsiders, it’s easy to focus on the differences. But living in North Korea reveals the similarities that connect people everywhere—the desire for safety, the hope for a better future, and the strength to endure. The stories that matter most are not the ones told in official speeches, but the quiet acts of kindness and resilience that shape everyday life.

In the end, the greatest lesson is one of empathy. Behind the slogans and the propaganda, North Korea is a country of individuals—each with their own dreams, struggles, and stories. To truly understand this place, it’s necessary to look beyond the headlines and see the people who live, work, and hope in the shadow of history.

## 6. Ethical Dilemmas and the Weight of Compromise

There’s a moment, often late at night, when the questions come. For anyone working in North Korea, the line between right and wrong is rarely clear. The rules of the outside world don’t always apply, and every decision seems to carry a hidden cost. It’s easy to judge from a distance, but living inside the system means facing choices that are never black and white.

One foreign manager finds himself wrestling with a simple request: a local colleague asks for help getting medicine for his sick child. The medicine is available, but only through unofficial channels. To help means bending the rules, risking trouble with the authorities. To refuse means turning away from someone in need. In the end, the manager decides to help, slipping the medicine into the colleague’s hands with a quiet word of caution. The relief on the father’s face is immediate, but the manager is left with a lingering sense of unease.

These moments are common. Every day brings new ethical puzzles—whether to speak up about a safety issue, knowing it might embarrass a local partner; whether to push for higher wages, knowing it could draw unwanted attention from officials. Most people outside North Korea never have to make these kinds of choices. Inside, compromise becomes a way of life.

There’s also the question of complicity. By working in North Korea, is a foreigner helping to prop up a repressive system? Or is it possible to make a positive difference, even in small ways? The answer is never simple. Some days, it feels like the work matters—creating jobs, sharing knowledge, building trust. Other days, the weight of the system feels overwhelming, and the urge to walk away is strong.

One evening, a foreigner sits with a group of local friends, sharing drinks and stories. The conversation turns to dreams for the future. One friend, usually quiet, speaks up: “We want the same things you do. We want our children to be safe, to have opportunities, to be happy.” In that moment, the lines between “us” and “them” blur, and the foreigner is reminded that, despite the system, people are people.

Living and working in North Korea means accepting that there are no easy answers. Every choice is a balancing act, every action a negotiation between ideals and reality. The experience is a lesson in humility, empathy, and the limits of good intentions.

## 7. The Impact of International Politics: Living in the Crossfire

The world outside North Korea is never far away. News of missile tests, diplomatic summits, and new rounds of sanctions filters in, sometimes through official channels, sometimes in whispered conversations. For those living and working inside the country, international politics isn’t just background noise—it shapes daily life in ways both obvious and subtle.

One morning, the mood in the office is tense. Overnight, a new round of sanctions has been announced, and everyone is waiting to see what will happen. The foreign team gathers for a meeting, reviewing the latest restrictions and trying to guess how they’ll be enforced. Local colleagues are anxious, worried about job security and the future of the business.

The impact is immediate. Shipments are delayed, bank transfers are frozen, and projects are put on hold. For the foreign staff, it’s a reminder of how fragile everything is—how quickly months of work can be undone by a single headline. For local workers, the uncertainty is even harder. Many have families to support, and the prospect of layoffs is frightening.

In the midst of this, there are moments of resilience. A local manager, faced with a shortage of raw materials, organizes a team to scour the city for alternatives. A group of workers volunteers to work extra hours, determined to keep the factory running. The foreign team does what it can—offering support, sharing information, and trying to keep morale high.

But the pressure takes its toll. One evening, a foreigner finds a colleague sitting alone, staring out the window. “Sometimes I wonder if it’s worth it,” the colleague admits. “We work so hard, and it can all disappear overnight.” The foreigner has no easy answer, only a shared sense of frustration and fatigue.

International politics is a force that can’t be controlled, only endured. For those inside North Korea, it’s a daily reminder of how connected—and how vulnerable—they are to the world beyond their borders. The experience is a lesson in patience, adaptability, and the importance of hope in the face of uncertainty.

## 8. Small Victories and Quiet Progress

In a place where change is slow and setbacks are common, progress often comes in small, almost invisible steps. It’s easy to overlook these moments, but for those living and working in North Korea, they are the milestones that matter most.

One project stands out—a training program for local workers. The goal is simple: teach new skills, improve efficiency, and give people a sense of pride in their work. At first, there’s skepticism. Many workers have never used modern equipment, and some are afraid to make mistakes. But as the weeks go by, confidence grows. A young technician, once shy and uncertain, becomes a leader on the shop floor. A group of women, previously assigned to menial tasks, master a new machine and take on more responsibility.

These changes don’t make headlines, but they transform lives. For the foreign team, it’s a source of quiet satisfaction. The work is hard, the obstacles many, but the impact is real. One day, a worker pulls the manager aside and says, “Thank you for believing in us.” It’s a small gesture, but it means everything.

There are setbacks, of course. A new product launch is delayed by a power outage. A shipment of supplies is lost at the border. But each challenge is met with determination and creativity. The team learns to celebrate the small victories—a successful training session, a repaired machine, a day without interruptions.

Outside of work, progress is just as subtle. A local family, once wary of foreigners, invites a foreign colleague to a birthday celebration. Children who once stared in curiosity now greet visitors with shy smiles. Trust, once fragile, begins to take root.

In North Korea, change is measured in inches, not miles. But for those willing to look closely, the signs of progress are everywhere. The experience is a reminder that even in the most difficult circumstances, hope and determination can make a difference.

## 9. Personal Transformation: Lessons Learned in the Hermit Kingdom

Living in North Korea changes a person. The experience is challenging, sometimes overwhelming, but always transformative. For many foreigners, the journey begins with curiosity and a sense of adventure. It ends with a deeper understanding of themselves and the world.

At first, the differences are all-consuming—the language, the customs, the ever-present sense of surveillance. But over time, these things become part of daily life. What stands out instead are the moments of connection—the shared meals, the quiet conversations, the small acts of kindness.

One foreigner, reflecting on his time in the country, realizes how much he’s changed. Patience, once in short supply, has become second nature. The ability to listen, to adapt, to see the world through another’s eyes—these are skills learned out of necessity, but they endure long after leaving.

There are moments of doubt, of course. Times when the isolation feels too much, when the challenges seem insurmountable. But these moments are balanced by a growing sense of resilience. The experience of living and working in North Korea is a test of character, a lesson in humility and empathy.

Leaving the country is bittersweet. There’s relief, of course—a return to freedom, to family, to the familiar comforts of home. But there’s also a sense of loss. The friendships forged, the lessons learned, the small victories achieved—they linger, shaping the way one sees the world.

For those who have lived in North Korea, the experience is never far away. It colors every conversation, every decision, every hope for the future. The greatest lesson is simple: in the most unlikely places, it’s possible to find connection, meaning, and hope.
