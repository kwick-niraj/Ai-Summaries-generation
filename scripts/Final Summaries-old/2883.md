## Chapter 1: Understanding Why Some Ideas Last and Others Fade

Every day, countless ideas compete for attention—some are quickly forgotten, while others linger, shape decisions, and even change lives. The difference between a fleeting thought and a lasting message often comes down to how the idea is crafted and shared. At the heart of this challenge lies a simple question: What makes certain ideas memorable and influential, while others vanish without a trace?

To answer this, it helps to picture the mind as a crowded room. In this room, only the most striking, clear, and emotionally resonant ideas manage to stand out. The rest blend into the background noise. The process of making an idea “stick” is about ensuring it not only gets noticed but also remains accessible and actionable long after the initial encounter.

A common obstacle is what might be called the “curse of knowledge.” Once someone understands something deeply, it becomes difficult to imagine not knowing it. This gap often leads to explanations that are too complex or abstract for others to grasp. The result is that many important messages are lost in translation, never reaching their intended audience in a meaningful way.

To bridge this gap, it’s helpful to think of sticky ideas as those that are easy to understand, hard to forget, and simple to act upon. These ideas share a set of core qualities that make them resilient in the face of distraction and skepticism. Over time, a practical framework has emerged to describe these qualities, often remembered by the acronym SUCCESs:

- **Simplicity:** The idea is stripped down to its most essential core.
- **Unexpectedness:** It grabs attention by breaking patterns or surprising the audience.
- **Concreteness:** Abstract concepts are made tangible and relatable.
- **Credibility:** The message is believable and trustworthy.
- **Emotions:** It connects on a personal, emotional level.
- **Stories:** The idea is embedded in a narrative that inspires action.

These six principles are not a checklist to be rigidly followed, but rather a set of guiding lights. The more of these qualities an idea possesses, the more likely it is to stick. Each principle addresses a different challenge in communication, from capturing attention to ensuring long-term recall.

Consider the example of a public health campaign. A message like “Eat healthier” is vague and easily ignored. But a campaign that says, “Don’t eat anything your grandmother wouldn’t recognize as food,” is simple, concrete, and emotionally resonant. It paints a vivid picture and is far more likely to be remembered and acted upon.

The journey to making ideas stick begins with understanding these core principles and recognizing the common pitfalls that cause messages to fail. By learning to see ideas through the eyes of those who receive them, communicators can craft messages that not only inform but also inspire and endure.

## Chapter 2: The Power of Simplicity—Finding the Core of an Idea

At the foundation of every sticky idea lies simplicity. But simplicity does not mean dumbing down or oversimplifying. Instead, it’s about distilling a message to its most essential element—the core that gives it meaning and direction. This process is much like chiseling away at a block of marble to reveal the statue within; the goal is to remove the unnecessary so that the essential can shine.

Simplicity is powerful because it helps people grasp and remember what matters most. In a world overflowing with information, only the clearest, most focused messages break through. The challenge is to identify the single most important point and express it in a way that is both compact and profound.

One way to achieve this is by using what might be called a “commander’s intent.” In military planning, leaders know that detailed instructions often fall apart in the chaos of action. Instead, they provide a clear, overarching goal—the intent—so that everyone understands the desired outcome, even if circumstances change. In communication, the same principle applies: by stating the core message in a way that guides decisions and actions, the idea becomes more resilient and adaptable.

For example, a fast-food chain might have a complex set of operational guidelines, but its core message could be as simple as “We serve fresh, hot food, fast.” This guiding principle helps employees make decisions in the moment, even when faced with unexpected challenges.

Simplicity also benefits from the use of analogies and metaphors. By linking new ideas to familiar concepts, communicators make the unfamiliar more accessible. A well-chosen analogy can compress a complex idea into a single, memorable image. For instance, describing a new software as “the Swiss Army knife of productivity tools” instantly conveys versatility and usefulness.

To craft a simple message, it’s important to:

- **Identify the most critical point:** What is the one thing that must be remembered?
- **Express it clearly and concisely:** Use plain language and avoid jargon.
- **Use analogies or metaphors:** Relate the idea to something familiar to make it stick.

Simplicity is not about reducing ideas to slogans, but about finding the deep truth at the heart of the message. When an idea is stripped to its core, it becomes easier to share, remember, and act upon. This clarity is the first step toward making any idea truly stick.

## Chapter 3: Grabbing Attention with the Unexpected

Once an idea is clear and simple, the next challenge is to capture attention. In a world where people are constantly bombarded with messages, the unexpected stands out. Surprises break through the mental filters that protect against information overload, forcing the mind to take notice.

Unexpectedness works by violating expectations. When something happens that doesn’t fit the usual pattern, it creates a moment of surprise. This moment is powerful because it jolts people out of autopilot and makes them pay attention. But surprise alone is not enough; it must be used thoughtfully to reinforce the core message, not distract from it.

A classic example comes from safety campaigns. Telling drivers that “Speed kills” is familiar and easy to ignore. But showing a dramatic, unexpected image—like a crumpled car with an empty child’s seat—forces people to confront the real consequences of their actions. The surprise grabs attention, and the message lingers.

To use the unexpected effectively, communicators can:

- **Break a pattern:** Start with something that challenges assumptions or defies expectations.
- **Create curiosity:** Pose a question or present a mystery that demands resolution.
- **Deliver a twist:** Reveal information in a way that surprises, but also clarifies the core idea.

Curiosity is a close cousin of surprise. When people encounter a gap in their knowledge, they feel compelled to fill it. This is why mysteries and puzzles are so engaging—they create a sense of tension that can only be resolved by learning more. By framing messages as questions or challenges, communicators can draw people in and keep them engaged.

For instance, a science teacher might begin a lesson by asking, “Why do astronauts float in space?” This question creates a knowledge gap that students are eager to close. The answer, when revealed, is more likely to be remembered because it resolved a moment of curiosity.

The key is to use surprise and curiosity not as gimmicks, but as tools to highlight and reinforce the core message. When the unexpected is tied to the heart of the idea, it not only captures attention but also makes the message more memorable.

## Chapter 4: Making Ideas Real with Concrete Details

Abstract ideas are often difficult to grasp and easy to forget. To make an idea stick, it must be made tangible—something that can be seen, touched, or imagined in vivid detail. Concreteness transforms vague concepts into real-world experiences, making them easier to understand and recall.

Concrete details anchor ideas in the senses. Instead of talking about “improving education,” a message that describes “a classroom where every child has a book and a desk” paints a clear picture. The mind remembers images and experiences far better than abstract principles.

This principle is especially important when communicating with people who do not share the same background or expertise. Experts often use abstract language because they are comfortable with complex concepts. But for most people, concrete examples are the bridge that connects new ideas to existing knowledge.

Consider the difference between saying “We need to reduce waste” and “Let’s cut down on the number of plastic bottles we use each week.” The second message is specific and actionable; it gives people a clear target and a way to measure progress.

To make ideas more concrete, communicators can:

- **Use sensory language:** Describe what can be seen, heard, or touched.
- **Provide specific examples:** Illustrate the idea with real-life scenarios or case studies.
- **Avoid jargon:** Replace technical terms with everyday language.

Concreteness also helps ideas survive over time. When a message is tied to a vivid image or experience, it is more likely to be remembered and shared. This is why stories and anecdotes are so powerful—they provide concrete details that stick in the mind.

For example, a charity seeking donations for clean water might describe a village where children walk miles each day to fetch water from a muddy stream. This image is far more compelling than statistics alone, because it makes the problem real and urgent.

By grounding ideas in concrete details, communicators ensure that their messages are not only understood, but also remembered and acted upon. Concreteness is the glue that holds abstract concepts together, making them accessible to everyone.

## Chapter 5: Building Trust and Believability Through Credibility

Even the clearest, most attention-grabbing ideas will falter if people do not believe them. Credibility is the quality that makes a message trustworthy and convincing. It answers the question: “Why should this be believed?”

Credibility can come from many sources. Sometimes, it is borrowed from experts or authorities—people whose knowledge or experience commands respect. Other times, it is built through the use of specific details, statistics, or testable claims that allow people to verify the message for themselves.

One effective way to establish credibility is to use what might be called “concrete proof.” Instead of making broad claims, communicators provide specific evidence that supports the message. For example, a toothpaste brand might say, “Four out of five dentists recommend this product.” The statistic is simple, concrete, and easy to believe.

But credibility is not limited to experts and numbers. Sometimes, the most persuasive messages come from ordinary people sharing their own experiences. Personal stories, testimonials, and demonstrations can be powerful because they show the idea in action.

To enhance credibility, communicators can:

- **Cite trusted sources:** Reference experts, authorities, or well-known organizations.
- **Use specific details:** Provide facts, statistics, or examples that can be checked.
- **Encourage testability:** Invite people to try the idea for themselves and see the results.

A memorable example comes from a food safety campaign. Instead of simply stating that a product is safe, the message might invite people to “smell the milk before drinking.” This simple test allows individuals to verify the claim on their own, building trust through personal experience.

Credibility is also reinforced by consistency. When a message aligns with what people already know or have experienced, it feels more believable. This is why it’s important to avoid exaggeration or overstatement; honesty and transparency are the foundation of trust.

By grounding ideas in credible evidence and inviting people to test them, communicators make their messages more persuasive and enduring. Credibility is the anchor that keeps ideas from drifting away in a sea of skepticism.

## Chapter 6: Stirring Emotions—Making People Care

After establishing credibility, the next step is to ensure that people actually care about the idea. Emotional connection is the fuel that moves ideas from the mind to the heart, prompting action and long-term memory. When an idea resonates emotionally, it becomes more than just information—it becomes personally meaningful.

Emotions act as a kind of glue, binding facts and stories to our sense of self. People are far more likely to remember and act on ideas that make them feel something, whether it’s hope, fear, pride, or empathy. The challenge is to tap into the right emotion for the message and the audience.

One way to do this is by focusing on the individual rather than the abstract. For example, a charity campaign that says, “Millions of children go hungry every day,” may be less effective than one that introduces a single child and tells their story. This approach, sometimes called the “identifiable victim effect,” makes the problem real and relatable.

To evoke emotion, communicators can:

- **Appeal to self-interest:** Show how the idea benefits the individual or their loved ones.
- **Tap into group identity:** Connect the message to shared values or a sense of belonging.
- **Use vivid language:** Paint emotional pictures that stir the imagination.

Consider a public health message about quitting smoking. Instead of listing statistics about lung cancer, a campaign might show a parent playing with their child, highlighting the joy and future that quitting can bring. This approach shifts the focus from abstract risk to tangible, positive emotion.

It’s important to note that not all emotions are equally effective for every message. The key is to match the emotion to the desired action. For example, a sense of urgency might be appropriate for a safety warning, while pride or hope might work better for a community initiative.

Emotional resonance is not about manipulation, but about authenticity. When a message genuinely reflects the values and concerns of its audience, it becomes more powerful and enduring. By making people care, communicators give their ideas the energy needed to spread and stick.

## Chapter 7: The Power of Stories—Inspiring Action Through Narrative

The final principle in the SUCCESs framework is storytelling. Stories are the vessels that carry ideas across time and space, making them memorable and actionable. While facts and logic appeal to the rational mind, stories engage the imagination and emotions, helping people see themselves in the message.

Stories work because they simulate experience. When we hear a story, our brains respond as if we are living it ourselves. This mental rehearsal makes it easier to remember the message and apply it in real life. Stories also provide context, showing how an idea works in practice and what results it can produce.

There are several types of stories that help ideas stick:

- **Challenge stories:** Show how someone overcomes obstacles or adversity.
- **Connection stories:** Illustrate how relationships are built or strengthened.
- **Creative stories:** Reveal how a new idea or solution emerges.

For example, a business leader might share a story about a team that faced a major setback but found a creative way to succeed. This narrative not only conveys the core message—resilience and innovation—but also inspires others to act in similar ways.

To use stories effectively, communicators can:

- **Find real-life examples:** Look for moments when the idea made a difference.
- **Highlight transformation:** Show how people or situations changed as a result.
- **Keep it simple and focused:** Avoid unnecessary details that distract from the core message.

Stories are especially powerful when they are concrete and emotionally resonant. A story about a single person’s journey is often more memorable than a list of statistics or a general statement. By embedding ideas in narrative, communicators make them easier to understand, remember, and share.

Stories also serve as mental templates. When people encounter a similar situation in the future, they can recall the story and use it as a guide for action. In this way, stories not only spread ideas but also shape behavior.

## Chapter 8: Weaving the Principles Together—The SUCCESs Checklist in Practice

With all six principles—Simplicity, Unexpectedness, Concreteness, Credibility, Emotions, and Stories—explored, the next step is to see how they work together in real-world communication. While each principle is powerful on its own, their true strength comes from their interplay.

Think of the SUCCESs framework as a toolkit. Depending on the situation, different tools may be more or less important, but the best ideas often combine several principles at once. For example, a public service announcement might use a simple, concrete message delivered through a surprising story that evokes strong emotion and is backed by credible evidence.

To apply the framework, communicators can use a mental checklist:

- **Is the core message clear and simple?**
- **Does the idea grab attention in an unexpected way?**
- **Are the details concrete and easy to visualize?**
- **Is the message credible and trustworthy?**
- **Does it make people care emotionally?**
- **Is the idea embedded in a memorable story?**

Consider a campaign to encourage seatbelt use. A simple, concrete slogan like “Click it or ticket” is easy to remember. Unexpected visuals—such as a slow-motion video of a crash—grab attention. Credibility comes from statistics and endorsements by safety experts. Emotional resonance is created by showing families affected by accidents. Finally, stories of real survivors inspire action.

The key is to blend the principles naturally, without forcing them. Not every message will use all six, but the more principles are present, the stickier the idea becomes. Over time, practicing this approach helps communicators instinctively craft messages that are both memorable and effective.

## Chapter 9: Overcoming Common Barriers—Practical Strategies for Sticky Communication

Even with a strong framework, real-world communication is full of obstacles. People are busy, skeptical, and easily distracted. To make ideas stick, it’s important to anticipate and address these barriers.

One common challenge is the “curse of knowledge”—the tendency to assume others know what we know. To overcome this, communicators must step outside their own perspective and see the message through the eyes of their audience. This often means simplifying language, using concrete examples, and testing messages with real people.

Another barrier is information overload. In a noisy world, only the clearest and most compelling messages break through. This is where the principles of simplicity and unexpectedness are especially valuable. By focusing on the core idea and presenting it in a surprising way, communicators can cut through the clutter.

Skepticism is also a hurdle. People are wary of exaggerated claims and manipulative tactics. Building credibility through honest, transparent communication and inviting people to test ideas for themselves helps build trust.

To address these barriers, communicators can:

- **Test messages with real audiences:** Get feedback and refine the message based on what resonates.
- **Use analogies and stories:** Make abstract ideas relatable and memorable.
- **Focus on action:** Give people clear, concrete steps they can take.

For example, a company launching a new product might run small pilot campaigns to see which messages stick. By observing what people remember and act on, they can refine their approach before scaling up.

Ultimately, sticky communication is an ongoing process of learning and adaptation. By staying curious and open to feedback, communicators can continually improve their ability to craft messages that endure.

## Chapter 10: Spreading Sticky Ideas—From Individual Messages to Cultural Change

The final step is to move beyond individual messages and think about how sticky ideas spread through groups, organizations, and even entire cultures. When an idea is truly sticky, it becomes part of the collective memory, shaping behavior and decision-making over time.

Sticky ideas often spread through social networks, as people share stories and experiences with others. This word-of-mouth transmission is especially powerful when the message is simple, concrete, and emotionally resonant. People are more likely to share ideas that make them feel something and that are easy to retell.

Leaders and change-makers can accelerate this process by creating environments that encourage sharing and storytelling. For example, organizations might celebrate employees who embody core values, turning their stories into legends that inspire others.

To foster the spread of sticky ideas, it helps to:

- **Empower messengers:** Encourage people at all levels to share stories and examples.
- **Create rituals and symbols:** Use concrete actions and images to reinforce the message.
- **Celebrate success:** Highlight real-life examples of the idea in action.

Consider a school that wants to promote a culture of kindness. By sharing stories of students helping each other, creating visible symbols (like a “kindness wall”), and celebrating acts of kindness publicly, the idea becomes woven into the fabric of daily life.

Over time, sticky ideas can transform not just individual behavior, but entire communities. By applying the SUCCESs principles consistently, communicators can plant seeds that grow into lasting change.

## Conclusion: Bringing Sticky Ideas to Life

The journey to making ideas stick is both an art and a science. By understanding and applying the SUCCESs framework—Simplicity, Unexpectedness, Concreteness, Credibility, Emotions, and Stories—anyone can craft messages that are clear, memorable, and influential. These principles are not rigid rules, but flexible tools that can be adapted to fit any context, from business and education to public health and personal relationships.

**Key Takeaways:**

- Strip ideas to their core to ensure clarity and focus every time.
- Use surprise and curiosity to break through mental filters and grab attention.
- Anchor messages in concrete details to make them real and relatable.
- Build trust with credible evidence, honest stories, and testable claims.
- Connect emotionally to inspire caring and motivate real action.
- Embed ideas in stories to make them memorable and easy to share.

Remember, making ideas stick is not about clever slogans or flashy presentations. It’s about seeing the world through the eyes of your audience, meeting them where they are, and guiding them step by step toward understanding and action. The most powerful ideas are those that feel true, relevant, and alive in the minds of others.

As you move forward, approach each message as an opportunity to connect, inspire, and make a difference. Start small—test your ideas, listen to feedback, and refine your approach. Over time, you’ll find that the principles of sticky communication become second nature, helping you shape messages that not only survive but thrive. Trust in the process, stay curious, and remember: every idea has the potential to stick, if you craft it with care and empathy.
