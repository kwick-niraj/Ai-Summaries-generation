## Introduction

Stepping into leadership often means navigating a landscape filled with both opportunity and challenge, especially for women forging their path in business and the workplace. The journey calls for a blend of confidence, strategic thinking, and emotional intelligence, as well as the ability to inspire and empower others. She Thinks Like a Boss: Leadership—9 Essential Skills for New Female Leaders in Business and the Workplace by Jemma Roedel delves into the unique dynamics women encounter as they rise to positions of influence, highlighting the importance of self-awareness, resilience, and effective communication. The world of leadership is not just about authority, but about cultivating a mindset that embraces growth, collaboration, and the courage to break through barriers.

Within this framework, the focus turns to the practical and transformative skills that can elevate new female leaders. From mastering assertiveness and decision-making to building trust and fostering inclusive teams, each essential skill becomes a stepping stone toward authentic leadership. The narrative weaves together actionable insights and empowering perspectives, encouraging women to claim their space and lead with purpose. As the journey unfolds, the emphasis remains on developing a leadership style that is both impactful and true to oneself, setting the stage for lasting success and meaningful change.

## Chapter 1 Building a Foundation of Self-Belief and Presence

Stepping into a leadership role for the first time can feel like standing at the edge of a new landscape—exciting, but also daunting. The journey begins not with external strategies, but with the internal work of cultivating self-confidence and a strong sense of presence. This foundation is essential, especially for women who may face unique challenges and expectations in the workplace.

Self-belief is more than just positive thinking; it’s the quiet conviction that one’s ideas, skills, and contributions are valuable. This conviction often needs to be built intentionally, especially in environments where women have historically been underrepresented or underestimated. The process starts with recognizing and challenging the internal narratives that can undermine confidence. These might sound like doubts about being “ready enough” or worries about being perceived as too assertive or not assertive enough.

A practical approach to strengthening self-belief involves reflecting on past achievements, however small, and using them as evidence of capability. This is not about boasting, but about creating a mental portfolio of successes to draw upon in moments of uncertainty. Visualizing successful outcomes and rehearsing key conversations can also help anchor a sense of readiness.

Presence, meanwhile, is the ability to project confidence and calm, even when feeling nervous inside. It’s communicated through body language, tone of voice, and the way one holds space in a room. Simple adjustments—standing tall, making eye contact, speaking at a measured pace—can signal authority and invite respect. These physical cues not only influence how others perceive a leader, but also reinforce self-assurance internally.

For many women, developing presence also means navigating the fine line between being seen as approachable and being taken seriously. This balance is not about changing personality, but about being intentional with how one shows up. It’s helpful to observe leaders who embody both warmth and authority, noting how they use gestures, language, and even silence to command attention.

Key practices for building self-belief and presence include:

- **Reflecting on personal strengths and past wins** to counteract self-doubt.
- **Practicing confident body language**—upright posture, steady eye contact, and purposeful movement.
- **Preparing for key interactions** by visualizing positive outcomes and rehearsing responses.
- **Seeking feedback from trusted colleagues or mentors** to gain perspective and encouragement.

This internal groundwork is not a one-time task, but an ongoing process. As new challenges arise, returning to these practices helps maintain a steady sense of self, which is the bedrock of effective leadership. With a strong foundation of self-belief and presence, the next steps—communicating clearly, setting boundaries, and leading teams—become more accessible and sustainable.

## Chapter 2 Communicating Assertively and Effectively

Once a foundation of self-confidence is established, the next essential skill is communication. In leadership, the ability to express ideas clearly, listen actively, and assert needs without aggression is crucial. For women, this often involves navigating stereotypes that can label assertiveness as abrasiveness, making it especially important to develop a communication style that is both direct and respectful.

Assertive communication is about stating opinions, needs, and boundaries in a way that is honest and clear, while also respecting the perspectives of others. It stands in contrast to passive communication, which can lead to being overlooked, and aggressive communication, which can damage relationships. The goal is to find a middle ground where one’s voice is heard and valued.

A practical way to approach assertive communication is to use “I” statements, which focus on personal experience rather than assigning blame. For example, saying “I need more time to complete this project” is more effective than “You’re giving me too much work.” This approach reduces defensiveness and keeps the conversation constructive.

Active listening is another cornerstone of effective communication. This means giving full attention to the speaker, asking clarifying questions, and reflecting back what has been heard. It signals respect and helps build trust, especially in team settings where diverse viewpoints are present.

Nonverbal cues also play a significant role. Tone of voice, facial expressions, and gestures can either reinforce or undermine the spoken message. Being mindful of these signals helps ensure that the intended message is received as intended.

Common barriers to assertive communication include fear of conflict, concern about being disliked, or uncertainty about how to phrase difficult messages. Overcoming these barriers involves practice and, sometimes, a willingness to tolerate discomfort in service of clarity and honesty.

Some practical techniques for assertive and effective communication include:

- **Using “I” statements** to express needs and boundaries without blame.
- **Practicing active listening** by summarizing what others have said before responding.
- **Maintaining open body language**—uncrossed arms, nodding, and facing the speaker.
- **Preparing for difficult conversations** by outlining key points and desired outcomes in advance.
- **Setting clear expectations** in both one-on-one and group settings.

In action, assertive communication might look like a manager calmly explaining the rationale behind a decision, inviting feedback, and addressing concerns without becoming defensive. It could also involve a team leader setting limits on after-hours emails to protect work-life balance, or a supervisor giving constructive feedback in a way that encourages growth rather than discouragement.

Developing this skill is an ongoing process, especially in environments where women’s voices have historically been marginalized. Each successful conversation builds confidence and sets a precedent for open, respectful dialogue. Over time, assertive communication becomes not just a tool for getting things done, but a way of modeling the kind of workplace culture that values clarity, respect, and collaboration.

## Chapter 3 Delegating Tasks and Empowering Teams

With a foundation of self-belief and strong communication skills, the next step in effective leadership is learning how to delegate tasks and empower others. Many new leaders, especially women, may feel pressure to prove themselves by taking on too much or by micromanaging details. However, true leadership is not about doing everything alone, but about enabling others to contribute their best.

Delegation is the process of assigning responsibility for specific tasks or projects to others, while maintaining overall accountability. It’s a skill that requires trust, clear communication, and a willingness to let go of control. For some, this can be challenging, especially if there is a fear that mistakes will reflect poorly on the leader. However, holding onto every task can lead to burnout and limits the growth of both the leader and the team.

Empowering teams means creating an environment where people feel trusted, supported, and motivated to take initiative. This involves more than just handing off tasks; it requires providing the resources, guidance, and feedback needed for others to succeed. When team members are empowered, they are more likely to take ownership, solve problems creatively, and develop their own leadership skills.

A practical approach to delegation and empowerment includes:

- **Identifying tasks that can be delegated** based on team members’ strengths and development goals.
- **Clearly communicating expectations, deadlines, and desired outcomes** for each delegated task.
- **Providing necessary resources and support** without micromanaging the process.
- **Offering feedback and recognition** to reinforce positive results and encourage learning from mistakes.
- **Encouraging autonomy** by allowing team members to make decisions within their areas of responsibility.

In practice, effective delegation might involve a manager assigning a project to a team member who has shown interest in developing new skills, outlining the objectives, and checking in at key milestones rather than overseeing every detail. It could also mean creating opportunities for team members to lead meetings or present their ideas, signaling trust in their abilities.

Empowerment is closely linked to team morale and performance. When people feel their contributions are valued and their growth is supported, they are more engaged and committed. This, in turn, reflects positively on the leader, who is seen as someone who develops talent rather than hoards responsibility.

For women in leadership, delegation and empowerment can also be a way to challenge stereotypes that expect them to be caretakers or perfectionists. By modeling trust and collaboration, female leaders can set new standards for what effective leadership looks like.

The process of learning to delegate and empower is ongoing. It requires self-awareness, patience, and a willingness to adjust as team dynamics evolve. Over time, these skills not only lighten the leader’s workload but also create a culture of shared responsibility and mutual respect.

## Chapter 4 Setting Boundaries and Preventing Burnout

As responsibilities grow, so does the risk of overextending oneself. Setting healthy boundaries is a critical leadership skill, especially for women who may feel pressure to be constantly available or to take on extra work to prove their worth. Without clear limits, the demands of leadership can quickly lead to stress and burnout, undermining both personal well-being and professional effectiveness.

Boundaries are the guidelines that define what is acceptable in terms of workload, communication, and personal time. They help protect energy and focus, ensuring that leaders can sustain their performance over the long term. Setting boundaries is not about being inflexible or unhelpful; it’s about making intentional choices that align with priorities and values.

Common challenges to setting boundaries include fear of disappointing others, concern about being seen as uncooperative, or uncertainty about how to say no. These challenges are often amplified for women, who may be socialized to prioritize others’ needs over their own. However, consistently saying yes to every request can lead to resentment, exhaustion, and diminished effectiveness.

A practical approach to setting boundaries involves:

- **Clarifying personal and professional priorities** to guide decision-making about where to invest time and energy.
- **Communicating limits clearly and respectfully**—for example, stating preferred hours for meetings or response times for emails.
- **Learning to say no or to negotiate requests** that do not align with current goals or capacity.
- **Scheduling regular breaks and downtime** to recharge and maintain perspective.
- **Modeling boundary-setting for others** to create a culture where self-care is respected.

In action, setting boundaries might look like a leader declining to take on an additional project when already at capacity, or establishing a policy of not responding to work messages after a certain hour. It could also involve delegating tasks that fall outside of core responsibilities, or asking for support when needed.

Preventing burnout is not just about managing workload; it’s also about recognizing the signs of stress and taking proactive steps to address them. This might include seeking support from mentors or peers, practicing mindfulness or other stress-reduction techniques, and making time for activities that bring joy and relaxation.

For women in leadership, setting boundaries can be an act of self-advocacy and a way to challenge workplace cultures that reward overwork. By prioritizing well-being, leaders not only protect their own health but also set an example for their teams, encouraging others to do the same.

Over time, the ability to set and maintain boundaries becomes a key factor in sustaining leadership effectiveness. It allows leaders to show up fully, make better decisions, and create environments where everyone can thrive.

## Chapter 5 Navigating Workplace Bias and Stereotypes

Even with strong skills and clear boundaries, women in leadership often encounter bias and stereotypes that can undermine their authority or limit their opportunities. Navigating these challenges requires both awareness and strategy, as well as a commitment to challenging the status quo.

Workplace bias can take many forms, from overt discrimination to subtle assumptions about competence, ambition, or leadership style. Stereotypes about how women “should” behave—such as being nurturing, agreeable, or self-sacrificing—can create double standards. For example, assertive behavior that is praised in men may be criticized in women as being too aggressive or unfeminine.

Recognizing bias is the first step. This involves paying attention to patterns in feedback, opportunities, or interactions that seem influenced by gender rather than performance. It also means being aware of internalized biases—beliefs that have been absorbed from the surrounding culture and may influence self-perception or behavior.

Responding to bias requires a combination of self-advocacy, allyship, and systemic change. On an individual level, it can be helpful to:

- **Document instances of bias or unfair treatment** to provide evidence if needed.
- **Seek out mentors, sponsors, or allies** who can offer support, advice, and advocacy.
- **Address biased comments or behaviors directly** when safe and appropriate, using calm and factual language.
- **Focus on results and impact** to counteract stereotypes with evidence of competence and leadership.
- **Participate in or initiate conversations about diversity and inclusion** to raise awareness and promote change.

In practice, navigating bias might involve a leader calmly correcting a colleague who interrupts or talks over her in meetings, or advocating for equitable access to high-visibility projects. It could also mean supporting other women or underrepresented colleagues, creating networks of mutual support.

Challenging stereotypes is not just about individual advancement; it’s about creating a more inclusive and equitable workplace for everyone. This can involve advocating for policies that promote diversity, participating in training or awareness initiatives, and modeling inclusive leadership behaviors.

Overcoming bias is an ongoing process, and setbacks are inevitable. However, each act of resistance—whether it’s speaking up, supporting a colleague, or simply persisting in the face of obstacles—contributes to a broader shift in workplace culture.

By developing the skills to recognize and respond to bias, women leaders not only protect their own interests but also pave the way for others. This work, while sometimes difficult, is essential for building organizations where everyone has the opportunity to lead and succeed.

## Chapter 6 Cultivating Emotional Intelligence in Leadership

As the journey of leadership continues, the ability to understand and manage emotions—both one’s own and those of others—becomes increasingly vital. Emotional intelligence (EQ) is the quiet engine behind effective leadership, shaping how leaders respond to challenges, build relationships, and inspire trust.

At its core, emotional intelligence involves four interconnected abilities: self-awareness, self-regulation, social awareness, and relationship management. Self-awareness is the practice of noticing one’s emotional responses in real time—recognizing, for example, when frustration is rising during a tense meeting. Self-regulation is the skill of pausing before reacting, choosing a response that aligns with values and goals rather than being driven by impulse.

Social awareness is the capacity to read the emotional climate of a room, to sense when a team is anxious, disengaged, or energized. Relationship management is the art of using this awareness to communicate with empathy, resolve conflicts, and motivate others.

A leader with high emotional intelligence might notice her own anxiety before a big presentation, take a few deep breaths to steady herself, and then focus on connecting with her audience. She might also pick up on a team member’s unspoken discomfort during a project review and gently invite them to share their perspective.

Practical ways to strengthen emotional intelligence include:

- **Regular self-reflection**—journaling or pausing to check in with one’s feelings.
- **Practicing mindful responses**—taking a breath before reacting to difficult situations.
- **Observing nonverbal cues**—noticing body language, tone, and energy shifts in others.
- **Asking open-ended questions** to understand team members’ experiences and concerns.
- **Offering empathy and validation**—acknowledging others’ feelings, even when solutions aren’t immediate.

Emotional intelligence is not about suppressing emotions or always being calm; it’s about using emotions as information. When leaders model emotional awareness and empathy, they create a culture where people feel seen and understood. This, in turn, fosters trust, loyalty, and resilience—qualities that sustain teams through both triumphs and setbacks.

For women, cultivating emotional intelligence can also be a way to counter stereotypes that equate emotion with weakness. By demonstrating that emotional insight is a leadership strength, women can redefine what effective leadership looks and feels like.

## Chapter 7 Making Decisions Under Pressure

Leadership often means making decisions in the midst of uncertainty, time constraints, or conflicting priorities. The ability to make sound choices under pressure is a hallmark of effective leaders, and it’s a skill that can be developed with practice and intention.

Decision-making under pressure begins with clarity—knowing what the real problem is, what the desired outcome looks like, and what constraints are in play. It’s easy to get swept up in urgency, but pausing to define the issue can prevent costly mistakes.

A helpful framework for decision-making includes:

- **Clarifying the decision to be made**—what is at stake, and why does it matter?
- **Gathering relevant information**—but avoiding the trap of analysis paralysis.
- **Identifying possible options**—listing out choices, even unconventional ones.
- **Weighing risks and benefits**—considering both short-term and long-term impacts.
- **Consulting trusted advisors or team members** for diverse perspectives.
- **Committing to a course of action**—accepting that no decision is perfect, but action is necessary.
- **Reviewing outcomes and learning from results**—using each decision as a learning opportunity.

In practice, a leader might face a sudden staffing shortage and need to reassign responsibilities quickly. By clarifying the most urgent needs, consulting with the team, and making a clear decision, she can keep the project moving forward while minimizing disruption.

Pressure can amplify self-doubt, especially for women who may feel scrutinized or second-guessed. Building confidence in decision-making involves trusting one’s judgment, learning from mistakes, and recognizing that leadership is as much about adaptability as it is about certainty.

Over time, making decisions under pressure becomes less about getting it “right” every time, and more about moving forward with integrity, learning, and resilience.

## Chapter 8 Building Influence and Leading Change

Leadership is not just about authority or position; it’s about influence—the ability to shape outcomes, inspire others, and drive positive change. Building influence is a gradual process, rooted in credibility, relationships, and a clear sense of purpose.

Influence grows when leaders consistently demonstrate integrity, deliver on promises, and communicate a compelling vision. It’s also strengthened by listening deeply, understanding what motivates others, and finding common ground.

Some practical ways to build influence include:

- **Developing expertise**—becoming a go-to resource in a specific area.
- **Building strong relationships**—investing time in getting to know colleagues, stakeholders, and team members.
- **Communicating vision and values**—articulating not just what needs to be done, but why it matters.
- **Supporting others’ success**—mentoring, advocating, and celebrating team achievements.
- **Navigating organizational politics with integrity**—understanding informal networks and using them to advance positive goals.

Leading change requires both vision and persistence. Change often meets resistance, whether due to fear, uncertainty, or competing interests. Effective leaders anticipate these challenges and address them with empathy and clarity.

For example, a leader introducing a new workflow might start by explaining the reasons for the change, listening to concerns, and involving the team in shaping the implementation. She might also identify informal influencers within the group and enlist their support to build momentum.

Influence is not about manipulation or self-promotion; it’s about creating alignment and shared purpose. For women, building influence can also mean challenging traditional power structures and advocating for more inclusive, collaborative ways of working.

## Chapter 9 Fostering a Culture of Growth and Feedback

Sustained leadership thrives in environments where learning and growth are valued. Creating a culture of feedback—where people feel safe to give and receive constructive input—is essential for both individual and team development.

Feedback is most effective when it is specific, timely, and focused on behaviors rather than personal traits. Leaders set the tone by inviting feedback on their own performance, modeling openness, and responding with curiosity rather than defensiveness.

Key practices for fostering growth and feedback include:

- **Regular check-ins**—short, focused conversations about progress, challenges, and goals.
- **Celebrating effort and improvement**—not just outcomes, but the process of learning.
- **Normalizing feedback**—making it a routine part of team interactions, not just a formal event.
- **Providing actionable suggestions**—offering clear, practical ideas for improvement.
- **Encouraging self-reflection**—inviting team members to assess their own strengths and areas for growth.

A leader might, for example, start team meetings with a quick round of “what’s working and what’s challenging,” creating space for honest dialogue. She might also share her own learning goals, signaling that growth is a shared journey.

For women, fostering a feedback culture can help counteract perfectionism and fear of criticism. By framing feedback as a tool for development rather than judgment, leaders can create environments where everyone feels empowered to stretch, experiment, and improve.

## Chapter 10 Sustaining Leadership Through Ongoing Development

Leadership is not a destination, but a continuous process of learning, adapting, and growing. The most effective leaders are those who remain curious, seek out new challenges, and invest in their own development as well as that of their teams.

Ongoing development can take many forms—formal education, mentorship, networking, or simply seeking out new experiences. It’s about staying open to feedback, reflecting on successes and setbacks, and being willing to evolve.

Some practical strategies for sustaining leadership growth include:

- **Setting personal learning goals**—identifying areas to strengthen or explore.
- **Seeking out mentors and role models**—learning from those with different perspectives or experiences.
- **Participating in professional networks**—building connections and sharing knowledge.
- **Embracing new challenges**—volunteering for projects outside of one’s comfort zone.
- **Practicing self-care and resilience**—recognizing that growth requires rest and renewal.

A leader might, for instance, join a cross-functional team to broaden her skills, attend workshops on emerging trends, or schedule regular reflection time to assess progress.

For women, ongoing development is also about advocating for opportunities, negotiating for resources, and supporting others on their leadership journeys. By committing to lifelong learning, leaders not only enhance their own effectiveness but also inspire those around them to do the same.

## Conclusion

Stepping into leadership is a journey of self-discovery, courage, and continuous growth. The principles outlined here—building self-belief, communicating assertively, empowering teams, setting boundaries, navigating bias, cultivating emotional intelligence, making decisions under pressure, building influence, fostering feedback, and committing to ongoing development—form a practical, human-centered roadmap for women who want to lead with authenticity and impact.

**Key takeaways:**

- Build self-belief by reflecting on strengths and practicing confident presence daily.
- Communicate assertively, using “I” statements and active listening to foster respect.
- Delegate tasks and empower teams to encourage growth and shared responsibility.
- Set clear boundaries to protect well-being and model sustainable leadership.
- Recognize and respond to bias, advocating for equity and inclusion at every level.
- Cultivate emotional intelligence to navigate challenges and build strong relationships.
- Make decisions under pressure by clarifying goals and trusting your judgment.
- Build influence through credibility, vision, and supporting others’ success.
- Foster a culture of feedback and learning to drive continuous improvement.
- Invest in ongoing development, seeking new challenges and supporting others’ growth.

Remember, leadership is not about perfection or having all the answers. It’s about showing up with integrity, learning from experience, and creating spaces where others can thrive. There will be moments of doubt, setbacks, and resistance—but each step forward, no matter how small, contributes to a larger legacy of positive change. Trust in your capacity to grow, adapt, and lead with heart. The journey is ongoing, and every act of courage, empathy, and resilience helps pave the way for others to follow. As you continue to lead, let your values guide you, seek support when needed, and celebrate the progress you make—both in yourself and in those you inspire along the way.
