## Introduction

Abolitionist visions challenge the very foundations of justice, asking what it means to truly be free and safe. In a world shaped by systems of punishment and control, the call to imagine something radically different grows louder. We Do This 'til We Free Us by Mariame Kaba brings forth the urgency and hope of collective action, centering the wisdom of communities who have long resisted carceral violence. The struggle for abolition is not just about dismantling prisons and policing, but about building new forms of care, accountability, and solidarity that honor the dignity of every person.

Within this landscape, the work of organizing becomes both a practice and a promise—an ongoing commitment to transformation, even when the path is uncertain. The questions of how to respond to harm, how to heal, and how to create safety without relying on punishment are explored through stories, strategies, and reflections rooted in lived experience. The journey is one of persistence, creativity, and deep belief in the possibility of change, inviting all who encounter these ideas to imagine and enact a world where freedom is not just a dream, but a shared reality.

## Chapter 1 Understanding Abolition as a Vision and a Daily Practice

Abolition, as explored in this framework, is not simply the act of tearing down prisons or eliminating police forces. Instead, it is a broad, living vision—a way of seeing the world and acting within it that centers on care, transformation, and the belief that no one is disposable. This vision is both a destination and a set of daily choices, rooted in the conviction that true safety and justice come from meeting people’s needs, not from punishment or exclusion.

At its core, abolition asks a fundamental question: What if society responded to harm not with more harm, but with healing and accountability? This question reframes justice from a system of retribution to one of transformation. The abolitionist approach recognizes that current systems—prisons, policing, and punitive laws—often perpetuate cycles of violence, especially against marginalized communities. These systems are not neutral; they are shaped by histories of racism, classism, and other forms of oppression.

To make this vision practical, abolition is understood as a dual process:

- **Dismantling Harmful Systems:** This involves challenging and working to end the reliance on prisons, policing, and punitive responses to harm.
- **Building New Structures:** At the same time, abolition is about creating and nurturing alternatives—community-based supports, transformative justice practices, and networks of mutual aid.

This dual approach is sometimes described as “shrinking the carceral system” while “growing community capacity.” The work is ongoing, requiring both imagination and persistence.

Abolition is not a utopian fantasy or a call for instant change. Instead, it is a commitment to a long-term process, one that unfolds through collective action and small, everyday choices. For example, when a community group organizes to support neighbors facing eviction, or when people come together to resolve conflict without calling the police, they are practicing abolition in real time.

Abolition also requires a shift in mindset. Rather than seeing people who cause harm as irredeemable, it asks: What conditions led to this harm? How can accountability and healing be fostered? This approach is grounded in the belief that everyone has the capacity to change, and that communities can develop the tools to address harm without resorting to state violence.

In summary, abolition is both a vision for a world without prisons and police, and a set of daily practices that move society closer to that world. It is a discipline of hope, rooted in the belief that transformation is possible—both for individuals and for entire communities.

## Chapter 2 The Limits of Policing and Prisons in Creating Safety

The widespread belief that police and prisons keep society safe is deeply ingrained, but this framework challenges that assumption by examining the real impact of these institutions. Rather than providing safety, policing and incarceration often create new forms of harm, especially for those already marginalized by race, class, gender, or immigration status.

Policing, as it currently exists, is not simply about responding to crime. It is a system designed to maintain social order, often at the expense of the most vulnerable. For example, police are frequently called to manage situations involving mental health crises, homelessness, or poverty—issues that are not crimes, but symptoms of unmet needs. The result is that people in crisis are criminalized rather than supported.

Prisons, similarly, are not neutral spaces for rehabilitation. Instead, they function as warehouses for people society has failed or chosen to abandon. The experience of incarceration is often traumatic, breaking apart families and communities, and making it harder for people to rebuild their lives after release. The cycle of arrest, incarceration, and reentry traps many in a revolving door, with little opportunity for true healing or accountability.

Several key points illustrate the limits of these systems:

- **Policing and Prisons Do Not Address Root Causes:** Most harm stems from unmet needs—poverty, lack of housing, untreated trauma, or substance use. Policing and incarceration do not solve these problems; they often make them worse.
- **Disproportionate Impact on Marginalized Groups:** Black, Indigenous, and other people of color, as well as those living in poverty, are far more likely to be targeted by police and to end up in prison.
- **Punishment Does Not Equal Accountability:** The criminal legal system focuses on punishment, not on helping people take responsibility for harm or make amends.

Real-life examples abound. When a person struggling with addiction is arrested rather than offered treatment, the underlying issue remains. When a survivor of violence is forced to navigate a punitive system, their needs for safety and healing are often unmet.

This framework encourages a shift in thinking: Instead of equating safety with policing and incarceration, safety should be understood as the presence of resources, support, and community connection. True safety comes from ensuring that everyone’s basic needs are met, and that communities have the tools to address harm without relying on state violence.

In practice, this means investing in mental health care, affordable housing, education, and other supports that prevent harm before it occurs. It also means developing community-based responses to conflict and crisis, so that police are not the default solution.

By recognizing the limits of policing and prisons, this approach opens the door to imagining and building new forms of safety—ones that are rooted in care, accountability, and collective well-being.

## Chapter 3 Building Community-Based Alternatives to Punitive Justice

If policing and prisons are not the answer to harm, what is? This chapter explores the practical alternatives that communities are developing to address conflict, violence, and harm without resorting to punishment or exclusion. These alternatives are grounded in the principles of transformative and restorative justice, which prioritize healing, accountability, and the repair of relationships.

Transformative justice is a community-driven approach that asks: How can harm be addressed in ways that transform the conditions that allowed it to happen? Rather than focusing solely on the individual who caused harm, transformative justice looks at the broader context—poverty, trauma, systemic oppression—and seeks to change those conditions.

Restorative justice, while similar, centers on bringing together those who have caused harm, those who have been harmed, and the wider community to collectively address the impact and determine steps toward repair. Both approaches reject the idea that punishment is the only—or even the best—way to respond to harm.

Key elements of community-based alternatives include:

- **Accountability Without Punishment:** Encouraging those who have caused harm to take responsibility, make amends, and change their behavior, without resorting to incarceration or exclusion.
- **Centering the Needs of Survivors:** Ensuring that those who have been harmed have a voice in the process and access to the support they need to heal.
- **Community Involvement:** Engaging the broader community in both prevention and response, recognizing that harm affects everyone.

Practical examples of these alternatives can be found in many communities. Some groups have developed crisis response teams that intervene in situations of domestic violence or mental health crises without involving police. Others have created circles or dialogues where people can come together to address conflict, share their experiences, and develop collective solutions.

These practices are not without challenges. They require time, trust, and a willingness to engage in difficult conversations. They also demand resources—training, facilitation, and ongoing support. However, the benefits are significant: people are more likely to feel heard, relationships can be repaired, and communities become stronger and more resilient.

To support these alternatives, several practical tools and approaches are often used:

- **Community Accountability Agreements:** Written or verbal commitments outlining steps for repair and change.
- **Support Circles:** Groups that provide ongoing support to both those who have caused harm and those who have been harmed.
- **Skill-Building Workshops:** Training in conflict resolution, active listening, and trauma-informed care.

By investing in these alternatives, communities can begin to break the cycle of harm and punishment, and move toward a model of justice that is truly transformative.

## Chapter 4 The Power of Collective Action and Mutual Aid

Abolition is not a solitary endeavor. It is built on the foundation of collective action—people coming together to support one another, challenge injustice, and build new systems of care. Mutual aid, in particular, is a key practice within this framework, offering a concrete way for communities to meet their own needs and reduce reliance on punitive systems.

Mutual aid is more than charity. It is a reciprocal process, where people share resources, skills, and support based on solidarity, not hierarchy. The idea is simple: when someone in the community is struggling, others step in to help, knowing that they, too, may need support in the future. This creates a web of interdependence, where everyone’s well-being is connected.

Collective action takes many forms, from organizing campaigns to free people from jail, to building networks that provide food, housing, or legal support. These efforts are often led by those most affected by the issues, ensuring that solutions are grounded in lived experience.

Several principles guide effective collective action and mutual aid:

- **Solidarity, Not Charity:** The goal is to build power together, not to “save” others.
- **Centering the Most Impacted:** Those who are most affected by harm and injustice should lead the work.
- **Sustainability:** Efforts should be designed to last, with attention to preventing burnout and sharing responsibility.

Real-world examples illustrate the impact of these practices. During times of crisis—such as natural disasters, pandemics, or mass protests—mutual aid networks have provided food, medical care, and shelter when official systems failed. In organizing for prison abolition, groups have raised funds for bail, supported families of incarcerated people, and advocated for policy changes.

Collective action also involves building coalitions across different movements—connecting struggles for racial justice, gender justice, economic justice, and more. By recognizing the interconnectedness of these issues, communities can build broader, more powerful movements.

To support collective action and mutual aid, several practical steps are often taken:

- **Mapping Community Resources:** Identifying what skills, knowledge, and assets already exist within the community.
- **Creating Communication Networks:** Using technology and in-person meetings to stay connected and coordinate efforts.
- **Developing Shared Agreements:** Establishing clear principles and expectations for how the group will work together.

Through collective action and mutual aid, communities can begin to create the conditions for true safety and justice—conditions that do not depend on policing or prisons, but on care, solidarity, and shared responsibility.

## Chapter 5 Hope as a Discipline in the Face of Setbacks

Abolitionist work is challenging, often marked by setbacks, disappointments, and slow progress. In this context, hope is not a fleeting feeling or naive optimism, but a deliberate practice—a discipline that sustains individuals and communities through difficult times.

Hope, in this sense, is an active choice. It is the decision to keep working toward a better world, even when change seems impossible or far away. This kind of hope is rooted in collective action and the belief that transformation is possible, not just for systems, but for people.

Practicing hope involves several key elements:

- **Staying Grounded in Community:** Drawing strength from relationships and shared purpose.
- **Celebrating Small Wins:** Recognizing and honoring progress, even when it is incremental.
- **Learning from Failure:** Viewing setbacks as opportunities for growth and adaptation.

Hope as a discipline also means resisting the temptation to settle for small reforms that leave the core of oppressive systems intact. While incremental changes can provide relief, the ultimate goal remains the dismantling of systems that perpetuate harm. This requires patience, persistence, and a willingness to imagine new possibilities.

In practice, hope is cultivated through rituals, storytelling, and collective reflection. Communities gather to share their experiences, mourn losses, and envision the future together. These practices help sustain the work, reminding everyone that they are part of something larger than themselves.

To nurture hope, several strategies are often used:

- **Regular Check-Ins:** Creating space for people to share how they are feeling and what support they need.
- **Storytelling:** Sharing stories of resistance, survival, and transformation to inspire and connect.
- **Rest and Renewal:** Prioritizing self-care and community care to prevent burnout.

By treating hope as a discipline, abolitionist movements can weather setbacks and continue moving forward. This kind of hope is not passive; it is an active, collective force that fuels the ongoing work of building a world rooted in care, justice, and liberation.

## Chapter 6 The Pitfalls of Reform and the Necessity of Abolitionist Imagination

As the movement for abolition grows, so too does the pressure to accept reforms—incremental changes to policing and prisons that promise improvement but often leave the core structures intact. This chapter explores why reforms, while sometimes offering short-term relief, can ultimately reinforce the very systems abolition seeks to dismantle.

Reforms are often presented as pragmatic solutions: body cameras for police, new training programs, or “humane” jails. Yet, these measures rarely address the root causes of harm or the fundamental logic of punishment and exclusion. Instead, they can legitimize and expand carceral systems by making them appear more just or effective. For example, adding mental health professionals to police departments may seem like progress, but it can also entrench the idea that police are the proper responders to crisis.

Abolitionist thinking asks us to look beyond surface-level fixes and imagine what true transformation would require. This means asking: Does this change reduce the power of policing and prisons, or does it simply make them more palatable? Does it shift resources toward community-based care, or does it reinforce the status quo?

To navigate the landscape of reform, several guiding questions are helpful:

- **Does the reform shrink the carceral system or expand it?**
- **Does it shift resources to community-led solutions?**
- **Does it create new forms of surveillance or control?**
- **Does it build the capacity for communities to address harm without state intervention?**

Real-life examples highlight these tensions. After high-profile incidents of police violence, cities may invest in new technologies or oversight boards, but the underlying patterns of harm persist. In contrast, when communities organize to redirect funding from police budgets to housing, health care, or youth programs, they begin to build the infrastructure for a different kind of safety.

Abolitionist imagination is essential here. It is the ability to envision a world beyond the limits of what currently exists—a world where safety is not synonymous with policing, and justice is not defined by punishment. This imagination is not escapism; it is a practical tool for designing new systems and practices.

Cultivating this imagination involves:

- **Engaging in collective visioning exercises:** Gathering people to dream together about what safety and justice could look like.
- **Learning from history:** Studying past movements that have radically transformed society, such as the end of slavery or the fight for civil rights.
- **Experimenting with alternatives:** Piloting new models of crisis response, conflict resolution, and community care.

By resisting the lure of superficial reforms and nurturing abolitionist imagination, communities can move closer to the root changes needed for true liberation.

## Chapter 7 Practicing Transformative Justice in Everyday Life

Transformative justice is not just a theory or a set of policies—it is a daily practice that can be woven into the fabric of ordinary life. This chapter explores how individuals and groups can embody transformative justice principles in their relationships, workplaces, and communities.

At its heart, transformative justice is about responding to harm in ways that foster accountability, healing, and change. It asks us to move away from blame and punishment, and toward curiosity and care. This shift can begin in small, everyday interactions.

For example, when conflict arises in a family or among friends, the instinct may be to assign blame or withdraw. A transformative approach would instead invite open dialogue: What happened? How did it affect each person? What needs to be repaired? This process requires patience, vulnerability, and a willingness to listen deeply.

Several practical tools support this work:

- **Active listening:** Focusing on understanding, rather than reacting or defending.
- **Circle processes:** Creating structured spaces for people to share their experiences and feelings.
- **Accountability agreements:** Co-creating clear steps for repair and change, with ongoing support.

In workplaces, transformative justice can look like addressing harm or discrimination through facilitated conversations, rather than punitive HR processes. In schools, it might mean using restorative circles to resolve conflicts among students, rather than suspensions or expulsions.

These practices are not always easy. They require skills that many people have not been taught, and they can bring up strong emotions. But over time, they build trust and resilience, making it possible to address harm without resorting to exclusion or punishment.

Transformative justice also involves recognizing the broader conditions that shape harm. For example, if a student acts out because of unmet needs at home, a transformative response would seek to address those needs, not just the behavior. This approach is rooted in compassion and a belief in the possibility of change.

By practicing transformative justice in daily life, individuals and communities can begin to embody the world they wish to create—one where harm is met with care, and justice is a collective, ongoing process.

## Chapter 8 Building Sustainable Movements and Avoiding Burnout

Abolitionist organizing is long-term work, often carried out in the face of significant obstacles. Sustaining this work requires attention not only to strategy and tactics, but also to the well-being of those involved. This chapter focuses on how movements can build sustainability and resilience, both individually and collectively.

Burnout is a common challenge in social justice work. The urgency of the issues, combined with limited resources and constant setbacks, can lead to exhaustion and disillusionment. To counter this, abolitionist movements emphasize the importance of care—both self-care and community care.

Key strategies for sustainability include:

- **Setting realistic goals:** Recognizing that change is incremental and celebrating progress along the way.
- **Sharing leadership:** Distributing responsibility to prevent overburdening a few individuals.
- **Building supportive relationships:** Creating networks of care where people can ask for help and offer support.
- **Prioritizing rest and renewal:** Encouraging breaks, reflection, and activities that nourish the spirit.

In practice, this might look like regular check-ins at meetings, collective decision-making processes, or organizing retreats focused on healing and connection. Some groups create “care teams” responsible for supporting members through crises or transitions.

Sustainability also means being honest about capacity and limits. It is better to do a few things well than to overextend and risk collapse. Movements that last are those that adapt, learn from mistakes, and make space for joy and celebration.

By centering care and sustainability, abolitionist movements can maintain momentum over the long haul, ensuring that the work continues even in the face of setbacks.

## Chapter 9 Navigating Conflict and Accountability Within Movements

No movement is free from conflict. Disagreements, misunderstandings, and harm can arise even among those working toward shared goals. This chapter explores how abolitionist principles can guide the resolution of conflict and the practice of accountability within organizing spaces.

Traditional responses to conflict often mirror the punitive logic of the systems abolitionists seek to dismantle—exclusion, shaming, or silencing. Instead, abolitionist organizing calls for approaches that prioritize repair, learning, and transformation.

When harm occurs within a group, several steps can support a transformative response:

- **Naming the harm:** Creating space for those affected to share their experiences and feelings.
- **Facilitating dialogue:** Bringing together those involved to listen, reflect, and understand the impact.
- **Co-creating accountability plans:** Developing clear steps for repair, change, and ongoing support.
- **Providing resources for healing:** Offering access to counseling, peer support, or other forms of care.

These processes require skilled facilitation and a commitment to transparency and trust. They also demand humility—the recognition that everyone is capable of causing harm, and everyone is capable of change.

Real-life examples include organizations that have developed internal accountability teams, or that use restorative circles to address conflict among members. These practices help prevent the fracturing of movements and build a culture of care and responsibility.

Navigating conflict in this way is not about avoiding difficult conversations or excusing harm. It is about creating conditions where learning and transformation are possible, and where the movement as a whole can grow stronger.

## Chapter 10 Envisioning a World Beyond Prisons and Policing

The final chapter brings together the threads of abolitionist practice, inviting readers to imagine—and begin building—a world where safety, justice, and care are not dependent on punishment or exclusion. This vision is not a distant dream, but a set of practices and relationships that can be cultivated here and now.

Envisioning this world involves asking: What would it look like if everyone’s needs were met? If harm was addressed through healing and accountability, rather than punishment? If communities had the resources and skills to care for one another?

Several elements are central to this vision:

- **Universal access to housing, healthcare, education, and meaningful work.**
- **Robust networks of mutual aid and community support.**
- **Widespread practice of transformative and restorative justice.**
- **A culture that values care, interdependence, and collective responsibility.**

This vision is already being realized in small ways—through mutual aid networks, community-based crisis response teams, and transformative justice circles. Each of these efforts is a building block, a step toward a world where prisons and policing are no longer necessary.

The work of abolition is ongoing. It requires imagination, persistence, and a willingness to challenge deeply held assumptions. But it is also a source of hope and possibility—a reminder that another world is not only possible, but already in the making.

## Conclusion: Living Abolition—A Guide for Everyday Practice

Abolition is not a distant ideal or a single act of protest. It is a living, breathing practice—a way of moving through the world with care, courage, and imagination. The journey toward abolition is both collective and personal, unfolding through daily choices, relationships, and acts of solidarity.

To begin living abolition in your own life and community, consider these guiding takeaways:

- **Shift from punishment to healing in everyday responses to harm.**
- **Invest in relationships and mutual aid as the foundation of safety.**
- **Question reforms that reinforce carceral logic, and imagine deeper transformation.**
- **Practice accountability and care, both individually and within groups.**
- **Sustain the work through rest, reflection, and shared leadership.**

Remember, abolition is not about perfection or instant change. It is about committing to a process—one that honors the dignity and potential of every person, and that seeks to build systems rooted in care rather than control. There will be setbacks, and the path will not always be clear. But each act of solidarity, each moment of accountability, and each effort to meet needs without punishment is a step toward a more just and liberated world.

You are not alone in this work. Across communities and generations, people are imagining and building new ways of living together—ways that honor our shared humanity and our capacity for transformation. Trust in the power of collective action, nurture your own hope as a discipline, and remember that every small act of care is part of a much larger movement. The world we seek is already taking shape, one relationship, one community, and one act of courage at a time.
