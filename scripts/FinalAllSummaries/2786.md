## Introduction

In the heart of America’s cities, the struggle for stable housing shapes destinies and defines the boundaries of opportunity. The cycle of eviction, often invisible to those outside its grasp, weaves through neighborhoods, uprooting families and fracturing communities. Evicted: Poverty and Profit in the American City by Matthew Desmond immerses itself in the lives of those caught at the intersection of poverty and profit, where landlords and tenants navigate a system that can both sustain and destroy. The relentless churn of moving trucks, court summons, and desperate negotiations reveals how housing insecurity is not just a symptom of poverty, but a powerful engine driving it.

Within this world, the daily choices of landlords, tenants, and city officials ripple outward, shaping the social fabric and economic realities of entire communities. The search for shelter becomes a battle for dignity and survival, as families face impossible decisions and landlords balance compassion with business. Through intimate portraits and unflinching observation, the forces that perpetuate urban poverty come into sharp focus, exposing the deep connections between housing, inequality, and the American dream. The journey through these stories invites a deeper understanding of how the quest for home is at the core of both hardship and hope.

## Chapter 1 The Search for Shelter and the Weight of Uncertainty

Every city has its hidden corners—places where families drift from one apartment to the next, always hoping the next move will bring a little more stability. In these neighborhoods, the search for a safe, affordable home is a constant, pressing concern. The struggle isn’t just about finding four walls and a roof; it’s about holding onto a sense of dignity, security, and hope in a world that seems determined to take them away.

Imagine a mother, her arms full of grocery bags, returning to a cramped apartment where the heat barely works. She’s already behind on rent, and the landlord’s warnings have grown sharper. Each day, she juggles work, childcare, and the gnawing fear that an eviction notice could appear at any moment. For her, and for so many others, housing is never just a backdrop—it’s the stage on which every other part of life plays out.

In the heart of Milwaukee, the daily reality for low-income renters is shaped by a relentless cycle of moving, scraping by, and starting over. The city’s poorest residents spend a huge portion of their income on rent, often more than half of what they bring home. This leaves little for food, medicine, or emergencies. When a job falls through or a child gets sick, the rent money disappears, and the threat of eviction looms larger.

Landlords, too, are part of this story. Some own dozens of properties, managing them with a mix of calculation and fatigue. For them, each missed payment is a risk, and the decision to evict is rarely personal—it’s business. Yet, the consequences for tenants are deeply personal. Losing a home means more than just packing up belongings; it means uprooting children from school, losing access to social services, and severing ties with neighbors.

One young man, recently out of work, spends his days searching for odd jobs and his nights worrying about where he’ll sleep next week. He’s fallen behind on rent, and the landlord’s patience is wearing thin. He tries to negotiate, offering to pay what he can, but the numbers never quite add up. When the eviction notice finally comes, it feels like a confirmation of everything he’s feared: that no matter how hard he tries, the system is stacked against him.

For families with children, the stakes are even higher. A single mother, balancing two jobs, watches her kids’ grades slip as they move from one school to another. Each eviction means a new address, new teachers, and new challenges. The children learn to pack quickly and keep quiet, sensing the stress in their mother’s voice. Over time, the constant upheaval chips away at their sense of belonging and stability.

Most people don’t realize how common eviction has become in American cities. It’s not just a rare misfortune—it’s a routine part of life for millions. The process is swift and unforgiving. A missed payment leads to a court summons, and soon after, a sheriff arrives to clear out the apartment. Belongings are piled on the curb, and families scatter, searching for shelter wherever they can find it.

The emotional toll is immense. Shame, anxiety, and exhaustion become constant companions. Parents worry about how the instability will affect their children, while children learn to hide their fears behind forced smiles. The community, too, feels the impact. As families come and go, neighborhoods lose their sense of cohesion. Trust erodes, and the bonds that once held people together begin to fray.

In this world, eviction is both a cause and a consequence of poverty. Losing a home makes it harder to keep a job, stay healthy, or maintain relationships. The cycle repeats, trapping families in a web of instability that’s nearly impossible to escape. It becomes clear that housing is not just a basic need—it’s the foundation on which everything else depends.

## Chapter 2 The Business of Renting and the Calculus of Profit

Behind every eviction notice is a landlord making decisions—sometimes reluctantly, sometimes with cold efficiency. The business of renting to the poor is shaped by a complex mix of risk, reward, and necessity. For property owners, especially those with large portfolios, each unit is an investment, and every tenant is a potential liability.

In neighborhoods where poverty is concentrated, landlords face their own set of challenges. Properties are often in disrepair, and tenants struggle to pay rent on time. Maintenance requests pile up, and the cost of repairs eats into profits. Some landlords respond by cutting corners, delaying fixes, or charging high rents to offset the risk of nonpayment. Others become hardened, seeing tenants less as people and more as numbers on a ledger.

One property owner, managing dozens of low-income units, spends her days fielding calls about broken appliances and leaky roofs. She’s learned to be tough, knowing that leniency can quickly lead to unpaid rent and mounting losses. When a tenant falls behind, she weighs her options: offer a payment plan, start the eviction process, or simply wait and hope things improve. Each choice carries its own risks, and the line between compassion and self-preservation is thin.

For some landlords, the business is lucrative. The demand for affordable housing far outstrips supply, allowing them to charge high rents for substandard units. In these markets, eviction becomes a tool—not just for removing nonpaying tenants, but for cycling through renters and maximizing income. The process is streamlined and efficient, with court systems that favor property owners and offer little protection for tenants.

A single father, recently evicted, searches for a new place to live. He finds that most landlords require hefty deposits and proof of steady income—requirements he can’t meet. His eviction record follows him, making it nearly impossible to secure decent housing. He’s forced to accept a rundown apartment at an inflated price, knowing that he’s one missed payment away from being back on the street.

The economics of renting to the poor are shaped by scarcity and desperation. With so few affordable options, tenants have little bargaining power. Landlords, aware of this imbalance, set the terms. Some exploit the situation, while others simply try to survive in a system that offers few incentives for fairness or compassion.

Public policy plays a role, too. Housing assistance programs are underfunded and difficult to access. Waiting lists stretch for years, and many families never receive help. Without subsidies, the poorest renters are left to fend for themselves in a market that’s stacked against them. The result is a system where profit and poverty are deeply intertwined, and where eviction is both a symptom and a driver of instability.

It becomes clear that the business of renting is not just about supply and demand—it’s about power, vulnerability, and the choices people make when faced with impossible odds. Landlords and tenants are caught in a cycle that benefits neither in the long run, yet the system persists, fueled by a mix of necessity, inertia, and profit.

## Chapter 3 The Human Cost of Losing Home

When a family is forced out of their home, the impact ripples far beyond the loss of a physical space. The experience of eviction leaves deep scars—emotional, financial, and social—that can last for years. For many, the trauma of being uprooted is compounded by the sense of failure and shame that comes with it.

A mother, recently evicted, tries to keep her children’s spirits up as they move into a friend’s basement. She tells them it’s just temporary, but the uncertainty weighs on her. Each day, she searches for work and a new place to live, but the options are limited. The children struggle to adjust, missing their old friends and routines. School becomes a challenge, as frequent moves disrupt their learning and make it hard to keep up.

The loss of a home often leads to a cascade of other problems. Without a stable address, it’s difficult to find or keep a job. Employers are wary of applicants who can’t provide a permanent residence, and the stress of homelessness makes it hard to focus. Health suffers, too. Living in overcrowded or unsafe conditions increases the risk of illness and injury, while the constant stress takes a toll on mental well-being.

For those with eviction records, the stigma is hard to shake. Landlords are reluctant to rent to anyone with a history of nonpayment or legal trouble, creating a cycle of rejection and instability. Some families end up in shelters, while others double up with relatives or friends. The lack of privacy and security makes it difficult to rebuild, and the sense of isolation grows.

Children are especially vulnerable. Frequent moves disrupt their education, making it hard to form lasting friendships or keep up with schoolwork. Teachers notice the signs—tired eyes, missing homework, sudden absences—but resources are limited. Over time, the instability chips away at children’s confidence and sense of belonging, setting the stage for future struggles.

The community feels the impact, too. As families come and go, neighborhoods lose their sense of continuity. Trust erodes, and the bonds that once held people together begin to fray. Local businesses suffer, schools struggle to keep up, and the cycle of poverty deepens.

Most people don’t see the full extent of the damage caused by eviction. It’s easy to blame individuals for their circumstances, but the reality is far more complex. The system is designed in a way that makes it nearly impossible for the poorest families to get ahead. Without stable housing, every other part of life becomes harder—work, health, education, and relationships all suffer.

It becomes clear that eviction is not just a personal failure—it’s a structural problem that affects entire communities. The cost is measured not just in lost homes, but in lost potential, broken families, and weakened neighborhoods. The cycle continues, trapping generation after generation in a web of instability and disadvantage.

## Chapter 4 Race, Discrimination, and the Uneven Burden of Eviction

In the landscape of American cities, the burden of eviction does not fall evenly. Race and discrimination shape who is most at risk, and the legacy of segregation continues to cast a long shadow over housing and opportunity.

In many neighborhoods, Black and Latino families are disproportionately affected by eviction. The reasons are complex, rooted in a history of redlining, discriminatory lending, and unequal access to resources. Even today, families of color are more likely to live in high-poverty areas, pay higher rents, and face greater barriers to stable housing.

A grandmother, raising her grandchildren after their mother’s eviction, struggles to keep up with the rent on her fixed income. She’s lived in the same neighborhood for decades, but rising costs and declining wages have made it harder to stay. When a new landlord takes over the building, she’s given notice to leave. The search for a new home is daunting—most landlords require background checks and high deposits, and her options are limited by both her income and her race.

Discrimination is often subtle, but its effects are profound. Landlords may steer families of color toward certain neighborhoods or refuse to rent to them altogether. Housing assistance programs, meant to level the playing field, are often underfunded and difficult to access. The result is a system where the most vulnerable are pushed to the margins, forced to accept substandard housing at inflated prices.

The intersection of race and poverty creates a double bind. Families of color are more likely to experience job loss, wage stagnation, and health problems—all factors that increase the risk of eviction. When they do lose their homes, the consequences are even more severe. The stigma of eviction, combined with racial bias, makes it nearly impossible to find new housing. Many end up in shelters or on the streets, their lives upended by forces beyond their control.

Children bear the brunt of this instability. In segregated neighborhoods, schools are underfunded and overcrowded. Frequent moves disrupt learning and make it hard to form lasting friendships. The cycle of poverty and eviction becomes self-perpetuating, as each setback makes it harder to break free.

Most people don’t see the ways in which race shapes the experience of eviction. It’s easy to overlook the structural barriers that keep families trapped in poverty. But for those living on the edge, the reality is inescapable. The system is designed in a way that makes it nearly impossible to get ahead, and the consequences are felt for generations.

It becomes clear that addressing eviction requires more than just individual effort—it demands a reckoning with the history and ongoing reality of racial discrimination. Without meaningful change, the cycle will continue, and the promise of equal opportunity will remain out of reach for millions.

## Chapter 5 The Limits of Public Policy and the Need for Empathy

As the housing crisis deepens, many look to public policy for solutions. Yet, the safety net is full of holes, and the support that does exist often fails to reach those who need it most. The gap between what is needed and what is provided is wide, leaving millions to fend for themselves in a system that offers little protection.

Housing assistance programs, such as vouchers and public housing, are chronically underfunded. Waiting lists stretch for years, and many families never receive help. Those who do often find that the assistance is not enough to cover rising rents. The process is bureaucratic and confusing, with strict eligibility requirements that exclude many of the most vulnerable.

A single mother, recently approved for a housing voucher, searches for an apartment that will accept it. She’s turned away again and again, as landlords refuse to rent to voucher holders. The few units that are available are in poor condition, far from work and school. The promise of stability remains just out of reach, and the stress of the search takes its toll.

Policy failures are not just a matter of funding—they reflect deeper attitudes about poverty and deservingness. Many believe that those who struggle to pay rent are simply not trying hard enough, ignoring the structural barriers that make it nearly impossible to get ahead. This mindset shapes the design of assistance programs, making them punitive and difficult to access.

The result is a system that punishes the poor for their poverty. Eviction records follow families for years, making it hard to find new housing or secure employment. The lack of affordable options forces people into unsafe or overcrowded conditions, increasing the risk of illness and instability. The cycle continues, with each setback making it harder to recover.

Empathy is often in short supply. Most people don’t see the daily struggles of those living on the edge, and the stories of eviction are rarely told. Yet, understanding the lived experience of poverty is essential to creating meaningful change. It’s not enough to tweak policies or increase funding—what’s needed is a shift in perspective, a recognition of the humanity and dignity of those who are most vulnerable.

The path forward is not easy. It requires confronting uncomfortable truths about inequality, discrimination, and the limits of the current system. But it also offers the possibility of something better—a society where everyone has a safe, stable place to call home, and where the cycle of eviction and poverty is finally broken.

## Chapter 6 The Machinery of Eviction—Courts, Sheriffs, and the Unseen Hand

The day an eviction becomes official, the process shifts from private struggle to public spectacle. For many, the courthouse is a place of last resort—a place where hope flickers, but rarely survives. The machinery of eviction is swift, impersonal, and deeply intimidating.

Picture a father, clutching a stack of crumpled pay stubs, waiting his turn in a crowded hallway. He rehearses what he’ll say to the judge, hoping for a little more time. Around him, others sit in silence, eyes fixed on the floor. The proceedings move quickly. Most tenants don’t have lawyers; most landlords do. The judge, pressed for time, listens for a few minutes before issuing a decision. For the father, the verdict is devastating: he has a week to leave.

The sheriff’s arrival is the final act. There’s no drama—just a knock, a few words, and the slow, painful process of moving out. Belongings are set on the curb, sometimes in the rain. Neighbors watch, some offering quiet sympathy, others looking away. The family gathers what they can, leaving behind what won’t fit in a borrowed car.

Most people don’t realize how routine this process has become. In cities like Milwaukee, eviction court is a daily event, with dozens of cases heard in a single morning. The system is designed for efficiency, not compassion. There’s little time to consider the circumstances that led to nonpayment, or the consequences of losing a home. For landlords, the process is a necessary tool; for tenants, it’s a gauntlet they’re rarely equipped to survive.

The legal system, meant to provide fairness, often deepens the imbalance of power. Without legal representation, tenants struggle to navigate the rules and procedures. Many don’t even show up, knowing the odds are against them. The result is a process that feels less like justice and more like a conveyor belt—one that moves families from home to homelessness with chilling regularity.

It becomes clear that eviction is not just a personal crisis—it’s a public event, shaped by institutions that prioritize order over mercy. The machinery grinds on, indifferent to the pain it leaves in its wake.

## Chapter 7 The Landlord’s Dilemma—Profit, Risk, and the Weight of Responsibility

Behind every eviction is a landlord making choices—sometimes reluctantly, sometimes with resignation. The business of renting to the poor is fraught with risk, and the line between survival and exploitation is often blurred.

Consider a property owner who started with a single duplex, hoping to build a better life. Over the years, she’s acquired more units, each one a mix of opportunity and headache. She fields calls at all hours—broken furnaces, leaking roofs, tenants behind on rent. The margins are thin, and the stress is constant.

When a tenant falls behind, she faces a dilemma. If she lets it slide, she risks missing her own mortgage payment. If she evicts, she knows she’s upending a family’s life. The decision is rarely easy. Sometimes, she tries to work out a payment plan, but the math rarely works in her favor. Other times, she feels hardened by experience, seeing eviction as a necessary part of the business.

Some landlords, especially those with large portfolios, become distant—managing properties through spreadsheets and property managers. For them, tenants are numbers, and eviction is a tool for managing risk. Others, especially small-scale owners, feel the weight of each decision. They know their tenants’ stories, and the choice to evict is never made lightly.

The system offers few incentives for compassion. Insurance companies, lenders, and city inspectors all demand compliance and payment. The cost of repairs, taxes, and vacancies eats into profits. In this environment, eviction becomes a way to manage uncertainty—a blunt instrument in a world of constant risk.

Most people don’t see the pressures landlords face. It’s easy to paint them as villains, but the reality is more complicated. Many are caught in a system that rewards efficiency over empathy, and the choices they make are shaped by forces beyond their control.

It becomes clear that the landlord’s dilemma is not just about money—it’s about responsibility, risk, and the limits of individual action in a broken system.

## Chapter 8 The Ripple Effects—Health, Work, and the Fragility of Stability

Losing a home is never just about moving. The effects ripple outward, touching every part of life. For many, eviction marks the beginning of a long, difficult journey—one that tests resilience and hope at every turn.

A woman, recently evicted, finds herself living in a motel with her two children. The room is cramped, the air stale. She spends her days searching for work, her nights worrying about the future. The children struggle to sleep, their routines shattered. School becomes a challenge, as the daily commute grows longer and more complicated.

The stress of instability takes a toll on health. Chronic conditions worsen, and new problems emerge. Without a stable address, it’s hard to keep up with doctor’s appointments or fill prescriptions. The constant anxiety makes it hard to focus, and depression sets in. For children, the effects are even more pronounced—missed vaccinations, untreated illnesses, and the emotional scars of upheaval.

Work becomes harder to find and keep. Employers are wary of applicants without a permanent address, and the logistics of getting to work from a shelter or motel are daunting. Missed shifts lead to lost jobs, and the cycle deepens. For those with eviction records, the stigma lingers, making it even harder to start over.

The community feels the impact, too. As families move in and out, neighborhoods lose their sense of continuity. Schools struggle to keep up with the constant churn, and local businesses suffer. The bonds that once held people together begin to fray, replaced by a sense of transience and uncertainty.

Most people don’t see the full extent of the damage caused by eviction. It’s easy to focus on the immediate crisis, but the effects linger for years. The loss of a home sets off a chain reaction, making it harder to recover and rebuild.

It becomes clear that stability is fragile, and that the loss of a home is never just a single event—it’s the start of a long, difficult journey.

For families who lose their homes, the financial fallout is often devastating. The costs of eviction go far beyond the loss of a security deposit or a few months’ rent. Debt piles up, credit scores plummet, and the path to recovery is steep.

A man, evicted after losing his job, finds himself facing a mountain of bills. The landlord sues for unpaid rent, and the court adds fees and interest. The man’s credit score drops, making it harder to rent a new apartment or secure a loan. He takes on odd jobs, but the money never seems to stretch far enough.

The debt follows him everywhere. Collection agencies call at all hours, and the stress is constant. Without good credit, he’s forced to accept substandard housing at inflated prices. The cycle continues, with each setback making it harder to get ahead.

For many, the financial damage lasts for years. Eviction records are public, and landlords routinely check them before renting to new tenants. Even a single eviction can make it nearly impossible to find decent housing. Some families end up in shelters, while others double up with relatives or friends.

The lack of affordable housing compounds the problem. With so few options, tenants have little bargaining power. Landlords set the terms, and those with eviction records are at the bottom of the list. The result is a system where the poorest families pay the highest prices for the worst housing.

Most people don’t see the hidden costs of eviction. It’s easy to focus on the immediate crisis, but the financial fallout lingers for years. The loss of a home sets off a chain reaction, making it harder to recover and rebuild.

It becomes clear that eviction is not just a personal crisis—it’s a financial catastrophe that can take years to overcome.
