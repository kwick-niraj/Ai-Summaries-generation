## Introduction

A sharp wind of cultural reckoning sweeps through the landscape, challenging the stories that have long shaped collective beliefs about power, gender, and truth. In The Witches Are Coming, Lindy West confronts the myths and narratives that have allowed misogyny, bigotry, and denialism to flourish in modern society. The specter of the "witch hunt" is reclaimed, not as a tool of oppression, but as a rallying cry for those who refuse to be silenced or gaslit by the status quo. Through incisive wit and unflinching honesty, the conversation turns to the ways in which pop culture, politics, and media have conspired to obscure reality and perpetuate injustice.

As the threads of outrage, humor, and vulnerability intertwine, a tapestry emerges that exposes the absurdities and dangers of dismissing women's voices and experiences. The world is seen through a lens that refuses to accept easy answers or comfortable lies, instead demanding accountability and change. With each argument and anecdote, the call grows louder for a society that recognizes the harm in ignoring inconvenient truths. The journey through these ideas invites a deeper understanding of how cultural forces shape perceptions and the urgent need to challenge them.

## Chapter 1 How Stories Shape What We See and Ignore

It’s easy to believe that the world is made up of facts and logic, that people act on what’s true and right. But most days, what really guides choices and opinions are the stories people tell themselves and each other. These stories—about who’s good, who’s bad, what’s possible, and what’s not—are everywhere, woven into news headlines, movies, jokes, and even the way friends talk over dinner. They’re so familiar that most don’t notice them at all.

Imagine a group of coworkers gathered around a break room table, scrolling through their phones. One of them, tired from a long week, sighs at a headline about another public figure accused of harassment. Someone else shrugs, saying, “It’s just a witch hunt. People are too sensitive these days.” The others nod, eager to move on. In that moment, a story is reinforced: that accusations are overblown, that the real danger is to innocent men, that the world is already fair enough.

This isn’t just about one conversation. It’s about the way entire cultures decide what’s real and what’s exaggerated. The myth of the “witch hunt” is a favorite—an old story dusted off and used to defend the powerful. It suggests that those who speak up about injustice are hysterical, out for blood, or simply making things up. The real pain, the real harm, is pushed aside.

But the truth is, these stories don’t come from nowhere. They’re built and rebuilt by people who benefit from them. When a famous comedian is accused of wrongdoing, the narrative quickly shifts: “He’s a good guy. He’s always been nice to me.” The focus moves from the harm done to the discomfort of the accused. The story becomes about redemption, misunderstanding, or the dangers of overreaction.

Most people don’t realize how much these stories shape what’s possible. They decide who gets believed, who gets a second chance, and who gets ignored. They make it easy to pretend that sexism, racism, and other forms of bigotry are things of the past, or at least not as bad as some claim. The myth that “things are better now” is comforting, but it’s also a way to avoid responsibility.

There’s a reason these myths are so persistent. They offer a way out—a way to avoid the hard work of change. If the problem is exaggerated, then there’s no need to do anything. If the real victims are the accused, then the rest can relax. It’s a story that keeps the status quo safe.

But every so often, someone refuses to play along. A friend at the table might quietly say, “But what if it’s true? What if people are finally being heard?” The room goes quiet. It’s uncomfortable, but it’s also a crack in the story. In that moment, it becomes clear that the stories people tell aren’t just entertainment—they’re the rules everyone lives by.

The power of narrative isn’t just in what it says, but in what it allows people to ignore. When the same myths are repeated over and over, they become invisible. They shape laws, workplaces, and even the way people see themselves. Most don’t notice until someone points it out, and even then, it’s tempting to look away.

But the only way to change the story is to see it for what it is. To notice the patterns, to ask who benefits, and to refuse to accept easy answers. It’s not about being perfect or always knowing what to say. It’s about being willing to see the world as it is, not just as it’s been described.

## Chapter 2 The Myth of Progress and the Denial of Harm

There’s a comforting belief that things are always getting better, that each generation is more enlightened than the last. It’s a story told in classrooms, in campaign speeches, and in the way people talk about the past. The idea is that sexism, racism, and other forms of bigotry are relics—problems that have mostly been solved.

But for many, this story doesn’t match reality. A young woman, fresh out of college, starts her first job in a bustling office. She’s excited, hopeful, and ready to prove herself. But soon, she notices the small things: the way her ideas are ignored until repeated by someone else, the jokes that make her uncomfortable, the way promotions seem to go to the same kind of people. When she brings it up, she’s told, “That’s just how it is. Things are better than they used to be.”

This is the myth of progress at work. It’s a way to dismiss real problems by comparing them to the worst moments of the past. “At least you can vote now,” someone might say, as if that erases everything else. The message is clear: be grateful, don’t complain, and trust that things will keep improving on their own.

But progress isn’t automatic. It’s not a law of nature. It happens because people demand it, often at great personal cost. The myth that things are always getting better is a way to avoid that work. It allows those in power to ignore the harm that still exists, to pretend that the fight is over.

This denial isn’t just about big issues. It shows up in everyday life, in the way people talk about movies, music, and even jokes. When a beloved film from the past is criticized for its sexism or racism, the response is often defensive. “It was a different time,” people say. “Don’t be so sensitive.” The story is that the past was bad, but now things are fine.

But the truth is, culture doesn’t change just because time passes. Harmful ideas linger, passed down in subtle ways. A parent might laugh at a joke on TV, not realizing that their child is learning what’s acceptable. A group of friends might repeat a story about a “crazy ex-girlfriend,” not noticing how it shapes their view of women. These aren’t just harmless habits—they’re the building blocks of bigger problems.

Most people want to believe they’re on the right side of history. They want to think that if they had lived in the past, they would have been the ones to speak up. But the real test isn’t what someone would have done then—it’s what they’re willing to do now. It’s easy to condemn the obvious villains of history. It’s harder to notice the ways the same patterns show up today.

The myth of progress is powerful because it offers an excuse. It says, “You’ve done enough. You don’t need to do more.” But real change requires more than good intentions. It means listening, noticing, and being willing to challenge the stories that make people comfortable.

Sometimes, it’s as simple as asking a question. When someone says, “Things are better now,” it’s worth wondering: better for whom? Who still gets left out? Who still pays the price for other people’s comfort? These aren’t easy questions, but they’re the ones that matter.

The denial of harm isn’t just about ignoring the past—it’s about refusing to see the present. It’s a way to protect the stories people like, even when those stories hurt others. But the only way forward is to face what’s real, not just what’s reassuring.

## Chapter 3 Pop Culture’s Role in Reinforcing and Challenging Norms

Most people grow up surrounded by stories—on TV, in movies, in music, and online. These stories aren’t just entertainment; they’re lessons about what’s normal, what’s funny, and what’s possible. They teach who gets to be the hero, who’s the punchline, and who’s invisible.

Think of a teenager, curled up on the couch, watching a favorite sitcom. The jokes come fast, the characters are familiar, and the world feels safe. But beneath the laughter, there are patterns. The women are often there to support the men, the jokes about race or gender are brushed off, and anyone who doesn’t fit the mold is treated as a problem to be solved or a joke to be made.

It’s easy to miss these patterns, especially when they’re wrapped in nostalgia. People remember the shows and movies they loved as kids, and it’s uncomfortable to admit that some of those stories were harmful. When someone points it out, the reaction is often defensive. “It’s just a joke,” people say. “Don’t ruin it.”

But pop culture isn’t neutral. It shapes the way people see themselves and each other. When the same kinds of characters are always the heroes, and others are always the sidekicks or villains, it sends a message about who matters. When jokes about women, people of color, or LGBTQ+ people are treated as harmless, it teaches that their pain doesn’t count.

This isn’t just about old shows. Even new stories can fall into the same traps. A blockbuster movie might feature a “strong female character,” but she’s still defined by how she relates to the men around her. A popular song might celebrate independence, but only within narrow limits. The surface changes, but the underlying message stays the same.

But pop culture can also be a place of resistance. Sometimes, a new show or movie breaks the pattern, offering characters and stories that haven’t been seen before. A group of friends might gather to watch a series that centers on women’s experiences, or a film that tells the truth about racism. These moments matter—not just because they’re entertaining, but because they offer new ways to see the world.

It’s not about demanding perfection from every story. It’s about noticing the patterns, asking questions, and being willing to let go of what doesn’t work anymore. When someone says, “That joke isn’t funny,” it’s not an attack—it’s an invitation to think differently.

Most people don’t realize how much pop culture shapes their beliefs. They think they’re just watching TV or listening to music. But over time, these stories add up. They become the background noise of daily life, the assumptions people don’t even notice.

But it’s possible to change the channel. To seek out stories that challenge old ideas, to support creators who tell the truth, and to be honest about what needs to change. It’s not always comfortable, but it’s necessary.

Pop culture is powerful because it feels personal. The stories people love become part of who they are. But that’s also why it matters so much. When the stories change, so does the world.

## Chapter 4 The Backlash Against Social Progress and the Power of Anger

Whenever there’s a push for change—whether it’s more women in leadership, better representation in media, or accountability for those who abuse power—there’s always a backlash. It’s as predictable as the sunrise. The more people demand justice, the louder the resistance becomes.

Picture a group of friends debating over dinner. One of them, frustrated by the news, says, “Why does everything have to be political now? Can’t we just enjoy things?” Another, quieter but determined, points out that for some, politics isn’t a choice—it’s survival. The conversation grows tense. Someone mutters about “outrage culture” and “witch hunts,” and the room grows colder.

This backlash isn’t new. It’s a pattern that repeats every time marginalized people demand to be heard. The language changes, but the message is the same: “You’re going too far. You’re making things worse. You’re the real problem.” The anger of those demanding change is treated as dangerous, irrational, or even evil.

The term “witch hunt” gets thrown around a lot. It’s meant to suggest that innocent people are being persecuted, that the real danger is to those in power. But the truth is, most of the time, the so-called “witches” are just people asking for fairness. The real harm isn’t in the accusations—it’s in the way the system protects itself.

Anger is often seen as a problem, especially when it comes from women or other marginalized groups. People are told to calm down, to be reasonable, to wait their turn. But sometimes, anger is the only thing that gets attention. It’s a sign that something is wrong, that the old stories aren’t working anymore.

There’s power in refusing to be quiet. When people speak up, even when it’s uncomfortable, they force others to pay attention. It’s not about being perfect or always having the right words. It’s about refusing to accept the unacceptable.

The backlash is a sign that change is happening. It means the old stories are being challenged, that people are no longer willing to play along. It’s not easy—there are real risks, real costs. But the alternative is to stay silent, to let the same patterns repeat.

Sometimes, the most radical thing someone can do is to refuse to be polite. To say, “This isn’t good enough,” even when everyone else wants to move on. It’s not about being angry for the sake of it—it’s about demanding better.

The power of anger isn’t in destruction, but in clarity. It cuts through the excuses, the denial, the comfortable myths. It says, “This matters. I matter. We all deserve better.” That’s why the backlash is so fierce—because those in power know that once people stop believing the old stories, anything is possible.

## Chapter 5 Everyday Choices and the Demand for Accountability

Most people like to think of themselves as good. They recycle, donate to charity, and try to be kind. But being good isn’t just about intentions—it’s about choices, big and small. Every day, people make decisions that shape the world, even when they don’t realize it.

Consider someone scrolling through social media, deciding what to watch or share. A video pops up—funny, but at someone else’s expense. It’s easy to laugh and move on. But each click, each share, is a small vote for what’s acceptable. Over time, these choices add up.

Accountability isn’t just for politicians or celebrities. It’s for everyone. When someone laughs at a sexist joke, ignores a racist comment, or stays silent when a friend is hurt, they’re part of the problem. It’s not about being perfect—it’s about noticing, about being willing to do better.

There’s a temptation to believe that small actions don’t matter. That one person can’t make a difference. But culture is built from millions of tiny choices. When enough people decide that something isn’t funny, or that certain behavior isn’t acceptable, things start to change.

It’s not always easy. Speaking up can be uncomfortable, even risky. It can mean losing friends, being called difficult, or feeling alone. But silence is its own kind of choice—a way of saying that the status quo is good enough.

Accountability means being honest, even when it’s hard. It means listening when someone says they’ve been hurt, instead of getting defensive. It means being willing to change, to let go of old habits, and to demand better from others.

Most people want to believe they’re on the right side. But the real test is in the moments when no one is watching. When someone chooses to speak up, to listen, or to change their mind, they’re helping to build a different story.

The demand for accountability isn’t about punishment—it’s about possibility. It’s about creating a world where everyone is treated with respect, where harm isn’t ignored, and where the stories people tell are true.

It’s not about being perfect. It’s about being willing to try, to fail, and to keep going. Every choice matters, even the small ones. That’s how change happens—not all at once, but one decision at a time.

## Chapter 6 Where the Personal and Political Collide

It’s easy to imagine that politics is something that happens far away—in government buildings, on TV, in the hands of people with power. But for most, politics is woven into the fabric of daily life, showing up in the smallest, most personal moments. The line between what’s personal and what’s political is blurrier than it seems.

Picture a parent at a playground, watching their child navigate a group of kids. The parent notices how the boys take up space, how the girls are expected to be quiet, how a child who doesn’t fit in is left out. The parent wants to step in, to make things fair, but worries about being “that parent.” The rules of the playground aren’t written down, but everyone knows them. They’re the same rules that show up in offices, in schools, in the stories people tell about who gets to lead and who has to follow.

Most people don’t realize how much their private lives are shaped by public forces. The way a woman is treated at work, the way a trans kid is welcomed or excluded at school, the way a family talks about race at the dinner table—these are all political acts, even if they don’t feel like it. The stories people inherit about what’s normal, what’s possible, and what’s right are shaped by laws, by media, by the choices of those in power.

Sometimes, the collision between the personal and the political is sudden and sharp. A teenager comes out to their family and is met with silence, or worse. A worker tries to organize for better pay and is told to be grateful for what they have. In these moments, it becomes clear that the personal is never just personal. The rules that govern people’s lives are bigger than any one person, but they’re enforced in the most intimate ways.

But there’s power in recognizing this connection. When people see that their struggles aren’t just their own—that they’re part of a bigger pattern—it becomes possible to imagine change. A parent who speaks up at the playground, a worker who refuses to stay silent, a friend who challenges a cruel joke—these small acts are political, even if they don’t look like it.

Most people want to believe that they’re in control of their own lives. But the truth is, everyone is shaped by forces they can’t see. The good news is that by noticing these patterns, by refusing to accept them as inevitable, people can start to change them. The personal and the political aren’t separate—they’re two sides of the same story.

## Chapter 7 Reclaiming “Witchiness” as Power

For centuries, the word “witch” has been used as a weapon—a way to punish women who were too loud, too independent, too different. The image of the witch is everywhere: in fairy tales, in Halloween costumes, in the way people talk about women who refuse to play by the rules. It’s a story that’s been told over and over, always with the same message: be careful, or you’ll be next.

But something has shifted. In recent years, more and more people have started to reclaim the idea of the witch—not as a figure of fear, but as a symbol of power. The witch is no longer just a villain; she’s a survivor, a rebel, a person who refuses to be silenced.

Imagine a group of friends gathered around a kitchen table, lighting candles and sharing stories. They talk about the ways they’ve been called “difficult,” “bossy,” or “too much.” They laugh, but there’s an edge to it—a recognition that these words are meant to keep them in line. Someone pulls out a deck of tarot cards, and the mood shifts. The cards aren’t magic, not really, but they’re a way to claim space, to say, “I get to decide who I am.”

Reclaiming “witchiness” isn’t about spells or potions. It’s about refusing to be afraid of your own power. It’s about seeing the ways that fear is used to control people, and choosing not to play along. When someone calls a woman a witch, they’re trying to make her small. But when she claims the word for herself, she becomes bigger than their fear.

This isn’t just about women. The story of the witch is a story about anyone who doesn’t fit, anyone who refuses to be quiet. It’s about queer people, people of color, anyone who’s been told they’re too much or not enough. The witch is a symbol of resistance—a reminder that the things people are taught to fear are often the things that make them strong.

Most people don’t realize how powerful it is to claim a story for yourself. When someone says, “Yes, I’m a witch,” they’re refusing to be defined by other people’s fear. They’re saying, “I get to decide what’s possible.” That’s real magic.

## Chapter 8 The Ongoing Necessity of Resistance

It would be comforting to believe that the work is done—that the battles for justice have been fought and won, that the world is as fair as it’s going to get. But every day brings new reminders that the fight isn’t over. The forces that resist change are persistent, creative, and well-practiced. They know how to adapt, how to repackage old ideas in new language, how to make the status quo seem inevitable.

Think of a teacher in a small town, trying to introduce new books to her classroom. She wants her students to see themselves in the stories they read, to learn about people who are different from them. But every step forward is met with pushback—from parents, from administrators, from the community. The teacher is told to “stick to the classics,” to avoid “controversy.” The message is clear: don’t rock the boat.

But resistance isn’t just about big protests or dramatic confrontations. It’s about the quiet, everyday choices people make to tell the truth, to refuse to look away, to keep pushing even when it’s hard. It’s about the parent who challenges a school board, the worker who organizes for better conditions, the friend who refuses to laugh at a cruel joke.

Most people want to believe that things will get better on their own. But the truth is, progress only happens when people refuse to accept less. The forces that benefit from the way things are will always find new ways to defend themselves. They’ll call for “civility,” for “patience,” for “understanding”—anything to slow things down.

But resistance is necessary, even when it’s exhausting. It’s what keeps the door open for those who come next. It’s what makes it possible for people to imagine a different world. The work is never finished, but that doesn’t mean it’s not worth doing.

Sometimes, resistance looks like anger. Sometimes it looks like hope. Most of the time, it looks like ordinary people refusing to give up. That’s how change happens—not all at once, but through the stubborn refusal to accept what’s unacceptable.

## Chapter 9 Critique as Care: The Importance of Speaking Up

It’s tempting to believe that criticism is always negative, that pointing out problems is just complaining. But real critique is an act of care—a way of saying, “I believe this can be better.” When people refuse to accept easy answers, when they ask hard questions, they’re not tearing things down. They’re making space for something new.

Imagine a group of coworkers watching a new show together. Most of them love it, but one person hesitates. She points out a scene that felt off, a joke that landed wrong. The others roll their eyes, accuse her of being too sensitive. But she doesn’t back down. She explains why it matters, how small things add up, how stories shape the way people see the world.

It’s not easy to be the one who speaks up. It can mean being labeled as difficult, as negative, as a killjoy. But critique is how things change. When someone points out a problem, they’re opening the door to something better. They’re refusing to settle for less.

Most people want to be liked. They want to fit in, to avoid conflict. But sometimes, the most loving thing someone can do is to tell the truth. To say, “This isn’t good enough,” even when it’s uncomfortable. Critique isn’t about tearing things down—it’s about building something stronger.

The world doesn’t change because people are polite. It changes because people are willing to ask for more. When someone speaks up, they’re showing that they care—not just about themselves, but about everyone who comes after.

It’s easy to dismiss criticism as negativity. But the truth is, the people who care the most are often the ones who refuse to look away. They’re the ones who believe that things can be better, and who are willing to do the work to make it so.

## Chapter 10 Living With Uncertainty and Choosing Hope

There’s no tidy ending to the story. The world is messy, complicated, and full of contradictions. Most people want certainty—a clear answer, a happy ending, a guarantee that things will work out. But real life doesn’t offer those kinds of promises.

Picture someone lying awake at night, worrying about the future. They wonder if anything they do really matters, if the world can ever change, if it’s worth trying at all. The doubts are real, and so is the fear. But in the quiet, something else emerges—a stubborn hope, a belief that things can be different.

Living with uncertainty isn’t easy. It means accepting that there are no guarantees, that progress isn’t inevitable, that setbacks are part of the process. It means being willing to try, even when the outcome isn’t clear. It means choosing hope, not because it’s easy, but because it’s necessary.

Most people want to believe that their actions matter. And the truth is, they do—even when the results aren’t obvious, even when change is slow. Every time someone refuses to accept the unacceptable, every time they speak up, every time they choose to care, they’re making a difference.

Hope isn’t about ignoring reality. It’s about facing it, and choosing to act anyway. It’s about believing that the story isn’t finished, that there’s still time to make things better. It’s about refusing to give up, even when it would be easier to look away.

The world is full of uncertainty. But it’s also full of possibility. The stories people tell, the choices they make, the ways they care for each other—these are the things that shape the future. There are no guarantees, but there’s always hope.

## Conclusion: How to Change the Story

If you’ve made it this far, you already know: the world’s stories are powerful. They shape what people see, what they ignore, and what they believe is possible. But stories aren’t set in stone. They can be questioned, challenged, and rewritten—one conversation, one choice, one act of courage at a time.

Here’s what to remember as you move forward:

- Every story you accept or reject shapes what’s possible for everyone else.
- Small acts of resistance—speaking up, listening, refusing to laugh—add up over time.
- Critique is an act of care, not destruction; it opens the door to better.
- The personal is always political, even when it feels private or small.
- Hope is a choice, not a guarantee; it’s what keeps the story moving forward.

You don’t have to be perfect. You don’t have to have all the answers. What matters is that you’re willing to notice the patterns, to ask hard questions, and to refuse to accept easy lies. The world changes when ordinary people decide that the old stories aren’t good enough anymore. It changes when someone chooses to care, to speak up, to try again even after failing.

There will be backlash. There will be moments of doubt, of exhaustion, of fear. But there will also be moments of connection, of clarity, of real progress. The work is never finished, but it’s always worth doing. You are not alone in this. Every time you choose to see, to listen, to act, you’re helping to write a new story—one where everyone matters, and where hope is always possible. Keep going. The story isn’t over yet.
