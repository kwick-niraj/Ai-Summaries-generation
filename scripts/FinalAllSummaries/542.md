## Introduction

Class divisions have shaped the American experience since the earliest days of colonization, weaving a complex tapestry of privilege, exclusion, and aspiration. The notion of a classless society has long been a cherished ideal, yet the reality tells a different story—one where social hierarchies persist, and the label of “white trash” becomes a powerful symbol of marginalization. In White Trash: The 400-Year Untold History of Class in America, Nancy Isenberg uncovers the deep roots of class prejudice, tracing how poor whites have been stigmatized, manipulated, and used as political pawns across centuries. The narrative challenges the myth of equal opportunity, revealing how class has always been a defining force in shaping identity, opportunity, and power in the United States.

From the earliest British settlements to the modern era, the journey of America’s so-called “waste people” unfolds through shifting landscapes of land, labor, and law. The story moves through colonial plantations, Civil War battlefields, and the rise of trailer parks, exposing the persistent stereotypes and policies that have kept class boundaries firmly in place. By examining the language, politics, and cultural attitudes that have defined and confined poor whites, Isenberg’s work invites a deeper understanding of how class continues to influence American life. The exploration of these hidden histories sets the stage for a broader conversation about inequality, identity, and the ongoing struggle for dignity and recognition.

## Chapter 1 The Roots of Class in Early America

It’s easy to imagine the founding of America as a story of bold adventurers and hopeful settlers, all arriving with equal chances to shape their destinies. But beneath the surface of this familiar tale, a quieter truth lingers—one that’s rarely discussed in classrooms or family stories. From the very beginning, the land that would become the United States was marked by deep divisions, not just of race or religion, but of class. The idea that America was a blank slate, free from the rigid hierarchies of Europe, was more wish than reality.

Picture a young laborer, hands rough from work, standing on the edge of a Virginia tobacco field in the early 1600s. He’s not a landowner, nor is he free to choose his path. Instead, he’s bound by a contract, his future mortgaged for the price of passage across the Atlantic. Around him, others toil under the same system—indentured servants, many of them poor and desperate, hoping that years of backbreaking labor might one day buy them a sliver of independence. But for most, the promise of land and freedom remains just out of reach.

The English who settled the colonies brought with them more than just their language and customs. They carried old ideas about who belonged where in society. In England, the poor were often seen as a problem to be managed, not as equals. The New World, in their minds, was a place to send the “waste people”—the vagrants, the criminals, the unwanted. Colonial leaders spoke openly about populating the land with those who could be “improved” through hard work, but who were always kept at arm’s length from the privileges of the elite.

As towns and plantations grew, so did the lines that separated people. Laws were written to keep the poor in their place. Land was parceled out in ways that favored the wealthy, and those without property found themselves shut out of political life. Even as the rhetoric of freedom and opportunity grew louder, the reality for many was one of exclusion and struggle.

In the backcountry, families scraped by on thin soil, far from the centers of power. They were called squatters, crackers, or simply “trash”—labels that stung and stuck. These names weren’t just insults; they were tools used to mark who mattered and who didn’t. The myth of a classless America was already being built, but it rested on the backs of those who were told, in a hundred subtle ways, that they would never truly belong.

Over time, the divide between the “better sort” and the rest became woven into the fabric of American life. The dream of upward mobility was dangled before the poor, but the ladder was often missing rungs. Most people didn’t notice how the rules were written to keep some people down. The story of America as a land of equal opportunity was powerful, but for many, it was just that—a story.

## Chapter 2 The Invention and Stigma of “White Trash”

As the colonies matured, the language used to describe the poor grew sharper and more creative. New words emerged—“redneck,” “clay-eater,” “sand-hiller”—each one a shorthand for poverty, ignorance, or laziness. These labels did more than describe; they defined. They turned whole groups of people into caricatures, easy to dismiss or ridicule.

Imagine a family living in a ramshackle cabin on the edge of a southern pine forest. Their clothes are patched, their meals simple. Neighbors whisper about their habits, their supposed lack of ambition. Children grow up hearing that they are “no account,” that their blood is somehow tainted. The shame seeps in, shaping how they see themselves and how others see them.

The term “white trash” didn’t appear overnight. It grew out of a long tradition of using language to draw lines between the “worthy” and the “unworthy.” In the South, where slavery created a rigid racial hierarchy, poor whites occupied a strange and uncomfortable place. They were not enslaved, but they were not respected either. Planters and politicians worried that these “mean whites” might upset the delicate balance of power. Sometimes, they were used as a buffer between the elite and the enslaved, a reminder that even the lowest white person was still above a Black person in the social order.

But this supposed privilege came with a price. Poor whites were often blamed for the region’s problems—accused of laziness, criminality, or moral weakness. Their poverty was seen not as a result of circumstance, but as a sign of personal failure. The idea took hold that some people were simply “waste”—a human version of the land that couldn’t be tamed or improved.

This way of thinking wasn’t limited to the South. In the North, too, the poor were marked by their accents, their clothes, their manners. Reformers and politicians debated what to do about the “dangerous classes,” often proposing solutions that involved more control and less compassion. The language of class became a way to police the boundaries of respectability, to decide who deserved help and who did not.

Over time, the image of “white trash” became a fixture in American culture. It showed up in novels, newspapers, and political speeches. The stereotype was both a warning and a joke—a way to reassure the middle and upper classes that their place in the world was secure. Most people didn’t realize how much these stories shaped their own beliefs about who belonged and who didn’t.

The shame attached to being poor and white was powerful. It could be seen in the way people tried to distance themselves from the label, in the way families hid their struggles or moved away in search of a fresh start. The myth of a classless America depended on pretending that “white trash” was an exception, not a product of the system itself.

## Chapter 3 Class, Race, and the American Dream

As the United States grew, so did the promise of the American Dream—the idea that anyone, regardless of background, could rise through hard work and determination. But for many, this dream was always just out of reach. The reality was that class boundaries were as real and as rigid as ever, even if people didn’t like to talk about them.

In the years after the Revolution, debates about who should have a say in government often came down to questions of property and respectability. Voting rights were tied to land ownership, and those without property were seen as unreliable or dangerous. The fear was that the “rabble” might use their numbers to upset the social order. So, the rules were written to keep power in the hands of the few.

A young man, eager to make his mark, might find himself shut out of opportunity by forces he couldn’t see. He might work hard, save what he could, but still find that the best land was already taken, the best jobs reserved for those with connections. The promise of meritocracy was real for some, but for many, it was a mirage.

The Civil War and its aftermath brought new challenges. The end of slavery upended the old order, but it didn’t erase the lines of class. In the South, poor whites were often pitted against newly freed Black people, each group struggling for a foothold in a world that offered little security. Politicians and landowners used these divisions to their advantage, stoking fears and resentments to keep the lower classes divided.

The language of class became more complicated, tangled up with questions of race and identity. Poor whites were sometimes held up as proof that race alone did not guarantee success. At other times, they were used as a warning about what could happen if people failed to live up to the ideals of hard work and self-improvement.

In the North, industrialization brought new opportunities, but also new forms of exploitation. Factory workers, many of them recent immigrants, faced long hours and low pay. The old labels—“trash,” “scum,” “riffraff”—were applied to these newcomers as well, showing how easily the boundaries of class could shift to include or exclude different groups.

The myth of the classless society persisted, even as evidence to the contrary piled up. Most people wanted to believe that America was different, that anyone could make it if they tried hard enough. But the reality was more complicated. The rules of the game were often hidden, and the deck was stacked in favor of those who already had a head start.

## Chapter 4 Stereotypes, Shame, and Social Control

Throughout American history, the image of poor whites has been shaped by a mix of fear, fascination, and contempt. Stereotypes about “white trash” have appeared in every era, each time reflecting deeper anxieties about class, race, and democracy.

In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, new forms of social science tried to explain why some people seemed to fall behind. Experts studied the habits and homes of the poor, looking for signs of weakness or degeneracy. They published reports filled with photographs and charts, hoping to find a scientific basis for old prejudices.

A mother, struggling to feed her children, might find herself the subject of a social worker’s report. Her home is described in detail—its dirt floor, its patched roof, its lack of books or pictures. The report notes her “shiftlessness,” her “lack of ambition.” The message is clear: her poverty is not just a matter of bad luck, but of bad character.

These studies were used to justify new forms of social control. Reformers argued that the poor needed to be taught better habits, or even removed from their homes and placed in institutions. The language of improvement masked a deeper desire to separate the “worthy” from the “unworthy,” to draw clear lines between those who belonged and those who did not.

Popular culture played its part as well. Novels, plays, and later movies and television shows turned poor whites into stock characters—comic, pitiable, or menacing, depending on the needs of the story. The “hillbilly,” the “cracker,” the “trailer park queen”—these images became shorthand for a whole set of assumptions about intelligence, morality, and worth.

Most people didn’t realize how much these stereotypes shaped their own views. The shame attached to being poor and white was powerful, leading many to hide their struggles or to look for someone else to blame. The myth of the classless society depended on keeping these stories alive, on pretending that poverty was always the result of personal failure.

At the same time, the boundaries of class were reinforced by law and policy. Zoning laws, school funding, and welfare programs were all designed in ways that kept the poor at a distance. The language of “deserving” and “undeserving” poor became a way to justify unequal treatment, to make inequality seem natural or inevitable.

The result was a society where class was both everywhere and nowhere—visible in every aspect of daily life, but rarely discussed openly. The shame and stigma attached to being “white trash” became a powerful force, shaping not just individual lives, but the very structure of American society.

## Chapter 5 Class and Politics in Modern America

As the twentieth century unfolded, the story of poor whites took on new forms. The Great Depression, the New Deal, and the rise of mass media all changed the way Americans thought about class, but the old divisions remained.

During the Depression, images of rural poverty filled newspapers and magazines. Photographers captured the faces of families living in shacks or tents, their eyes tired but determined. These images stirred sympathy, but also reinforced old stereotypes. The poor were seen as both victims and problems to be solved.

Government programs offered some relief, but they also came with strings attached. Social workers and bureaucrats decided who was “worthy” of help, often using the same old language of respectability and shame. The idea that some people were “waste” persisted, even as the country tried to pull itself out of crisis.

In the postwar years, the rise of suburbia and the growth of the middle class seemed to promise a new era of equality. But the reality was more complicated. Many were left behind, their struggles hidden from view. The language of “white trash” shifted, but it didn’t disappear. It showed up in jokes, in television shows, in political speeches.

A teenager growing up in a small town might dream of escape, but find that the world outside is just as quick to judge. College, jobs, and even military service offer new opportunities, but also new forms of exclusion. The old labels follow, shaping how people are seen and how they see themselves.

Politics became a battleground for questions of class and identity. Politicians courted the “forgotten man,” promising to speak for those left behind. But often, these promises were more about winning votes than changing lives. The language of class was used to divide as much as to unite, to pit one group against another.

The myth of the classless society remained powerful, but cracks began to show. Debates about welfare, crime, and education revealed deep anxieties about who deserved help and who did not. The image of “white trash” was used to justify cuts to social programs, to argue that poverty was a matter of bad choices rather than bad luck.

Most people didn’t see how these debates were shaped by centuries of history. The shame and stigma attached to being poor and white continued to shape lives, even as the country changed around them. The story of “white trash” was not just about individuals, but about the ways in which class, race, and politics have always been intertwined in American life.

## Chapter 6 The Politics of Respectability and the Rise of the “Redneck”

By the late twentieth century, the old boundaries of class had not disappeared—they had simply found new ways to express themselves. The language of “white trash” evolved, sometimes softening, sometimes sharpening, but always finding a way to mark out who belonged and who didn’t. In the South, the figure of the “redneck” began to take center stage, both celebrated and mocked, a symbol of pride for some and a punchline for others.

It’s easy to picture a man in a faded ball cap, standing in the sun at a county fair, laughing with friends. He’s proud of where he comes from, but he knows the world outside his town sees him differently. The word “redneck” is tossed around on television and in music, sometimes as a badge of honor, sometimes as a slur. He shrugs it off, but the sting is still there.

The politics of respectability—who gets to be seen as “decent” or “worthy”—became a powerful force. Politicians and pundits argued over the fate of the “working class,” but rarely listened to the people they claimed to represent. The media painted rural and poor whites as either salt-of-the-earth heroes or as backward, dangerous, and out of touch. Most people didn’t notice how these stories shaped the way they saw their neighbors, or even themselves.

In the 1970s and 1980s, as factories closed and jobs disappeared, the old promise of upward mobility faded for many. Towns that had once thrived on manufacturing or mining were left behind, their main streets empty, their schools struggling. The people who stayed were often blamed for their own misfortune, told that they just needed to work harder or move away. The myth of the classless society was still alive, but it rang hollow in places where opportunity had dried up.

Music, comedy, and television began to reflect these changes. Country songs told stories of pride and pain, of holding on to dignity in the face of loss. Sitcoms and stand-up routines poked fun at “rednecks” and “hillbillies,” sometimes with affection, sometimes with cruelty. The line between laughing with and laughing at was thin, and many felt it keenly.

The politics of the era often used class as a wedge. Debates over welfare, crime, and education were framed in ways that blamed the poor for their own struggles. The image of the “welfare queen” or the “lazy redneck” became shorthand for a whole set of anxieties about change, race, and belonging. Most people didn’t see how these images were used to justify policies that made life harder for those already struggling.

Through it all, the shame and stigma attached to being poor and white persisted. Families tried to hide their struggles, to pass as middle class even when the bills piled up. The dream of respectability was always just out of reach, and the cost of chasing it was high.

## Chapter 7 The Culture Wars and the Politics of Blame

As the twentieth century gave way to the twenty-first, the old divisions of class found new battlegrounds. The so-called “culture wars”—fights over values, identity, and belonging—became a way for politicians and pundits to rally supporters and distract from deeper issues of inequality.

Imagine a mother working two jobs, trying to keep her family afloat in a small town where the factory closed years ago. She hears politicians on television talking about “real Americans,” about who works hard and who doesn’t. Sometimes she recognizes herself in their words, but more often she feels invisible, or worse, blamed for problems she didn’t create.

The language of class was rarely used openly, but it was always there, just beneath the surface. Debates over school funding, healthcare, and taxes were framed in ways that pitted one group against another. The “hardworking taxpayer” was set against the “welfare cheat,” the “middle class” against the “undeserving poor.” Most people didn’t notice how these stories shaped their own beliefs about who deserved help and who did not.

Popular culture continued to play its part. Reality television shows like “Here Comes Honey Boo Boo” and “Duck Dynasty” put the lives of rural and working-class whites on display, sometimes celebrating their resilience, sometimes turning them into spectacles. The line between entertainment and exploitation was often blurred, and the people at the center of these stories rarely got to tell their own side.

The rise of talk radio and cable news brought new voices into the conversation, but often in ways that deepened old divides. Hosts railed against “elites” and “coastal snobs,” painting a picture of a country split between hardworking, “real” Americans and out-of-touch urbanites. The language was familiar, echoing old debates about who belonged and who did not.

At the same time, economic changes continued to reshape the landscape. The decline of manufacturing, the rise of service jobs, and the growth of the gig economy left many feeling adrift. The promise of the American Dream seemed more distant than ever, and the old myth of a classless society was harder to believe.

In this environment, blame became a powerful tool. Politicians and pundits pointed fingers at immigrants, at the poor, at anyone who could be cast as “other.” The shame and stigma attached to being poor and white remained, but now it was joined by a sense of anger and betrayal. Many felt that the system was rigged against them, and they weren’t wrong.

The culture wars offered easy answers, but they did little to address the real problems facing working-class and poor Americans. The old divisions of class persisted, hidden in plain sight, shaping lives and choices in ways most people never saw.

## Chapter 8 The Persistence of Stereotypes in the Modern Era

Even as America changed, the old stereotypes about “white trash” and “rednecks” refused to fade away. They showed up in new forms, adapted to new times, but always serving the same purpose: to mark out who mattered and who didn’t.

A young woman scrolling through social media might see jokes about “trailer trash” or memes mocking rural accents. She laughs along, not wanting to seem thin-skinned, but the message is clear. The world still sees people like her as less than, as objects of ridicule or pity. The shame is quieter now, but it’s still there, shaping how she sees herself and her place in the world.

The persistence of these stereotypes is not an accident. They serve a purpose, helping to justify inequality and exclusion. By turning poverty into a joke or a moral failing, society can avoid asking harder questions about why some people are left behind. Most people don’t notice how these stories shape their own beliefs, or how they make it easier to look away from suffering.

In politics, the language of class is still rarely used openly. Instead, debates are framed in terms of “values,” “culture,” or “personal responsibility.” The old idea that poverty is a sign of weakness or failure remains powerful, even as evidence to the contrary piles up. The myth of the classless society is kept alive by pretending that those who struggle are exceptions, not products of a system that rewards some and punishes others.

Popular culture continues to reflect and reinforce these ideas. Movies, television shows, and news stories often focus on the most extreme or sensational examples, turning real lives into entertainment. The people at the center of these stories rarely get to speak for themselves, and their struggles are often reduced to punchlines or cautionary tales.

At the same time, there are signs of change. Writers, artists, and activists from working-class backgrounds are beginning to tell their own stories, challenging the old stereotypes and demanding to be seen on their own terms. Their voices are still too often drowned out by the noise of the culture wars, but they are there, insisting that their lives and experiences matter.

The persistence of these stereotypes is a reminder that the old divisions of class are still with us, shaping lives and choices in ways most people never see. The shame and stigma attached to being poor and white remain powerful forces, but they are not unbreakable.

## Chapter 9 Class, Race, and the Politics of Division

The story of “white trash” is not just about class—it is also about race, and about the ways in which the two have always been intertwined in American life. From the earliest days of the colonies, the lines between black and white, rich and poor, have been used to divide and control.

Picture a community where neighbors look out for each other, sharing what little they have. But outside forces—politicians, employers, the media—work to sow distrust, to pit one group against another. The message is always the same: there is not enough to go around, so you must fight for your share.

Throughout American history, the powerful have used the language of class and race to keep the lower classes divided. Poor whites have been told that their skin color gives them a special status, even as they are excluded from real power and opportunity. At the same time, they are warned that any gains for black Americans or immigrants come at their expense.

This strategy has been remarkably effective. By keeping the lower classes focused on their differences, those at the top have been able to maintain their own position. Most people don’t see how these divisions are created and maintained, or how they serve the interests of the powerful.

In recent years, these old strategies have found new life. Debates over immigration, policing, and voting rights are often framed in ways that pit one group against another. The language of “real Americans” and “outsiders” is used to draw lines between those who belong and those who do not.

At the same time, there are moments of solidarity, when people come together across lines of race and class to demand change. These moments are often fleeting, but they offer a glimpse of what is possible when the old divisions are challenged.

The story of “white trash” is a reminder that the lines between class and race are not natural or inevitable—they are created and maintained by those who benefit from them. Breaking down these divisions is not easy, but it is possible.

## Chapter 10 Reclaiming Dignity and Challenging the Myth

A father working long hours to provide for his family, a mother fighting for her children’s education, a young person determined to make a better life—these are the real stories that rarely make the headlines. Their struggles are not the result of personal failure, but of a system that has always favored some at the expense of others.

In recent years, there has been a growing movement to challenge the old myths about class in America. Writers, activists, and ordinary people are telling their own stories, demanding to be seen and heard on their own terms. They are pushing back against the language of shame and exclusion, insisting that their lives have value and meaning.

This movement is not just about changing the way we talk about class—it is about changing the way we think about ourselves and each other. It is about recognizing that the myth of the classless society has always been just that—a myth. Real equality requires more than just opportunity; it requires a willingness to confront the ways in which the system is rigged.

Most people don’t realize how much their own beliefs about class have been shaped by stories they have heard all their lives. Challenging these stories is not easy, but it is necessary if we are to build a more just and equal society.

The story of “white trash” is a reminder that dignity is not something that can be given or taken away by others. It is something that must be claimed, again and again, in the face of a world that is all too ready to judge and exclude.
