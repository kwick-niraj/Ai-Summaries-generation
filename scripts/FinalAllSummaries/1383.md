## Introduction

Within the high-stakes world of cancer treatment, moments of hope and heartbreak intertwine as lives hang in the balance. In My Hands: Compelling Stories from a Surgeon and His Patients Fighting Cancer by Steven A. Curley brings forth the deeply human side of medicine, where the scalpel is not just an instrument of precision but a symbol of trust, courage, and resilience. The journey through cancer is never walked alone; it is shaped by the bonds between surgeon and patient, the weight of difficult decisions, and the unwavering pursuit of healing even in the face of uncertainty.

Each encounter in the operating room becomes a testament to the strength found in vulnerability and the profound impact of compassion. The narrative weaves together the technical challenges of surgery with the emotional landscapes navigated by both doctor and patient, revealing the triumphs and setbacks that define the fight against cancer. Through these stories, the complexity of the human spirit emerges—marked by fear, hope, and the relentless will to survive—offering a window into the realities of those who confront illness with bravery and those who stand beside them, determined to make a difference. The stage is set for an exploration of what it truly means to heal, to endure, and to find meaning in the most trying of circumstances.

## Chapter 1 Stepping Into the World of Cancer Surgery

There’s a moment, just before the first incision, when the room seems to hold its breath. The hum of machines, the quiet shuffle of nurses, the gentle squeeze of a loved one’s hand before the patient is wheeled away—all of it blends into a single, heavy pause. For those who work in cancer surgery, this is the threshold between hope and fear, between the known and the unknown. It’s a space where science and humanity meet, and where every decision carries the weight of a life.

The journey into this world rarely begins with a single, dramatic event. More often, it’s a gradual awakening—a realization that medicine is not just about fixing what’s broken, but about standing with people as they face some of the hardest moments of their lives. Early on, the technical side of surgery can feel all-consuming: the precision of the scalpel, the choreography of the team, the relentless pursuit of perfection. But as the days turn into years, something deeper takes root. The faces of patients linger long after the operating room lights have dimmed. Their stories, their fears, their small acts of courage—they become the true measure of the work.

One morning, a young adult sits in a chilly exam room, fidgeting with the edge of a hospital gown. The diagnosis is new, the words still echoing in the air. There’s a look—part disbelief, part determination—that’s familiar to anyone who’s spent time in oncology. The conversation that follows is careful, honest, and full of uncertainty. There are no easy promises, only the assurance that every effort will be made, and that no one will face this alone.

In another corner of the hospital, an older man waits with his partner, their hands intertwined. They’ve been through this before—scans, biopsies, the endless cycle of hope and disappointment. This time, the news is not what they’d hoped for. The options are limited, the risks high. Yet, even in the face of daunting odds, there’s a quiet resolve. They ask questions, weigh choices, and ultimately decide to move forward with surgery. It’s not just about survival; it’s about reclaiming a sense of agency, about fighting for more time together.

These moments are not rare. They unfold every day, in hospitals large and small, in cities and rural towns. Each patient brings a unique story, shaped by family, culture, and personal history. Some arrive with a fierce determination to beat the odds; others are weary, carrying the scars of previous battles. For the surgeon, the challenge is not just technical—it’s deeply personal. Every case is a reminder that behind every scan, every lab result, is a person with dreams, fears, and loved ones.

Over time, it becomes clear that the most important skill in medicine is not found in textbooks or surgical manuals. It’s the ability to listen, to truly see the person behind the diagnosis. It’s the willingness to sit with discomfort, to acknowledge uncertainty, and to offer hope even when the path ahead is unclear. In the world of cancer surgery, compassion is not an optional extra—it’s the foundation of everything.

The operating room itself is a world apart. The rituals are familiar: the careful scrubbing of hands, the donning of gowns and gloves, the quiet focus as the team assembles. There’s a sense of purpose, a shared commitment to doing everything possible for the person on the table. Yet, beneath the surface, there’s always a current of vulnerability. No matter how skilled the team, no matter how advanced the technology, there are limits to what medicine can achieve. Every surgeon carries the memory of cases that didn’t go as planned, of lives lost despite their best efforts.

And yet, hope persists. It’s found in the laughter that sometimes breaks the tension, in the gratitude of a family after a successful operation, in the small victories that add up over time. It’s what keeps people coming back, day after day, to do this difficult, necessary work.

As the years pass, the lines between doctor and patient blur. The stories of those who have faced cancer become woven into the fabric of the surgeon’s own life. Their courage, their resilience, their willingness to trust—these are the true lessons of medicine. In the end, it’s not the number of surgeries performed or the accolades received that matter most. It’s the relationships built, the lives touched, and the knowledge that, for a time, someone’s life was quite literally in your hands.

## Chapter 2 The Human Side of the Operating Room

The operating room is often imagined as a place of cold precision, where science reigns and emotion is left at the door. But for those who spend their days there, it’s a space filled with humanity—raw, unfiltered, and ever-present. Every patient who enters brings a world of hopes, fears, and memories. Every member of the team carries their own burdens, their own stories. The work is technical, yes, but it’s also deeply personal.

Consider the middle-aged parent who arrives for surgery after months of uncertainty. The diagnosis has upended their life, forcing them to confront questions they never wanted to ask. As they’re prepped for the procedure, there’s a moment of quiet—an unspoken understanding that everything could change in the hours ahead. The surgeon meets their gaze, offering reassurance not just with words, but with presence. It’s a promise: whatever happens, no one will be abandoned.

In another case, a teenager faces a rare and aggressive tumor. The odds are daunting, the treatment grueling. Yet, in the days leading up to surgery, there’s a spark—a stubborn refusal to be defined by illness. Friends visit, laughter echoes down the hallway, and for a moment, the hospital feels less like a place of suffering and more like a community rallying around one of its own. The surgical team feels this energy, drawing strength from the patient’s resilience.

Not every story ends in triumph. Sometimes, despite the best efforts, the disease proves relentless. A retired teacher, beloved by generations of students, comes in for what everyone hopes will be a curative operation. The procedure is long and complex, the risks high. In the end, the cancer has spread farther than anyone realized. The news is delivered gently, with honesty and compassion. There are tears, of course, but also gratitude—for the chance to fight, for the care received, for the dignity preserved.

These experiences leave a mark. For the surgeon, each case is a reminder of the limits of medicine, and of the importance of humility. It’s easy to become focused on outcomes, on statistics and survival rates. But the true measure of success is found in the relationships built, in the trust earned, in the moments of connection that transcend the boundaries of doctor and patient.

There are times when the emotional weight of the work becomes almost too much to bear. The loss of a patient is never just a professional setback—it’s a personal grief, a reminder of the fragility of life. Some days, the memories linger long after the shift has ended. The faces of those lost return in quiet moments, their stories replaying in the mind. It’s tempting to build walls, to protect oneself from the pain. But over time, it becomes clear that vulnerability is not a weakness—it’s a source of strength. It’s what allows the surgeon to truly care, to be present, to offer more than just technical skill.

The operating room is also a place of collaboration. Surgeons, nurses, anesthesiologists, and countless others work together, each bringing their own expertise and perspective. There’s a rhythm to the work, a sense of shared purpose. Disagreements arise, of course—about the best approach, about how to handle unexpected complications. But at the heart of it all is a commitment to the patient, to doing what’s right, even when the path is unclear.

Communication is key. The best outcomes are achieved not just through skillful hands, but through open, honest dialogue. Patients are encouraged to ask questions, to voice their fears, to participate in decisions about their care. Families are included, their insights valued. The goal is not just to treat a disease, but to care for a person—to honor their values, their wishes, their dignity.

Over time, the lessons of the operating room spill over into the rest of life. The importance of listening, of being present, of meeting people where they are—these are skills that serve well beyond the hospital walls. The work is demanding, often exhausting, but it’s also deeply rewarding. Every day brings new challenges, new opportunities to make a difference.

## Chapter 3 Facing Ethical Dilemmas and Difficult Choices

Every cancer diagnosis brings with it a series of choices—some straightforward, others fraught with uncertainty. For patients and their families, the decisions can feel overwhelming. For the surgeon, the responsibility is immense. Each recommendation, each action, carries ethical weight. The stakes are high, and the consequences often irreversible.

One afternoon, a young parent sits across from the surgical team, grappling with a decision that no one should have to make. The tumor is operable, but the risks are significant. Surgery could mean more time with family, but it could also lead to complications, to a diminished quality of life. The conversation is honest, sometimes painfully so. There are no guarantees, only probabilities. The parent listens, asks questions, and ultimately chooses to proceed. The decision is not made lightly—it’s a leap of faith, a testament to the power of hope.

In another case, an elderly patient faces a different set of challenges. The cancer is advanced, the options limited. Aggressive treatment might buy a few more months, but at what cost? The patient is tired, weary from years of illness. Together with the medical team, they weigh the benefits and burdens of further intervention. In the end, the choice is made to focus on comfort, on spending time with loved ones, on living fully in the days that remain. It’s a decision rooted in dignity, in the recognition that sometimes, less is more.

These moments are never easy. The surgeon is often called upon to balance competing values: the desire to prolong life, the need to minimize suffering, the importance of respecting autonomy. There are no simple answers, no one-size-fits-all solutions. Each case is unique, shaped by the patient’s values, their goals, their understanding of what matters most.

Sometimes, the ethical dilemmas are less obvious. A patient may request a procedure that offers little chance of success, driven by fear or desperation. The surgeon must navigate these conversations with care, offering guidance without imposing their own values. It’s a delicate dance, one that requires empathy, patience, and a willingness to sit with uncertainty.

There are also moments when the right course of action is clear, but the emotional toll is heavy. A child with a rare cancer requires a risky operation. The team knows the odds are slim, but the family is determined to try. The surgery is performed, the outcome uncertain. In the days that follow, the team rallies around the family, offering support, comfort, and, when needed, honest answers. The experience leaves a mark—a reminder of the limits of medicine, and of the courage required to face the unknown.

Over time, it becomes clear that the most important decisions are not made in isolation. They are the product of collaboration—between doctors, patients, families, and sometimes, ethics committees. The best outcomes are achieved when everyone’s voice is heard, when decisions are made with compassion and respect.

The work is not without its challenges. There are days when the weight of responsibility feels almost unbearable. The fear of making the wrong choice, of causing harm, is ever-present. But with experience comes a kind of wisdom—a recognition that perfection is impossible, that the best anyone can do is to act with integrity, to listen deeply, and to honor the trust placed in their hands.

## Chapter 4 The Power of Compassion and Connection

In the world of cancer care, technical skill is essential. But what truly sets the best caregivers apart is something less tangible—a deep, abiding compassion. It’s the ability to see the person behind the diagnosis, to recognize the fears and hopes that shape every decision. It’s the willingness to be present, to offer comfort, to share in both the joys and the sorrows.

One evening, a patient recovering from surgery struggles with pain and fear. The room is quiet, the shadows long. A nurse sits at the bedside, offering not just medication, but a listening ear. The conversation drifts from medical concerns to memories of childhood, to dreams for the future. In that moment, the hospital becomes a place of healing, not just for the body, but for the spirit.

In another case, a family gathers around a loved one facing a difficult prognosis. The surgeon joins them, not as an authority figure, but as a fellow traveler on a hard road. There are tears, of course, but also laughter—stories shared, memories recalled. The boundaries between doctor and patient blur, replaced by a sense of shared humanity. The experience is a reminder that healing is not always about curing. Sometimes, it’s about bearing witness, about offering presence in the face of suffering.

Compassion is not always easy. The demands of the job are relentless, the emotional toll significant. There are days when exhaustion threatens to overwhelm, when the temptation to retreat behind a wall of detachment is strong. But over time, it becomes clear that the greatest rewards come from connection—from allowing oneself to be moved, to be changed, by the people one serves.

Communication is at the heart of compassionate care. The best outcomes are achieved when patients feel heard, when their concerns are taken seriously, when their values are respected. This requires more than just medical knowledge—it requires humility, curiosity, and a willingness to learn from every encounter.

There are moments when a simple gesture—a hand on the shoulder, a few words of encouragement—makes all the difference. A patient facing a long recovery finds strength in the kindness of a physical therapist. A family struggling with grief is comforted by the gentle presence of a chaplain. These acts of compassion ripple outward, touching lives in ways that are often unseen, but deeply felt.

Over time, the stories of patients become woven into the fabric of the caregiver’s own life. Their courage, their resilience, their capacity for hope in the face of adversity—these are the true lessons of medicine. The work is demanding, often heartbreaking, but it is also a source of profound meaning.

## Chapter 5 Hope, Loss, and Finding Meaning in Illness

Cancer is a thief. It steals time, health, and sometimes, the future people imagined for themselves and their loved ones. But even in the midst of loss, there are moments of hope—small, fragile, but fiercely persistent. These moments are what give meaning to the work, what sustain patients and caregivers alike through the darkest days.

A young adult, newly diagnosed, faces a long road of treatment. The prognosis is uncertain, the path ahead daunting. Yet, in the midst of fear, there is also determination—a refusal to give in, a commitment to fight for every day. Friends and family rally, offering support, encouragement, and, when needed, a shoulder to cry on. The journey is hard, but not without its moments of grace.

In another case, an older patient receives news that the cancer has returned. The options are limited, the outlook grim. Yet, rather than retreat into despair, the patient chooses to focus on what remains—time with loved ones, the beauty of a sunset, the comfort of familiar routines. The decision is not about giving up, but about living fully, about finding meaning in the midst of uncertainty.

Loss is an inevitable part of cancer care. Not every story ends in survival. There are days when the weight of grief feels almost unbearable—when the memory of a patient lost lingers long after the last goodbyes have been said. For caregivers, these moments are a reminder of the limits of medicine, of the fragility of life. But they are also a source of motivation—a call to do better, to keep searching for answers, to honor the lives of those lost by caring even more deeply for those who remain.

Hope is not a naive belief that everything will turn out well. It’s a choice—a decision to keep moving forward, to find meaning even in the face of suffering. It’s what allows patients to endure grueling treatments, to face uncertain futures with courage. It’s what sustains caregivers through long nights and difficult days.

Over time, the search for meaning becomes central to the experience of illness. Patients and families grapple with questions of purpose, of legacy, of what it means to live a good life. For some, faith provides comfort; for others, it’s the love of family, the support of friends, the satisfaction of knowing they fought with everything they had.

For caregivers, the work is both a privilege and a burden. The opportunity to walk alongside people in their most vulnerable moments is a gift, but it comes at a cost. The stories of patients—of hope, of loss, of resilience—become part of the caregiver’s own story. They are a source of inspiration, a reminder of what truly matters.

## Chapter 6 The Long Road of Recovery

Recovery after cancer surgery is rarely a straight line. It’s more like a winding path, full of unexpected turns, setbacks, and small, hard-won victories. For many, the hardest part comes after the operation, when the adrenaline fades and the reality of healing sets in. The hospital room, once a place of urgent action, becomes a quiet space where time seems to slow down.

A middle-aged woman wakes to the beeping of monitors and the gentle murmur of nurses. The pain is sharp, but manageable. What’s harder is the uncertainty—will the cancer return? Will life ever feel normal again? Each day brings new challenges: learning to walk again, managing medications, facing the mirror and the scars that tell a story she never wanted to live. Family visits bring comfort, but also reminders of all that’s changed.

In another room, a young man struggles with frustration. The surgery was a success, but the recovery is slow. He’s used to being active, independent, in control. Now, even simple tasks require help. There are moments of anger, of sadness, of feeling like a burden. But there are also moments of progress—a few extra steps, a meal eaten without nausea, a laugh shared with a friend. These small milestones become lifelines, proof that healing is possible, even if it’s not always visible.

The medical team plays a crucial role in this phase. Physical therapists coax stiff muscles into motion, nurses offer encouragement and gentle reminders, doctors check incisions and adjust medications. But the most important support often comes from those who have walked this road before—other patients, survivors, people who understand the unique challenges of recovery. Their stories, shared in waiting rooms and online forums, offer hope and practical advice.

It becomes clear that recovery is not just about the body. It’s about rebuilding a sense of self, of finding meaning in a changed life. Some discover new passions, new strengths, new ways of connecting with others. Others struggle with fear, with grief for the life they’ve lost. There’s no right way to heal, no single path forward. Each person must find their own way, at their own pace.

For caregivers, this phase is both rewarding and challenging. Watching a patient regain strength, reclaim independence, is a source of pride. But there are also setbacks—complications, infections, the slow return of old symptoms. The work requires patience, persistence, and a willingness to celebrate even the smallest victories.

Over time, the scars fade, but the experience leaves a mark. Many survivors speak of a new appreciation for life, a deeper sense of gratitude, a commitment to living fully. Others carry a quiet sadness, a sense of vulnerability that never quite goes away. Both are valid, both are part of the journey.

## Chapter 7 The Ripple Effect—How Illness Changes Families

Cancer doesn’t just affect the person with the diagnosis. Its impact ripples outward, touching partners, children, parents, friends, and even colleagues. The diagnosis is like a stone dropped into a pond—suddenly, everything shifts, and the waves keep spreading.

A father, once the steady anchor of his family, finds himself relying on his teenage daughter for help with daily tasks. She juggles school, chores, and hospital visits, growing up faster than she ever expected. There are moments of resentment, of exhaustion, but also of unexpected closeness. They share late-night talks, inside jokes, a new understanding of what it means to care for someone.

Elsewhere, a couple faces the strain of uncertainty. The routines that once defined their days—work, meals, plans for the future—are upended by appointments, treatments, and the ever-present question: what comes next? There are arguments, tears, and moments of silence. But there are also gestures of love—a favorite meal cooked, a hand held during a difficult conversation, a whispered promise to keep going, together.

Children, too, are changed by a parent’s illness. Some become fiercely protective, others withdraw, unsure how to process their fear. A mother, worried about her young son, tries to shield him from the worst of her pain. But he senses the change, asks questions she struggles to answer. Over time, they find a new way to communicate—through drawings, through stories, through quiet moments of connection.

Friends and extended family often want to help, but don’t always know how. Some step up, offering rides, meals, or simply a listening ear. Others pull away, uncomfortable with the reality of illness. The patient learns who they can count on, who will show up when things are hard.

The financial impact can be profound. Medical bills pile up, jobs are lost or put on hold, savings dwindle. The stress of money adds another layer of worry, another challenge to navigate. Some families find support through community organizations, through the kindness of strangers, through creative problem-solving.

Through it all, the experience of illness can bring people closer—or drive them apart. It reveals strengths and vulnerabilities, tests relationships, and forces everyone to confront what truly matters. Many families emerge changed, with a deeper sense of connection, a new appreciation for each other. Others struggle to find their footing, to rebuild after the storm.

For the medical team, supporting families is as important as treating the patient. Social workers, counselors, and chaplains offer guidance, resources, and a safe space to talk. The best care recognizes that healing is a family affair, that everyone touched by illness needs support.

## Chapter 8 When the Cure Isn’t Possible—Palliative Care and Saying Goodbye

There comes a time, for some, when the focus shifts from cure to comfort. The word “palliative” can sound final, even frightening, but for many, it’s a path to dignity, to peace, to a different kind of hope. It’s about making the most of the time that remains, about living as fully as possible, even in the face of loss.

A grandmother, surrounded by her children and grandchildren, chooses to stop aggressive treatment. The decision is not about giving up, but about choosing how she wants to spend her days. The medical team shifts gears, focusing on pain control, on easing symptoms, on supporting the family through the process. There are hard conversations, moments of grief, but also laughter, stories, and a sense of closure.

In another home, a young man with advanced cancer works with a palliative care nurse to manage his symptoms. They talk about music, about dreams, about the things that still bring joy. The nurse helps him plan a small celebration with friends, a chance to say goodbye on his own terms. The focus is on quality of life, on honoring his wishes, on making space for both sorrow and celebration.

For families, this phase is bittersweet. There is relief in seeing a loved one’s pain eased, but also the ache of impending loss. The days are filled with practical tasks—arranging hospice care, managing medications, navigating the maze of paperwork. But there are also moments of connection, of forgiveness, of saying the things that matter most.

The medical team becomes a source of comfort, of guidance, of steady presence. They help families navigate the unknown, answer questions, and offer reassurance. They bear witness to the final chapter of a life, honoring the person’s story, their values, their legacy.

Saying goodbye is never easy. Grief comes in waves—sometimes sharp and overwhelming, sometimes quiet and lingering. For some, faith offers comfort; for others, it’s the support of friends, the rituals of remembrance, the simple act of sharing memories.

Over time, the pain of loss softens, replaced by gratitude for the time shared, for the love given and received. The experience of walking someone to the end of their life is both heartbreaking and sacred—a reminder of the fragility of life, and of the power of presence.

For those who work in palliative care, the lessons are profound. They learn to listen, to honor each person’s wishes, to find meaning in the midst of sorrow. They see, again and again, that even when cure is not possible, healing can still happen—in relationships, in forgiveness, in the peace that comes from being truly seen and cared for.

## Chapter 9 Lessons from the Front Lines—What Patients Teach Us

Every patient who walks through the doors of a cancer center brings a lesson. Some teach resilience, others vulnerability, others the quiet strength of acceptance. Over the years, these lessons accumulate, shaping not just the practice of medicine, but the lives of those who provide care.

A retired engineer, meticulous and methodical, approaches his diagnosis like a problem to be solved. He asks questions, researches options, takes notes at every appointment. But as treatment progresses, he learns to let go of control, to trust the process, to accept help. His journey is a reminder that strength can be found in surrender, that it’s okay to lean on others.

A young artist, facing a rare and aggressive cancer, refuses to let illness define her. She brings her sketchbook to every appointment, filling the margins of her medical records with drawings. Her art becomes a way to process fear, to find beauty in the midst of pain, to leave a mark on the world. Her story is a testament to the power of creativity, to the ways we find meaning even in the hardest times.

A single parent, juggling work, treatment, and childcare, teaches the team about perseverance. There are days when exhaustion threatens to overwhelm, when the future feels impossibly uncertain. But she keeps going, fueled by love for her children, by the hope of seeing them grow up. Her determination inspires everyone around her, a reminder that courage is not the absence of fear, but the decision to keep moving forward.

Not every lesson is about triumph. Some patients teach the importance of honesty—of naming fears, of acknowledging limits, of having hard conversations. Others teach the value of humor, of finding moments of lightness even in the darkest days. Still others teach the art of letting go, of saying goodbye with grace and gratitude.

For the medical team, these lessons are a source of growth, of humility, of perspective. They remind everyone that medicine is not just about curing disease, but about caring for people. They challenge assumptions, broaden understanding, and deepen empathy.

Over time, the stories of patients become a kind of guide—a map for navigating the complexities of illness, of healing, of being human. They offer wisdom that can’t be found in textbooks, lessons that linger long after the last appointment.

## Chapter 10 The Evolving Landscape of Cancer Care

Cancer care is always changing. New treatments, new technologies, new ways of thinking about illness and healing. For those on the front lines, the pace of change can be dizzying—but it’s also a source of hope, of possibility, of progress.

A patient with a diagnosis that once meant certain death now has options—targeted therapies, immunotherapy, clinical trials. The conversations in the exam room are different than they were a decade ago. There’s more hope, more choices, more reason to believe that the future will be better.

But progress brings new challenges. The cost of care is rising, access is uneven, and not every breakthrough reaches every patient. There are questions about equity, about who benefits from new advances, about how to ensure that no one is left behind.

The role of the patient is changing, too. People are more informed, more empowered, more involved in decisions about their care. Shared decision-making is the norm, not the exception. Patients and families are partners, not just recipients of care.

The medical team must adapt—learning new skills, staying up to date with research, embracing new ways of working. There’s a growing recognition that cancer care is not just about treating disease, but about supporting the whole person—body, mind, and spirit.

Technology is transforming every aspect of care. Telemedicine brings expertise to remote communities, electronic records improve communication, genetic testing opens new doors for prevention and treatment. But technology is only as good as the people who use it. The human connection remains at the heart of healing.

There are still gaps, still challenges, still work to be done. Disparities in care persist, especially for those in rural areas, for people of color, for those without insurance. The fight for equity is ongoing, a reminder that progress must be measured not just in new drugs, but in lives improved.

For those who work in cancer care, the future is both exciting and daunting. There is hope in every new discovery, but also a responsibility to ensure that progress benefits everyone. The work is never finished, the journey never complete.

### Conclusion: What It Means to Hold a Life in Your Hands

If you’ve walked with these stories—if you’ve felt the weight of uncertainty, the ache of loss, the quiet triumph of hope—then you already know: cancer care is not just a profession, but a calling. It’s a journey marked by courage, by compassion, by the willingness to stand with others in their most vulnerable moments.

The lessons from the front lines are simple, but profound:

- Healing is about presence, not just procedures or prescriptions.
- Every patient’s story is unique, deserving of respect and deep listening.
- Families need support as much as patients—illness is never faced alone.
- Recovery is a journey, not a destination, and every step matters.
- Even when cure isn’t possible, comfort, dignity, and meaning remain.

If you find yourself in the role of patient, caregiver, or healer, remember: you are not alone. The path may be hard, but it is also filled with moments of connection, of grace, of unexpected joy. The scars—physical and emotional—are not just reminders of pain, but of survival, of love, of the strength it takes to keep going.

Let yourself be changed by the stories you encounter. Let them teach you to listen more deeply, to care more fiercely, to honor the humanity in every person you meet. The work is never easy, and the losses are real. But the moments of hope, of laughter, of shared understanding—these are what make the journey worthwhile.
