## Introduction

Visions of a better world have long inspired both dreamers and pragmatists, fueling debates about what is truly possible for society. Utopia for Realists: How We Can Build the Ideal World by Rutger Bregman delves into the radical notion that many ideas once dismissed as utopian—universal basic income, a fifteen-hour workweek, and open borders—are not only achievable but may be essential for progress. The narrative challenges the boundaries of imagination and practicality, inviting a reexamination of what is considered realistic in the pursuit of collective well-being.

Exploring the intersection of history, economics, and social innovation, the journey unfolds through bold proposals and real-world experiments that have already begun to reshape communities. The persistent question emerges: why settle for the status quo when evidence and creativity can point toward a more just and prosperous future? As the contours of possibility expand, the conversation shifts from mere wishful thinking to actionable steps, setting the stage for a deeper understanding of how societies can transform ideals into reality.

## Chapter 1 Imagining a Better World Starts with Daring to Dream

It’s easy to forget how much the world has changed in just a few generations. Most people, caught up in the daily grind, rarely pause to consider how different life could be. The routines of work, bills, and news cycles create a sense that things are set in stone. But every so often, someone looks up and wonders: Why do we accept the world as it is? Why not imagine something better?

In the early twentieth century, a group of thinkers and activists gathered in a small, smoke-filled room. They were tired of the endless struggle for survival that defined their era. For them, the idea of a world without poverty, where everyone had enough to eat and a roof over their heads, seemed like a distant fantasy. Yet, they dared to dream. Their vision was dismissed as utopian, even dangerous. But over time, their ideas—like the eight-hour workday and universal suffrage—became reality.

Most people don’t realize that what seems impossible today can become tomorrow’s common sense. The world is full of examples: the abolition of slavery, the right to vote, weekends, and public education. Each of these began as a radical idea, championed by a few who refused to accept the status quo. They faced ridicule and resistance, but their persistence changed the course of history.

A young person, frustrated by the monotony of their job, might wonder why they spend most of their waking hours working. An older neighbor, having lived through decades of change, recalls a time when things were different—when people believed in progress and the possibility of a better future. These moments of reflection are the seeds of utopia. They remind us that the world is not fixed; it is shaped by the dreams and actions of ordinary people.

The problem is that, somewhere along the way, society lost its appetite for big ideas. The word “utopia” became a punchline, a way to dismiss ambitious visions as naïve or unrealistic. Instead, the focus shifted to managing problems rather than solving them. The result is a world where many feel stuck, unable to imagine alternatives to the systems that govern their lives.

But history shows that progress depends on imagination. Without it, there would be no weekends, no public libraries, no social safety nets. The first step toward building a better world is to reclaim the courage to dream. It’s not about pie-in-the-sky fantasies, but about asking bold questions: What if poverty could be eliminated? What if work didn’t dominate our lives? What if borders were open, and people could move freely?

These questions are not just thought experiments. They are the starting point for real change. When people dare to imagine a different world, they begin to see possibilities where others see only obstacles. The journey toward utopia begins with a simple act: refusing to accept that things must always be as they are.

## Chapter 2 The Case for Universal Basic Income—Ending Poverty with Simplicity

Picture a single parent, juggling two jobs and still struggling to make ends meet. Each month is a race against the clock—rent, groceries, school supplies, and the constant worry that one unexpected expense could tip everything into chaos. For millions, this is not a temporary setback but a permanent state of anxiety. The question that lingers is: Why, in a world of abundance, does poverty persist?

Most social programs are designed to help, but they often come with strings attached—forms to fill out, hoops to jump through, and a sense of being judged. The system is built on the assumption that people in need must prove their worthiness. This approach creates barriers and leaves many behind.

Now, imagine a different approach. Instead of complicated welfare programs, every person receives a regular, unconditional payment—enough to cover basic needs. No questions asked, no paperwork, no stigma. This is the idea of a universal basic income (UBI). It sounds radical, but the concept is surprisingly old and has been tested in various forms.

In a small Canadian town decades ago, a bold experiment unfolded. Every resident received a guaranteed income, regardless of their circumstances. At first, skeptics predicted disaster—people would stop working, laziness would spread, and the economy would collapse. But the reality was different. People continued to work, but with less stress. Hospital visits dropped, school performance improved, and the community became healthier and more stable. The experiment quietly ended, but its lessons lingered.

Most people don’t notice that poverty is not caused by a lack of character, but by a lack of cash. When people have enough to meet their basic needs, they make better decisions, invest in their futures, and contribute more to society. The evidence from UBI experiments around the world is clear: giving people money works.

A young adult, recently laid off, receives a basic income payment. Instead of spiraling into debt or despair, they use the money to retrain for a new job. An elderly neighbor, no longer able to work, finds dignity and security in knowing their needs are met. These stories are not exceptions—they are the rule when people are given the freedom to choose their own paths.

Critics argue that UBI is too expensive or that it would encourage idleness. But most don’t realize that the cost of poverty—healthcare, crime, lost productivity—is far greater than the price of simply giving people what they need. In fact, many existing welfare programs are more expensive to administer than a straightforward basic income.

The real obstacle is not money, but imagination. For decades, society has accepted poverty as inevitable, a problem to be managed rather than solved. But the evidence shows that ending poverty is possible—and surprisingly simple. It requires a shift in thinking, from suspicion and control to trust and empowerment.

Universal basic income is not a silver bullet, but it is a powerful tool. It offers a way to ensure that no one falls through the cracks, that everyone has a foundation on which to build a better life. The lesson is clear: when people are given the means to thrive, they do.

## Chapter 3 Rethinking Work—The Promise of a Shorter Workweek

Most people spend the majority of their lives at work. The alarm rings early, the commute drags on, and the hours blur together in a cycle of meetings, emails, and deadlines. For many, work is not just a source of income, but a defining feature of identity. Yet, beneath the surface, there is a growing sense of exhaustion—a feeling that life is passing by while work takes center stage.

A young professional, once excited by their career, now finds themselves counting down the days to the weekend. A parent, torn between job demands and family time, wonders if there is a better way. These quiet moments of frustration are more common than most admit.

The idea of a shorter workweek is not new. In the past, as technology advanced and productivity increased, people expected that work hours would decrease. Early economists predicted that by the twenty-first century, people would work just a few hours a day, leaving more time for leisure, creativity, and community. But something changed. Instead of working less, people began working more—often to the point of burnout.

Most don’t notice that much of modern work is not about producing goods or services, but about keeping busy. Entire industries revolve around paperwork, meetings, and tasks that add little value. The rise of “bullshit jobs”—roles that exist mainly to fill time—has left many feeling unfulfilled and disconnected from the results of their labor.

In one memorable story, a group of office workers realized that their entire department could disappear without anyone noticing. Their days were filled with reports that no one read and meetings that led nowhere. The realization was both liberating and unsettling. It became clear that much of what passes for work is simply a way to justify a paycheck.

The consequences are profound. Long hours lead to stress, health problems, and strained relationships. Productivity suffers, and creativity withers. Yet, the idea of working less is often dismissed as lazy or unrealistic.

But history tells a different story. When the workweek was reduced from sixty to forty hours, society flourished. People had more time for family, education, and civic engagement. Crime rates fell, and communities grew stronger. The lesson is simple: when people have more free time, everyone benefits.

A parent, able to leave work early, spends the afternoon with their children. An artist, freed from the pressure of a full-time job, creates something beautiful. These moments are not luxuries—they are the building blocks of a healthy society.

The promise of a shorter workweek is not just about leisure. It’s about reimagining what matters. Most people want to contribute, to feel useful, and to have time for the things that make life meaningful. By working less, people can work better—more focused, more creative, and more fulfilled.

The challenge is not technical, but cultural. It requires letting go of the belief that busyness equals value. It means trusting that people will use their time wisely when given the chance. The evidence is clear: when work hours are reduced, productivity often increases, and well-being soars.

The future of work does not have to be a treadmill. It can be a path to freedom, creativity, and connection. The first step is daring to imagine a world where work serves life, not the other way around.

## Chapter 4 The Power of Open Borders—Unlocking Global Potential

Imagine a world where people can move as freely as ideas. For most, the thought of open borders conjures images of chaos and uncertainty. But beneath the fear lies a simple truth: the place where someone is born determines their opportunities more than any other factor. A child born in a wealthy country has access to education, healthcare, and security, while another, born just across an invisible line, faces a lifetime of struggle.

A young man, desperate to provide for his family, risks everything to cross a border in search of work. He leaves behind his home, his friends, and the familiar rhythms of daily life. The journey is dangerous, but the hope of a better future outweighs the risks. For him, the border is not just a line on a map—it is the difference between poverty and possibility.

Most people don’t realize that borders are a relatively recent invention. For much of history, people moved freely in search of opportunity. It was only in the last century that nations began to restrict movement, building walls and fences to keep people out. The result is a world where talent and ambition are trapped by geography.

The economic impact is staggering. Studies show that if people could move freely, global wealth would increase dramatically. The gains from open borders dwarf those of any other policy—far greater than foreign aid or trade agreements. When people are allowed to go where their skills are needed, everyone benefits.

A nurse from a poor country moves to a city where her talents are in demand. She earns more, sends money home, and helps fill a critical shortage. Her new community gains a dedicated caregiver, and her family escapes poverty. These stories play out every day, but most go unnoticed.

Critics worry that open borders would lead to job losses, lower wages, or cultural conflict. But the evidence tells a different story. In places where migration is allowed, economies grow, innovation flourishes, and communities become more vibrant. Most fears are rooted in myths, not facts.

The real challenge is psychological. People are wired to fear outsiders, to see difference as a threat. But history shows that diversity is a source of strength. Societies that welcome newcomers are more dynamic, creative, and resilient.

A small town, once in decline, is revitalized by new arrivals. Shops reopen, schools fill with children, and the community comes alive. The newcomers bring fresh ideas, energy, and hope. The lesson is clear: when people are free to move, everyone gains.

Open borders are not about erasing differences, but about unlocking potential. They offer a way to reduce global inequality, foster innovation, and build a more just world. The first step is to see migration not as a problem to be managed, but as an opportunity to be embraced.

Most people never question the lines that divide the world. But those who do discover that the greatest barriers are not physical, but mental. The future belongs to those who dare to imagine a world without walls.

A group of activists, dismissed as dreamers, gather in a cramped apartment. They share stories of injustice and imagine a world where everyone has a voice. Their ideas are mocked in the newspapers, and politicians call them dangerous. But they persist, organizing marches, writing pamphlets, and building alliances. Over time, their vision spreads, and what once seemed impossible becomes law.

Most people don’t notice that progress is rarely linear. It comes in fits and starts, driven by those willing to challenge the status quo. The abolition of slavery, the fight for women’s rights, and the creation of the welfare state all began with a handful of people refusing to accept the world as it was.

Change is often met with resistance. Those in power argue that radical ideas are impractical, too expensive, or a threat to order. But history shows that the real danger lies in complacency. When society stops dreaming, it stops moving forward.

A teacher, inspired by the stories of past reformers, encourages students to question everything. A factory worker, tired of long hours and low pay, joins a union and demands better conditions. These small acts of courage add up, creating a wave of change that transforms society.

The key is not to wait for perfect solutions, but to start with bold ideas and adapt as reality unfolds. Most successful reforms began as experiments—pilot programs, local initiatives, or small-scale trials. When they worked, they spread. When they failed, they were revised or replaced.

The lesson is that utopia is not a fixed destination, but a direction. It is the willingness to imagine a better world and to take practical steps toward it. Most people underestimate the power of big ideas, but history shows that they are the engine of progress.

A community, once divided by fear, comes together to build something new. They face setbacks and disappointments, but they keep moving forward. Over time, their efforts change not just their own lives, but the lives of generations to come.

The story of progress is the story of ordinary people daring to dream. It is a reminder that the world is not finished, that the best days may still lie ahead. The first step is to believe that change is possible—and to act on that belief.

## Chapter 6 The Data-Driven Revolution—Why Evidence Matters More Than Ideology

Sometimes, the world feels like it’s run on gut feelings and old habits. People argue about what’s best, clinging to beliefs passed down or picked up from headlines. But every so often, someone steps back and asks: What actually works? What if, instead of following tradition or ideology, we let evidence guide our choices?

In a bustling city office, a group of policy researchers gather around a table, sifting through stacks of reports. They’re not interested in grand theories or political slogans. Instead, they want to know what the numbers say. Does a new housing program actually reduce homelessness? Does a job training initiative help people find work? Their approach is simple: test, measure, learn, and adapt.

Most people don’t realize how rare this mindset is. For decades, governments and organizations have launched programs based on hunches or political pressure, rarely checking if they deliver results. Billions are spent, but the impact is often unclear. The result is frustration—both for those in need and those trying to help.

A social worker, tired of seeing the same families cycle through the system, wonders if there’s a better way. She starts tracking outcomes, noticing which interventions make a difference and which don’t. Over time, her small experiment grows. Others take notice. The data reveals patterns that no one expected—some programs work wonders, others barely move the needle.

It became clear that progress isn’t about having the right ideology, but about being willing to learn. The most successful reforms are those that start small, gather evidence, and scale up what works. When mistakes happen, they’re seen as opportunities to improve, not reasons to give up.

In one city, a pilot program gives homeless people apartments with no strings attached. Critics predict disaster, but the data tells a different story. Hospital visits drop, police calls decrease, and people begin to rebuild their lives. The evidence is so compelling that the program expands, changing the way the city tackles homelessness.

Most people don’t notice that the best ideas often come from unexpected places. A teacher’s experiment in one classroom becomes a national model. A local health clinic’s new approach spreads across the country. The lesson is that innovation thrives when people are free to test, fail, and try again.

The challenge is overcoming the fear of change. Many worry that new ideas will backfire, or that admitting failure means losing face. But the real risk is sticking with what doesn’t work. When evidence leads the way, progress becomes possible—not just in theory, but in practice.

A policymaker, once skeptical of “utopian” ideas, is won over by the results. She sees that bold experiments, grounded in data, can transform lives. The story of evidence-based policy is not about cold numbers, but about real people—families finding stability, communities growing stronger, and hope returning where it was once lost.

The future belongs to those who are willing to learn, adapt, and let the facts guide them. It’s not about being right from the start, but about having the courage to change course when the evidence demands it.

## Chapter 7 The Hidden Value of “Useless” Work—Rethinking What Matters

In the rush to measure productivity and profit, it’s easy to overlook the quiet work that holds society together. The world celebrates inventors, CEOs, and entrepreneurs, but rarely pauses to honor the caregivers, volunteers, and helpers whose efforts rarely show up in economic statistics.

A retired neighbor spends her mornings reading to children at the local library. She’s not paid, and her work doesn’t boost the GDP, but the impact is real. The kids grow more confident, their parents find a moment of peace, and the community feels a little more connected. Most people don’t notice these small acts, but they are the glue that binds society.

For years, economists dismissed unpaid work as “unproductive.” The focus was on jobs that generated income, not on the countless hours spent raising children, caring for the sick, or supporting neighbors. But as the world grows more complex, it’s becoming clear that these so-called “useless” jobs are anything but.

A young man, laid off from his corporate job, finds purpose volunteering at a food bank. The work is hard, the hours long, but he feels more fulfilled than he ever did in the office. He sees firsthand how a simple act—handing out groceries, listening to someone’s story—can change a life.

It became clear that value isn’t always measured in dollars. Most people want to feel useful, to make a difference, to be part of something bigger than themselves. When society ignores or undervalues this kind of work, everyone loses.

In one city, a new program pays people to care for elderly neighbors. The results are striking—loneliness drops, health improves, and the community grows closer. The cost is small, but the benefits ripple outward, touching lives in ways that can’t be captured by spreadsheets.

Most don’t realize that the future of work may look very different from the past. As automation and technology replace routine jobs, the most important roles may be those that require empathy, creativity, and human connection. The challenge is to recognize and reward these contributions, even if they don’t fit neatly into economic models.

A parent, once worried about returning to work, finds meaning in raising their child. A friend, struggling with depression, discovers hope through volunteering. These stories remind us that the most valuable work is often invisible.

The lesson is simple: a good society is one that values all forms of contribution, not just those that generate profit. When people are free to do work that matters to them, everyone benefits.

The future is not about maximizing output, but about maximizing meaning. It’s about building a world where everyone’s efforts are seen, valued, and supported.

It became clear that the most successful places are those that welcome newcomers, embrace diversity, and invest in public spaces. Most don’t realize that isolation is the enemy of progress. When communities are cut off—by geography, prejudice, or poverty—they stagnate. But when barriers fall, growth follows.

A city, once divided by race and class, invests in affordable housing and public art. The changes are slow, but over time, trust grows and divisions fade. The city becomes a place where anyone can belong.

The lesson is that progress is not just about technology or wealth, but about connection. The future belongs to those who build bridges, not walls.

A world of thriving cities and connected communities is within reach. It starts with small acts—planting a garden, opening a door, sharing a meal. When people come together, anything is possible.
