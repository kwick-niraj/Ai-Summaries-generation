## Introduction

Curiosity, courage, and the relentless pursuit of truth shape the world of journalism, where every word carries the weight of a story waiting to be told. In Action Words: Journey of a Journalist by Shaune Bordere, the landscape of reporting unfolds through the eyes of someone who has navigated the frontlines of news, culture, and society. The narrative pulses with the energy of deadlines, the tension of ethical dilemmas, and the thrill of uncovering hidden realities. Each assignment becomes a window into the complexities of human experience, revealing how the act of bearing witness can transform both the observer and the observed.

Through the lens of a journalist’s evolving career, the journey traverses moments of triumph and vulnerability, highlighting the personal growth that comes from engaging with the world’s most pressing issues. Encounters with diverse communities, the challenge of maintaining integrity under pressure, and the constant adaptation to a rapidly changing media landscape all converge to paint a vivid portrait of what it means to seek and share the truth. The story invites reflection on the power of words and the responsibility that comes with telling stories that matter, setting the stage for a deeper exploration of the lessons and insights gained along the way.

## Chapter 1 Early Curiosity and the First Steps into Newsrooms

There’s a certain restlessness that comes with wanting to know how the world works. For some, it starts in childhood, with a fascination for the stories behind the headlines, the voices on the radio, or the faces on the evening news. In one small Australian town, a teenager found herself drawn to the hum of the local newspaper office, where the clatter of typewriters and the smell of ink hinted at a world beyond the familiar. The urge to ask questions, to understand what made people tick, and to see her own words in print became a quiet but persistent calling.

The path into journalism rarely follows a straight line. For this young woman, the journey began with a mixture of boldness and uncertainty. She didn’t come from a family of writers or media professionals. Instead, she watched her parents work hard in ordinary jobs, instilling in her a sense of responsibility and a belief that effort mattered. School was a place of both comfort and challenge. Teachers noticed her knack for storytelling and encouraged her to write for the school newsletter, where she learned the thrill of seeing her name in print and the sting of criticism when her work didn’t land as intended.

After finishing school, the options felt limited. University seemed out of reach, and the idea of moving to a big city was daunting. Still, the pull of journalism was strong. She started by volunteering at the local paper, running errands, and helping with small tasks. The newsroom was a world of its own—chaotic, noisy, and full of characters. There was the gruff editor who barked orders but secretly took pride in mentoring newcomers, and the seasoned reporter who always seemed to know the right questions to ask. Watching them work, she realized that journalism was less about glamour and more about persistence, curiosity, and a willingness to learn from mistakes.

Her first real assignment was nothing glamorous—a community event that most people would have overlooked. She arrived nervous, notebook in hand, unsure of what to ask. The people she interviewed were friendly but guarded, and she struggled to find the right angle for her story. Back at the office, she agonized over every sentence, worried that her words wouldn’t do justice to the people she’d met. When the story finally appeared in print, it was a small victory. It wasn’t perfect, but it was hers.

Most people don’t realize how much of journalism is about showing up, listening, and trying again after setbacks. Early on, she learned that mistakes were inevitable. There were times when she misquoted someone or missed a crucial detail, and the embarrassment stung. But each misstep became a lesson in humility and resilience. The newsroom could be unforgiving, but it was also a place where growth happened quickly. Over time, she found her voice, learned to trust her instincts, and discovered that the best stories often came from the most unexpected places.

The world of journalism was changing, even then. Technology was beginning to reshape how news was gathered and shared. The old-timers grumbled about computers replacing typewriters, but the younger staff saw opportunity. She watched as the newsroom adapted, sometimes reluctantly, to new ways of working. It became clear that adaptability was as important as skill. Those who clung to the old ways risked being left behind, while those willing to learn found new avenues for storytelling.

Looking back, those early years were a mix of excitement, fear, and discovery. The lessons learned in that small-town newsroom—about persistence, integrity, and the power of words—would shape everything that followed. The journey had only just begun, but the foundation was set: journalism was not just a job, but a way of seeing and engaging with the world.

## Chapter 2 Breaking Barriers and Finding a Place in the Newsroom

Stepping into a newsroom as a woman in the late twentieth century meant navigating a landscape shaped by tradition and unspoken rules. The atmosphere was often charged with competition, and the presence of women was still seen as something of a novelty in many quarters. For those determined to make their mark, every day brought a new set of challenges—some obvious, others more subtle.

In the early days, the newsroom felt like a club with its own rituals and hierarchies. The desks were crowded with men who had been there for years, their banter laced with inside jokes and a certain skepticism toward newcomers. For a young woman eager to prove herself, the pressure was intense. There were moments when her ideas were dismissed or her questions brushed aside, not because they lacked merit, but because of who was asking them. The expectation was clear: she would have to work twice as hard to be taken seriously.

Yet, there were also allies—editors and colleagues who recognized talent and offered guidance. One senior reporter, known for his sharp wit and encyclopedic knowledge of local politics, took her under his wing. He didn’t sugarcoat the realities of the job, but he shared tips on how to dig deeper, how to spot a story where others saw only routine, and how to handle sources who tried to manipulate the narrative. Through these informal lessons, she learned that journalism was as much about relationships as it was about facts.

The stories she covered began to change. No longer confined to community events, she was assigned to more complex topics—court cases, political debates, and social issues that demanded sensitivity and tenacity. Each assignment brought new obstacles. There were times when sources refused to speak to her, or when she was excluded from key meetings. The frustration was real, but so was the determination to push through. It became clear that the only way to earn respect was to deliver work that couldn’t be ignored.

One turning point came during a high-profile investigation into local government corruption. The story required patience, discretion, and a willingness to ask uncomfortable questions. She spent weeks sifting through documents, meeting with whistleblowers in quiet cafes, and piecing together a narrative that others had missed. When the story broke, it caused a stir—not just in the community, but within the newsroom itself. Colleagues who had once doubted her abilities now sought her input, and editors began to trust her with more significant assignments.

Despite these successes, the challenges didn’t disappear. The culture of the newsroom was slow to change, and there were still moments when she felt isolated or underestimated. But with each story, her confidence grew. She found strength in the camaraderie of other women in the industry, sharing experiences and strategies for navigating the unique pressures they faced. Together, they celebrated victories and supported each other through setbacks.

The experience of breaking into a male-dominated field left a lasting impression. It became clear that progress was possible, but it required persistence, solidarity, and a willingness to challenge the status quo. The newsroom was evolving, albeit slowly, and those who pushed for change were laying the groundwork for future generations. Through it all, the core values of journalism—curiosity, integrity, and a commitment to truth—remained the guiding lights.

## Chapter 3 The Realities of Reporting—Ethics, Mistakes, and Responsibility

The heart of journalism beats in the tension between urgency and accuracy. Every day, reporters face the pressure to deliver news quickly, but the responsibility to get it right weighs just as heavily. For those who care deeply about their work, the stakes feel personal. Each story is a chance to inform, to connect, and sometimes to make a difference—but also an opportunity to stumble.

In the thick of a breaking news story, the newsroom becomes a hive of activity. Phones ring, editors shout instructions, and reporters race against the clock. In these moments, the temptation to cut corners can be strong. There was a time when a promising lead arrived late in the day—a tip about a local business accused of wrongdoing. The facts were murky, and the pressure to publish was intense. She worked late into the night, chasing down sources and double-checking details. In the end, the story held up, but the experience left her shaken. It became clear that the line between speed and accuracy was razor-thin, and that one misstep could have real consequences for the people involved.

Mistakes did happen. There was the time a source was misquoted, leading to confusion and anger. The fallout was immediate—angry calls, demands for corrections, and a sense of having let people down. Owning up to the error was painful, but necessary. The newsroom had a culture of accountability, and editors insisted on transparency. Corrections were published, apologies made, and lessons learned. Most people don’t realize how much courage it takes to admit a mistake in public, but it’s part of the job.

Ethical dilemmas were a constant companion. Some stories involved vulnerable people—victims of crime, families in crisis, or individuals facing public scrutiny. Deciding what to include, what to leave out, and how to balance the public’s right to know with the need for compassion was never easy. There was a case involving a family tragedy that drew intense media attention. The pressure to get exclusive details was immense, but she chose to focus on the human side of the story, respecting the family’s privacy while still informing the public. The decision wasn’t universally popular, but it felt right.

Over time, the importance of integrity became a guiding principle. There were moments when sources tried to manipulate the narrative, offering information in exchange for favorable coverage. The temptation to take shortcuts or bend the rules was real, especially when the competition was fierce. But the cost of compromising on ethics was too high. The trust of readers, once lost, was hard to regain.

The newsroom itself was a place of constant debate. Editors and reporters argued over headlines, story angles, and the use of anonymous sources. These discussions could be heated, but they were essential. It became clear that journalism was not just about reporting facts, but about making choices—sometimes difficult ones—about what mattered and why.

Looking back, the lessons learned in those high-pressure moments were some of the most valuable. The responsibility to report honestly, to own up to mistakes, and to treat people with respect became the foundation of a career. The work was demanding, but the sense of purpose made it worthwhile. In the end, the real measure of a journalist was not just the stories told, but the way they were told.

## Chapter 4 Adapting to Change—Technology, Culture, and the Shifting Media Landscape

Change is a constant in journalism, but some shifts feel seismic. The arrival of new technology, the rise of digital media, and the changing expectations of audiences transformed the newsroom in ways that few could have predicted. For those who had grown up with typewriters and print deadlines, the transition to computers, email, and online publishing was both exciting and unsettling.

The first computers arrived in the newsroom with little fanfare. Some of the older staff eyed them warily, convinced that nothing could replace the feel of a well-worn typewriter. Others, especially the younger reporters, saw opportunity. Learning to use new tools became a necessity, not a choice. There were moments of frustration—lost files, crashed systems, and the steep learning curve of unfamiliar software. But over time, the benefits became clear. Research that once took hours could now be done in minutes, and stories could be filed from anywhere.

The pace of news accelerated. Deadlines became more frequent, and the pressure to produce content quickly increased. The newsroom adapted, sometimes reluctantly, to the demands of a 24-hour news cycle. There was less time for reflection, and the risk of errors grew. It became clear that the skills needed to succeed were changing. Reporters had to be not just good writers, but also adept at using technology, managing multiple platforms, and engaging with readers in new ways.

The culture of journalism was also evolving. The rise of social media brought new challenges and opportunities. Stories could reach wider audiences, but the line between news and opinion became blurred. There were times when misinformation spread quickly, and the responsibility to verify facts became even more important. The newsroom became a place of constant learning, as reporters adapted to new tools and new ways of connecting with the public.

One memorable moment came when a major story broke online before it appeared in print. The newsroom scrambled to catch up, realizing that the old model of news delivery was no longer enough. It became clear that adaptability was not just an asset, but a requirement. Those who resisted change found themselves sidelined, while those who embraced new ways of working thrived.

The shift to digital also brought new ethical dilemmas. The pressure to attract clicks and drive traffic sometimes clashed with the values of responsible reporting. There were debates about sensationalism, privacy, and the role of journalists in a rapidly changing world. Through it all, the core principles of accuracy, fairness, and integrity remained the touchstones.

Looking back, the experience of adapting to change was both challenging and rewarding. The newsroom became a place of innovation, where new ideas were tested and old assumptions questioned. The ability to learn, to adapt, and to stay true to core values became the keys to survival. The landscape of journalism would continue to evolve, but the commitment to telling important stories endured.

## Chapter 5 The Power of Storytelling and the Lasting Impact of Journalism

At its heart, journalism is about stories—about finding the threads that connect people, places, and events, and weaving them into something meaningful. The most memorable moments in a journalist’s career often come not from the big headlines, but from the quieter stories that reveal something essential about the human experience.

There was a time when a seemingly ordinary assignment—a profile of a local teacher—turned into something much more. The teacher, known for her dedication and warmth, had quietly changed the lives of countless students. As the story unfolded, it became clear that her impact reached far beyond the classroom. Former students shared memories, parents expressed gratitude, and the community rallied around her. The article struck a chord, sparking conversations and inspiring others to recognize the everyday heroes in their midst.

Most people don’t notice the ripple effects of a well-told story. Sometimes, a single article can bring attention to an overlooked issue, prompt action, or offer comfort to those who feel unseen. There was the case of a family struggling with illness, whose story prompted an outpouring of support from readers. Or the investigation into a local injustice that led to real change. These moments reinforced the belief that journalism, at its best, could make a difference.

The process of storytelling was rarely straightforward. It required patience, empathy, and a willingness to listen. There were times when sources were reluctant to share their stories, fearing judgment or misunderstanding. Building trust became essential. The best stories emerged from genuine connections, from taking the time to understand people’s experiences and perspectives.

Over the years, the meaning of success shifted. Early on, it was about bylines and recognition. Later, it became about impact—about knowing that a story had touched someone, changed a mind, or sparked a conversation. The work was often demanding and sometimes thankless, but the rewards were real.

The power of storytelling extended beyond the newsroom. It shaped the way people saw the world, challenged assumptions, and fostered empathy. In a time of rapid change and uncertainty, the need for honest, thoughtful journalism felt more important than ever. The stories that mattered were not always the ones that made the front page, but the ones that lingered in people’s minds and hearts.

Looking back, the journey through journalism was marked by challenges, triumphs, and countless lessons. The work was never easy, but it was always meaningful. The stories told, the people met, and the impact made became a legacy—a reminder that words, when used with care and integrity, could change lives.

## Chapter 6 Mentorship, Guidance, and the Quiet Power of Encouragement

Sometimes, the most profound changes in a career come not from a single event, but from the steady presence of someone who believes in you. In the world of journalism, where deadlines loom and criticism can be sharp, the value of a mentor is hard to overstate. For many, the newsroom is a place of both competition and camaraderie, and the difference often comes down to who’s willing to offer a guiding hand.

There was a period when the pressure felt relentless. The stories were more complex, the stakes higher, and the margin for error smaller. In those moments, it was easy to doubt one’s abilities. But there was always someone—a senior editor, a thoughtful colleague—who noticed the struggle and offered quiet encouragement. Sometimes it was a simple gesture: a note left on a desk after a tough day, or a cup of coffee shared in the early morning hours before the city woke up. Other times, it was more direct: a frank conversation about what went wrong, paired with practical advice on how to do better next time.

One memory stands out—a time when a promising story fell apart at the last minute. The disappointment was sharp, and the temptation to give up was real. But instead of criticism, the editor offered perspective. “Everyone loses a story now and then,” he said. “What matters is what you do next.” That moment of understanding, delivered without judgment, became a turning point. It became clear that growth wasn’t just about getting things right, but about learning from what went wrong.

Mentorship in journalism often happens in small, unplanned ways. A passing comment in the hallway, a shared laugh over a headline that didn’t quite work, or a late-night phone call to talk through a difficult decision. These moments build confidence and resilience. They remind you that you’re not alone, even when the work feels isolating.

Over time, the roles began to shift. The once-nervous newcomer found herself offering advice to others—interns just starting out, or colleagues facing their own doubts. The cycle of mentorship continued, each generation learning from the last. It became clear that the legacy of a journalist wasn’t just the stories published, but the people encouraged along the way.

Most people don’t realize how much of a newsroom’s strength comes from these quiet acts of support. The headlines may belong to individuals, but the real work is collective. The encouragement of a mentor, the solidarity of colleagues, and the willingness to lift each other up—these are the things that sustain a career, and that make the work meaningful, even on the hardest days.

## Chapter 7 Facing Crisis—Reporting in Times of Upheaval

There are moments in every journalist’s life when the world seems to tilt on its axis. Natural disasters, political upheaval, or sudden tragedy can turn an ordinary day into one that will be remembered for years. In these times, the role of the reporter shifts. The work becomes not just about telling stories, but about bearing witness.

One morning, the newsroom was jolted awake by news of a devastating flood. Streets were underwater, families displaced, and the usual routines upended. The assignment was clear: get out there, find the stories, and bring them back. But nothing could prepare anyone for the reality on the ground. The air was thick with anxiety and loss. People clung to what little they had left, searching for loved ones and answers.

In the chaos, the job was to listen. A shop owner, standing in the ruins of his business, spoke quietly about starting over. A mother, separated from her children, waited for news with a mixture of hope and dread. The task was to capture these moments with honesty and care, to show the world what was happening without adding to the pain.

Reporting in crisis meant making difficult choices. There were times when the camera was put down, when the questions stopped, because what people needed most was compassion, not coverage. It became clear that the best journalism was rooted in empathy. The facts mattered, but so did the way they were delivered.

The newsroom itself became a place of urgency and exhaustion. Reporters worked around the clock, fueled by adrenaline and a sense of duty. Mistakes were made, tempers flared, but the shared purpose held everyone together. In the aftermath, there was time to reflect. The stories told during those days were not just about loss, but about resilience—the small acts of kindness, the communities coming together, the hope that survived even in the darkest moments.

Most people don’t see the toll that crisis reporting takes. The images linger long after the headlines fade. But there is also a sense of privilege—a recognition that being trusted with people’s stories, especially in their most vulnerable moments, is an honor and a responsibility.

Looking back, those experiences became a touchstone. They shaped the way stories were approached, the questions asked, and the care taken with every word. In times of upheaval, journalism became more than a job. It became a way to help people make sense of the world, to find meaning in the chaos, and to remind everyone that even in crisis, stories matter.

## Chapter 8 Legacy, Reflection, and the Changing Face of Journalism

As the years passed, the newsroom changed. Faces came and went, technology evolved, and the pace of news only quickened. For those who had spent decades in the business, there came a time to look back—to consider not just the stories told, but the impact left behind.

There was a quiet pride in seeing former colleagues and mentees thrive. Some had moved on to bigger publications, others had shifted to new careers, but the lessons learned in the newsroom stayed with them. The values of curiosity, integrity, and persistence echoed in their work, no matter where they landed.

Reflection brought a mix of satisfaction and regret. There were stories that made a difference—investigations that led to change, profiles that gave voice to the unheard, features that brought comfort or understanding. But there were also missed opportunities, stories that slipped away, or moments when the pressure to deliver overshadowed the need for care.

The industry itself was in flux. The rise of digital media, the decline of print, and the constant churn of the news cycle created uncertainty. Younger journalists faced new challenges—precarious contracts, online harassment, and the pressure to build a personal brand. The old certainties were gone, replaced by a landscape that demanded constant adaptation.

Yet, through all the change, some things endured. The need for honest, thoughtful reporting. The importance of listening. The belief that stories could still make a difference. It became clear that the legacy of a journalist was not just in the bylines, but in the trust built with readers, the relationships forged with sources, and the quiet moments of encouragement shared with colleagues.

There was a sense of responsibility to help shape the future. Speaking at schools, mentoring young reporters, and advocating for ethical standards became part of the work. The hope was that the next generation would carry forward the best of what had come before, while finding new ways to meet the challenges ahead.

Most people don’t realize how much of journalism’s legacy is built on small, everyday acts—choosing the right word, asking the extra question, standing up for what’s right even when it’s hard. Looking back, it became clear that the real impact was measured not in awards or headlines, but in the lives touched along the way.

## Chapter 9 The Future—Hope, Uncertainty, and the Next Chapter

The future of journalism is a question that lingers in every newsroom. The tools may change, the platforms may shift, but the need for stories endures. For those who have spent a lifetime chasing deadlines, the prospect of stepping back brings both relief and uncertainty.

There’s a new generation coming up—digital natives who move effortlessly between platforms, who see possibilities where others see obstacles. They bring fresh energy, new ideas, and a willingness to challenge old assumptions. Watching them work is both humbling and inspiring. It’s a reminder that the story of journalism is still being written.

But the challenges are real. The business model is fragile, trust in media is under strain, and the noise of misinformation grows louder every day. The temptation to despair is strong. Yet, there are reasons for hope. In small newsrooms and independent outlets, in podcasts and newsletters, people are finding new ways to tell important stories. The hunger for truth, for connection, for understanding—these things haven’t changed.

For those nearing the end of their careers, the question becomes: what now? Some find new purpose in teaching, in writing books, or in simply taking time to reflect. Others stay involved, offering guidance and support to those just starting out. The transition is bittersweet, but there is comfort in knowing that the work mattered.

Most people don’t see the quiet moments of doubt, the late nights spent wrestling with a story, or the satisfaction of getting it right. But those who have lived it know: journalism is not just a job, but a calling. The future is uncertain, but the need for honest, thoughtful storytelling endures.

Looking ahead, there is hope. The tools may change, but the heart of journalism remains the same—a commitment to truth, to empathy, and to making sense of the world. The next chapter is unwritten, but the story continues.

## Chapter 10 Wisdom Gathered—Lessons for Life and Work

After years spent chasing stories, meeting deadlines, and navigating the shifting tides of the newsroom, certain truths become clear. The lessons learned in journalism reach far beyond the world of news—they shape the way we see ourselves, each other, and the world around us.

One lesson stands out: the importance of listening. In every interview, every conversation, the real story often lies beneath the surface. Taking the time to listen—not just to the words, but to the silences—can reveal what truly matters. It’s a skill that serves not just journalists, but anyone seeking to understand others.

Another truth: mistakes are inevitable. No one gets it right every time. The key is to own up, to learn, and to keep moving forward. The newsroom is a place where errors are public, but so is growth. The courage to admit when you’re wrong, and the humility to learn from it, are qualities that matter in every field.

Integrity is non-negotiable. The temptation to cut corners, to chase clicks, or to bend the truth is real. But the cost is too high. Trust, once lost, is hard to regain. The best journalists—and the best people—are those who hold fast to their values, even when it’s difficult.

Finally, the power of stories endures. Whether told in print, online, or face-to-face, stories connect us. They help us make sense of the world, challenge our assumptions, and inspire us to act. The work of journalism is never finished, but every story told with care and honesty leaves a mark.

Looking back, the journey through journalism was about more than headlines and deadlines. It was about people—those who shared their stories, those who offered guidance, and those who listened. The lessons learned in the newsroom are lessons for life: be curious, be kind, and never stop seeking the truth.
