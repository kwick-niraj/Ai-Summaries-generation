## Introduction

The journey into the moral and psychological terrain of war reveals unsettling truths about the transformation of ordinary individuals under extraordinary circumstances. None of Us Were Like This Before by Joshua E.S. Phillips delves into the harrowing experiences of American soldiers who found themselves entangled in acts of torture during the conflicts in Iraq and Afghanistan. The narrative traces the subtle and overt pressures that led these soldiers down a path they never anticipated, exploring the complex interplay between duty, obedience, and the erosion of personal boundaries. The consequences of these actions ripple far beyond the battlefield, raising profound questions about accountability, trauma, and the cost of compromising one’s values.

Through intimate accounts and careful investigation, the story unfolds to reveal how institutional culture, ambiguous orders, and the fog of war can reshape the very core of a person’s identity. The soldiers’ voices echo with regret, confusion, and a longing for understanding, painting a vivid portrait of the psychological aftermath that lingers long after the guns fall silent. The exploration moves beyond individual culpability to examine the broader systems and policies that enabled such behavior, inviting reflection on the responsibilities of leadership and the enduring scars left on those who serve. In this landscape, the boundaries between victim and perpetrator blur, setting the stage for a deeper examination of conscience, memory, and the search for redemption.

## Chapter 1 The First Steps Toward War and Uncertainty

The world changed for many Americans in the months after September 11, 2001. For some, the shift was immediate—a sense of duty, a surge of patriotism, or simply the need for a steady job led them to military recruitment offices. Most of these new soldiers were young, often just out of high school, carrying with them the ordinary hopes and anxieties of people on the edge of adulthood. They didn’t see themselves as warriors or heroes. They were kids from small towns, city neighborhoods, and suburbs, suddenly swept into a conflict that felt both distant and deeply personal.

The early days of the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq were marked by confusion and improvisation. Soldiers found themselves in unfamiliar landscapes, surrounded by languages and customs they didn’t understand. The rules of engagement were often unclear, and the enemy was hard to define. Some days, the biggest challenge was boredom; on others, it was the constant threat of roadside bombs or sniper fire. The line between friend and foe blurred quickly, and trust became a rare commodity.

One young man, who had joined the Army to escape a dead-end job and maybe see the world, found himself patrolling dusty streets in Iraq. He remembered the training back home—lessons about honor, discipline, and the laws of war. But in the field, those lessons felt distant. Orders changed from day to day. Sometimes, the people they were supposed to protect seemed just as likely to turn against them. The pressure to keep everyone safe, to avoid mistakes, weighed heavily.

In the midst of this uncertainty, rumors began to circulate. Stories of captured insurgents, of intelligence that could save lives, of interrogations that went further than anyone wanted to admit. Most soldiers didn’t see themselves as the kind of people who would cross the line. They believed in the rules, in the idea that America was different. But the stress of constant danger, the fear of the unknown, and the desire to protect their friends started to erode those convictions.

It became clear that the war was not just being fought on the battlefield. It was also a struggle within each person—a battle between the values they brought from home and the realities they faced every day. The seeds of something darker were being planted, often unnoticed, in the cracks between orders and actions, between fear and duty.

## Chapter 2 How Ordinary People Become Part of Something Unthinkable

As the months dragged on, the war’s demands grew heavier. Soldiers who had once joked about going home soon realized that their tours would be longer than expected. The sense of purpose that had carried them through the early days began to fade, replaced by exhaustion and frustration. The enemy seemed to be everywhere and nowhere at once. Every civilian was a potential threat; every shadow could hide danger.

In this environment, the pressure to get results intensified. Commanders wanted information—names, locations, plans. The safety of the unit depended on knowing what the enemy was planning. Interrogations became more frequent, and the line between questioning and coercion started to blur. Some soldiers, eager to prove themselves or protect their friends, pushed harder than they should have. Others watched, uneasy, as boundaries shifted.

One night, a group of soldiers brought in a man suspected of planting bombs. He was scared, shaking, and spoke little English. The interrogator, a peer who had never imagined himself in this role, felt the weight of responsibility. He remembered the faces of friends injured by roadside explosions. The urge to get answers was overwhelming. At first, he tried to follow the rules—asking questions, offering water, trying to build rapport. But when the man refused to talk, frustration took over. The methods became harsher. Shouting, threats, and eventually physical force.

Most people don’t notice when the line is crossed. It happens in small steps—a shove instead of a push, a slap instead of a warning. Each time, it feels justified. The mission is too important, the risks too great. The rules start to feel like obstacles, not protections. And when no one steps in to stop it, the new normal takes hold.

For many, the first time was the hardest. After that, it became easier to justify. The enemy was dangerous, they told themselves. He would do the same to us. The sense of moral clarity that had once guided them faded, replaced by a grim pragmatism. The war was changing them, in ways they barely understood.

Back at home, families and friends had no idea what was happening. Letters and phone calls were filled with reassurances—stories about the weather, about missing home, about the food. No one talked about the things that kept them up at night. The shame, the guilt, the fear of what they were becoming. It was easier to pretend that nothing had changed.

But deep down, most knew that something was slipping away. The person they had been before the war was starting to feel like a stranger.

## Chapter 3 The System That Allowed Torture to Spread

It would be comforting to believe that the acts of torture and abuse that emerged during the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq were the work of a few bad actors—people who were cruel by nature, or who snapped under pressure. But the reality was more complicated. The system itself, with its shifting policies and lack of oversight, created an environment where abuse could flourish.

Orders from above were often vague or contradictory. Interrogators were told to get results, but not given clear guidelines about what was allowed. Some commanders looked the other way, more concerned with numbers and outcomes than with how those outcomes were achieved. Others actively encouraged harsher methods, convinced that the ends justified the means.

In one detention facility, a group of soldiers found themselves responsible for dozens of prisoners. They had little training in interrogation, and even less support from their superiors. The rules seemed to change with each new shift. One day, they were told to treat prisoners with respect; the next, they were encouraged to “soften them up” for questioning. The confusion bred resentment and fear.

Most of the soldiers were not monsters. They were ordinary people, trying to do an impossible job with too little guidance. But the system made it easy to rationalize cruelty. When abuse did occur, it was often ignored or covered up. Reports were lost, complaints dismissed. The message was clear: results mattered more than methods.

Some tried to speak out. A few wrote letters, filed reports, or confided in chaplains. But the risks were high. Whistleblowers were often ostracized, their careers threatened. The culture of silence was powerful, and most chose to keep their heads down.

The public, for the most part, remained unaware. When stories of abuse did surface—grainy photos, leaked memos, whispered rumors—they were dismissed as isolated incidents. Few wanted to believe that the problem was systemic, that the institutions they trusted were complicit.

It became clear that the real danger was not just in the acts themselves, but in the way the system allowed them to happen. The lack of accountability, the shifting standards, the willingness to look the other way—all of these made it possible for ordinary people to become part of something unthinkable.

## Chapter 4 The Human Cost: Moral Injury and Lasting Trauma

Long after the headlines faded and the wars moved on, the people who had been part of these events carried the weight of what they had seen and done. For some, the memories came back in nightmares—faces, voices, moments they couldn’t forget. For others, the pain was quieter, a sense of shame or loss that lingered just below the surface.

One former soldier, now back in his hometown, struggled to reconnect with the life he had left behind. Friends noticed that he was quieter, more withdrawn. He avoided crowds, flinched at sudden noises, and rarely talked about his time overseas. When he did, it was in vague terms—“It was tough,” or “We did what we had to do.” But the truth was harder to admit. He couldn’t shake the feeling that he had crossed a line, that he had become someone he didn’t recognize.

Many who participated in or witnessed acts of torture found themselves haunted by what psychologists call “moral injury.” Unlike physical wounds, these injuries were invisible, but no less real. They came from the sense that they had betrayed their own values, that they had done something unforgivable. The military had taught them to be strong, to follow orders, to protect their fellow soldiers. But it had not prepared them for the aftermath—the guilt, the self-doubt, the isolation.

Some tried to numb the pain with alcohol or drugs. Others threw themselves into work or family, hoping that the routines of daily life would drown out the memories. A few sought help, but found that the stigma around mental health made it hard to talk about what they had experienced. The fear of being judged, of being seen as weak or broken, kept many silent.

The impact was not limited to those who had committed acts of abuse. Witnesses, too, carried the burden. Some felt complicit, guilty for not speaking out or for failing to stop what was happening. Others struggled with anger—at their leaders, at the system, at themselves.

Over time, the cost of these experiences became clear. Relationships suffered. Trust was hard to rebuild. The sense of purpose that had once driven them was replaced by a deep uncertainty. What had it all been for? Was it worth the price?

Most people never saw this side of the war. The stories that made the news were about battles and politics, not about the quiet suffering of those who came home changed. But for the people who lived it, the war never really ended. The wounds, both visible and invisible, remained.

## Chapter 5 Dehumanization and the Erosion of Values

Looking back, it’s easy to wonder how things went so wrong. How did people who started out with good intentions end up doing things they once would have condemned? The answer, in many cases, lay in the slow process of dehumanization.

At first, the enemy was just that—an enemy. Someone on the other side, someone dangerous. But over time, the language changed. Prisoners became “detainees,” then “subjects,” then something less than human. The constant stress, the fear, the anger—all of it made it easier to see the other as an object, not a person.

In one unit, soldiers joked about the people they were guarding. The jokes were a way to cope, to make the job easier. But they also chipped away at empathy. It became easier to justify harsh treatment, to see pain as deserved or necessary. The rituals of war—nicknames, routines, inside jokes—created a sense of distance, a barrier between “us” and “them.”

Most didn’t notice the change as it was happening. It was gradual, almost invisible. Each small step made the next one easier. The first time someone was mistreated, there was hesitation, maybe even regret. But the more it happened, the more normal it became. The group’s approval mattered more than individual doubts.

The military’s training was supposed to prevent this. Soldiers were taught about the laws of war, about the importance of treating prisoners humanely. But in the chaos of conflict, those lessons were often forgotten. The pressure to conform, to fit in, to be part of the team was overwhelming.

Some tried to hold on to their values. They refused to participate, or spoke out when they saw something wrong. But they were often isolated, seen as troublemakers or weak links. The cost of resistance was high, and most chose to go along.

The process of dehumanization didn’t just affect the way soldiers treated the enemy. It also changed the way they saw themselves. The sense of pride and honor that had once defined their service was replaced by shame and doubt. The war had not just changed what they did—it had changed who they were.

## Chapter 6 Coming Home to a Changed World

Returning home was supposed to be a relief—a chance to leave the war behind and start over. But for many, stepping off the plane was just the beginning of a new kind of struggle. The world they came back to looked the same, but nothing felt familiar. Family and friends tried to welcome them, but the distance was there, unspoken and heavy.

One woman, who had spent months working in a detention facility, found herself unable to sleep in her own bed. The quiet of her childhood home felt suffocating after the constant noise and tension of the base. She tried to slip back into old routines—coffee with her mother, walks with her dog—but the memories followed her everywhere. Sometimes, she would catch herself flinching at the sound of a car backfiring, or scanning crowds for threats that weren’t there.

Conversations with loved ones were the hardest. People wanted to know what it had been like, but she didn’t have the words. How could she explain the things she had seen, or the choices she had made? When she tried, the questions came—Did you ever hurt anyone? Did you see anything bad? She would shake her head, change the subject, or offer a sanitized version of the truth. It was easier to let people believe that everything was fine.

Most people don’t realize how hard it is to come home changed. The world expects you to pick up where you left off, but the person you were before is gone. The habits and instincts that kept you alive overseas don’t fit in civilian life. The guilt and shame linger, making it hard to trust yourself, let alone others.

Some veterans found comfort in each other. They gathered in bars or support groups, sharing stories that no one else could understand. There was relief in knowing you weren’t alone, but also a sense of being trapped—bound together by experiences that set you apart from everyone else.

The struggle to adjust was not just about trauma or regret. It was about finding a way to live with what had happened, to make sense of a world that no longer made sense. For many, the hardest part was realizing that the war would always be with them, shaping their lives in ways they could never have imagined.

## Chapter 7 The Search for Accountability

As stories of abuse and torture began to surface in the media, the country was forced to confront uncomfortable questions. Who was responsible? How could this have happened? The answers were rarely simple.

Some officials insisted that the acts were isolated incidents, the work of a few “bad apples.” Investigations were launched, and a handful of low-ranking soldiers were put on trial. The public watched as photos and testimonies emerged—images of hooded prisoners, stories of humiliation and pain. There was outrage, but also confusion. Was this really what America stood for?

Behind the scenes, the search for accountability was complicated by politics and bureaucracy. Policies had been written in vague language, leaving room for interpretation. Orders had been given verbally, with little paper trail. When investigators tried to trace responsibility up the chain of command, they ran into walls—denials, missing documents, shifting stories.

One officer, who had tried to report abuses in his unit, found himself caught in a web of blame and denial. His superiors dismissed his concerns, insisting that he was exaggerating or misunderstood. When he persisted, he was reassigned, his career stalled. The message was clear: speaking out came at a cost.

For many, the sense of betrayal was profound. They had believed in the system, trusted their leaders, and followed orders. Now, they felt abandoned—scapegoated for decisions made far above their heads. The promise of justice felt hollow.

Most people don’t see how hard it is to hold institutions accountable. The machinery of government moves slowly, and those with power are skilled at protecting themselves. The truth is often buried under layers of secrecy and spin.

Still, some refused to give up. Journalists, lawyers, and activists pushed for answers, demanding transparency and reform. Their efforts brought some change—new policies, better training, more oversight. But the scars remained, both for those who had suffered and for those who had been complicit.

The search for accountability was not just about punishment. It was about facing the truth, however painful, and trying to build something better from the wreckage.

## Chapter 8 The Ripple Effects: Families, Communities, and the Nation

The impact of torture and abuse did not end with the soldiers or the prisoners. It rippled outward, touching families, communities, and the country as a whole. The wounds were not always visible, but they were real.

In one small town, a mother noticed her son had changed since coming home. He was quick to anger, slow to trust, and seemed haunted by things he wouldn’t talk about. She tried to help, but nothing seemed to reach him. The distance grew, and she wondered if she would ever get her boy back.

Elsewhere, a group of veterans struggled to find work. Employers were wary, unsure how to handle the emotional baggage that came with military service. Some veterans withdrew, feeling misunderstood and unwanted. Others lashed out, their pain spilling over into relationships and daily life.

Communities, too, felt the strain. The wars had divided people—over politics, over values, over what it meant to be American. Some saw the revelations of abuse as a betrayal of everything the country stood for. Others insisted that harsh measures were necessary, that the ends justified the means. The arguments played out in living rooms, on talk shows, and in the halls of Congress.

Most people don’t realize how deeply these divisions run. The legacy of torture is not just about what happened overseas. It’s about the stories we tell ourselves, the values we claim to hold, and the ways we respond when those values are tested.

For some, the revelations sparked a reckoning—a determination to confront the past and demand better. For others, the instinct was to look away, to move on, to forget. But the wounds lingered, shaping the country’s sense of itself and its place in the world.

The ripple effects of war and abuse are hard to measure. They show up in broken families, in lost trust, in the quiet struggles of people trying to find their way home. The cost is paid not just by those who served, but by everyone who lives in the shadow of what was done.

## Chapter 9 The Long Road to Healing

Healing from the wounds of war and torture is a slow, uneven process. There are no easy answers, no quick fixes. For many, the journey begins with the simple act of telling the truth—admitting what happened, to themselves and to others.

One former interrogator, wracked by guilt, finally reached out to a therapist. The first sessions were awkward, filled with long silences and half-finished sentences. But over time, the words came. He talked about the pressure, the fear, the moments when he lost himself. He talked about the nightmares, the shame, the longing for forgiveness.

The process was painful, but also freeing. For the first time, he felt seen—not as a monster, but as a person who had made mistakes and wanted to make amends. The therapist didn’t judge or excuse, but listened. That simple act of listening made all the difference.

Others found healing in different ways. Some joined support groups, sharing their stories with people who understood. Some turned to faith, seeking comfort in rituals and community. A few became activists, determined to prevent others from making the same mistakes.

Most people don’t realize how much courage it takes to face the past. The temptation to bury the pain, to pretend it never happened, is strong. But real healing begins with honesty—with the willingness to look at what was done, and to ask what can be done differently.

The road to healing is long, and there are setbacks along the way. Some days, the memories are too much. Some days, the anger or shame feels overwhelming. But with time, and with support, it is possible to find a measure of peace.

Healing is not about forgetting. It’s about learning to live with the past, to carry it without letting it define you. It’s about finding hope, even in the aftermath of terrible choices.

## Chapter 10 Lessons for the Future

Looking back, it’s easy to feel overwhelmed by the weight of what happened. The stories of torture and abuse are hard to hear, and harder still to accept. But they also offer lessons—about power, about responsibility, about what it means to be human.

One lesson is that ordinary people, in extraordinary circumstances, can be led to do things they never imagined. The line between right and wrong is not always clear, especially when fear and pressure are constant companions. Most people don’t notice when they start to slip, when small compromises add up to something unthinkable.

Another lesson is that systems matter. Policies, training, oversight—these are not just bureaucratic details. They shape the choices people make, the boundaries they respect, the values they uphold. When systems fail, the consequences are profound.

But perhaps the most important lesson is about the power of empathy. The process of dehumanization is slow and subtle, but it can be resisted. It takes effort to see the humanity in others, especially in the midst of conflict. It takes courage to speak out, to refuse to go along, to hold on to your values when it would be easier to let them go.

The legacy of torture is a warning, but also a call to action. It reminds us that the choices we make—individually and collectively—matter. That the values we claim are only as strong as our willingness to defend them.

Most people want to believe that they would do the right thing, even in the hardest circumstances. The stories in this book are a reminder that doing the right thing is rarely easy, but always necessary.

## Conclusion

If you’ve followed the stories and struggles in these pages, you know that the journey from idealism to disillusionment, from action to aftermath, is never simple. The experiences of American soldiers in the wars of the early 21st century reveal how easily good intentions can be twisted by fear, pressure, and broken systems. But they also show the enduring possibility of reflection, accountability, and healing.

**Key takeaways to carry forward:**

- Small compromises, repeated, can lead anyone across lines they never imagined.
- Systems and leadership shape the boundaries of what is possible and permissible.
- The pain of moral injury is real, and healing requires honesty and support.
- Dehumanization is gradual, but empathy and resistance are always possible.
- Accountability is not just about blame—it’s about facing the truth and changing course.

If you find yourself wrestling with the weight of your own choices, or the choices of your community or country, remember that you are not alone. The path to healing and change is rarely straight, and it is never easy. But it is always worth walking. The stories in this book are not just warnings—they are invitations. Invitations to listen, to reflect, to speak up, and to act with courage and compassion, even when the world makes it hard. The past cannot be undone, but the future is always being written—by the choices we make, and the values we choose to live by, every single day.
