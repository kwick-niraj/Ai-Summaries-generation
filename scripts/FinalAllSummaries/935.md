## Introduction

Generational pain often lingers in the body, shaping lives in ways that words alone cannot express. My Grandmother's Hands: Racialized Trauma and the Mending of Our Bodies and Hearts by Resmaa Menakem delves into the profound impact of racialized trauma, not just as a psychological or social phenomenon, but as a deeply embodied experience. The legacy of oppression, violence, and fear is carried within muscles, nervous systems, and even the smallest gestures, influencing how people relate to themselves and to others. Healing, therefore, must reach beyond intellect and conversation, touching the very core of physical being.

Within this exploration, the journey toward mending unfolds through the recognition that trauma is not only inherited but also perpetuated through unhealed wounds. The path to wholeness involves learning to listen to the body’s signals, understanding the roots of pain, and cultivating practices that foster resilience and connection. By weaving together personal stories, historical context, and somatic wisdom, the narrative invites a compassionate reckoning with the past and a hopeful vision for collective healing. The invitation is to move from pain to possibility, from isolation to community, and from inherited suffering to embodied liberation.

## Chapter 1 How Racial Trauma Lives in the Body

It’s easy to think of racism as something that happens out in the world—an argument, a news headline, a policy. But for many, the real impact of racism is felt much closer to home, deep inside the body. The story often begins in small, everyday moments: a child tenses up when a parent’s voice sharpens, or a teenager’s heart pounds after a tense encounter at school. These reactions seem ordinary, but they are the body’s way of holding onto pain, fear, and uncertainty—sometimes for years, sometimes for generations.

One memory stands out: a young boy sits at his grandmother’s kitchen table, watching her hands as she kneads dough. Her hands are strong, marked by years of work and worry. She hums softly, but her eyes carry a heaviness. The boy doesn’t know the details, but he senses something in the way she moves—a kind of caution, a readiness to flinch. Later, he learns that his grandmother grew up in a time and place where safety was never guaranteed, where a knock at the door could mean trouble. Her body learned to brace itself, and that lesson was passed down, not just in stories, but in muscle and bone.

Most people don’t realize that trauma isn’t just a memory or a feeling. It’s a physical experience, stored in the nervous system. When someone faces a threat—real or imagined—the body reacts: muscles tighten, breathing changes, the heart races. Over time, if these threats are constant or unpredictable, the body adapts by staying on high alert. This is especially true for those who live with the daily stress of racism. The body learns to expect danger, even when none is present.

This kind of trauma doesn’t just affect individuals. It ripples through families and communities. A parent who grew up with fear may become overprotective, teaching their children to be wary. A teacher, shaped by their own experiences, might react with suspicion or impatience. Even those who don’t experience racism directly can carry its weight—sometimes as guilt, sometimes as anxiety, sometimes as a vague sense of unease.

The body’s response to trauma is universal, but the reasons for that trauma are not. In America, the legacy of slavery, segregation, and violence has left deep marks on Black bodies. But White bodies, too, carry their own burdens—often unspoken, sometimes denied. Police officers, caught between duty and fear, develop their own patterns of tension and vigilance. Each group holds pain in different ways, but the result is the same: a society where bodies are always bracing, always waiting for the next blow.

Healing begins with noticing. Most people rush through their days, ignoring the tightness in their shoulders or the knot in their stomach. But when someone pauses and pays attention, they start to see the ways trauma shapes their life. A simple breath, a moment of stillness, can reveal old wounds. It’s not easy—sometimes the pain feels too big, too old to face. But every act of noticing is a step toward healing.

The journey is not just personal. When one person begins to heal, it creates space for others to do the same. A parent who learns to calm their own body can offer comfort to a frightened child. A teacher who understands their own triggers can respond with patience instead of anger. Over time, these small changes add up, creating a ripple effect that can transform families, schools, and communities.

But the work is slow, and it requires courage. It means facing not just personal pain, but the pain of ancestors and the pain of a nation. It means learning to sit with discomfort, to listen to the body’s wisdom, and to trust that healing is possible—even when the wounds are deep.

## Chapter 2 The Roots of Generational Pain

In many families, stories are passed down like heirlooms. Some are told with pride—a grandfather’s bravery, a mother’s kindness. Others are whispered, or left unspoken, but their presence is felt all the same. For many Black families in America, the stories of survival are woven with pain: the fear of violence, the loss of loved ones, the constant need to adapt. These stories don’t just live in memory; they live in the body.

Imagine a woman who grew up hearing her mother warn her to be careful, to keep her head down, to avoid drawing attention. She learns to move quietly, to watch for danger, to hide her feelings. As she grows older, she passes these lessons to her own children—not out of malice, but out of love. She wants them to be safe. But the cost is high: her children learn to be afraid, to mistrust their own instincts, to carry a weight they can’t name.

This is how trauma moves through generations. It’s not just the big events—the violence, the loss, the injustice. It’s the small, everyday moments: a parent’s warning, a neighbor’s glance, a teacher’s impatience. Each moment leaves a mark, shaping the way people see themselves and the world.

But generational trauma is not unique to Black families. White families, too, carry their own burdens. Some grew up with stories of hardship and struggle, of ancestors who fled war or poverty. Others learned to look away from suffering, to deny or minimize the pain of others. These lessons, too, are passed down—not always in words, but in attitudes, in habits, in the way bodies tense or relax in certain situations.

Police officers, often caught in the middle, develop their own patterns of trauma. Many enter the profession with a desire to help, but quickly learn to expect danger. Their bodies become attuned to threat, their minds trained to react quickly. Over time, this constant vigilance takes a toll. Some become hardened, others numb. Many struggle to connect with their own families, unable to turn off the alarm bells that ring in their bodies.

Most people don’t realize how much of their behavior is shaped by these old wounds. A child who grows up in fear may become an adult who avoids conflict, or who lashes out at the slightest provocation. A police officer who has seen too much may become suspicious of everyone, unable to trust even those closest to them. A teacher who grew up with privilege may struggle to understand the pain of their students, or may react defensively when challenged.

Healing generational trauma requires more than just understanding the past. It means learning to recognize the ways old pain shows up in the present. It means noticing the body’s reactions—tightness, numbness, anger, fear—and learning to respond with compassion instead of judgment. It means creating new stories, new patterns, new ways of being.

This work is not easy. It often brings up feelings of shame, guilt, or helplessness. Many people would rather look away, or blame others, than face their own pain. But the only way out is through. By facing the past, by listening to the body, by reaching out for support, it’s possible to break the cycle of trauma and create a new legacy for future generations.

## Chapter 3 The Body’s Wisdom and the Limits of Talk

Most people are taught to solve problems with words. When something hurts, the instinct is to talk it out, to analyze, to find a solution. But some wounds run deeper than language. The body remembers what the mind forgets. A sudden noise, a harsh word, a certain look—these can trigger a flood of feelings that seem to come from nowhere. The body reacts before the mind can catch up.

Consider a man who prides himself on being rational. He believes that if he can just understand his feelings, he can control them. But when he’s pulled over by the police, his hands start to shake, his heart races, and his mind goes blank. Later, he’s embarrassed by his reaction. He tells himself to calm down, to be reasonable. But the fear lingers, and he can’t explain why.

This is the body’s wisdom at work. The nervous system is designed to keep people safe. When it senses danger, it prepares to fight, flee, or freeze. These reactions are automatic, shaped by years of experience—both personal and inherited. For those who live with the constant threat of racism, the body learns to react quickly, to stay alert, to expect the worst.

Talking about trauma can help, but it’s not enough. Words can explain, but they can’t always heal. The body needs to feel safe before it can let go of old pain. This is why so many people struggle to change, even when they want to. The mind says one thing, but the body says another.

Healing requires a different approach. It means learning to listen to the body, to notice its signals, to respond with care. Simple practices—like deep breathing, gentle movement, or mindful touch—can help the body feel safe again. These practices are not a cure-all, but they are a starting point. Over time, they help the nervous system learn new patterns, new ways of responding to stress.

One woman, after years of talk therapy, found that her anxiety didn’t budge until she started paying attention to her body. She learned to notice when her shoulders tensed, when her breath grew shallow, when her jaw clenched. Instead of ignoring these signals, she learned to pause, to breathe, to let her body know it was safe. Slowly, the anxiety eased. She still had hard days, but she no longer felt trapped by her own reactions.

Most people underestimate the power of these small shifts. They want big changes, quick fixes. But healing is a slow process, built on tiny moments of awareness and care. It’s not about erasing the past, but about learning to live with it in a new way.

This approach is especially important for those who carry racialized trauma. The body’s reactions are not just personal—they are shaped by history, by family, by community. Healing requires more than individual effort. It means creating spaces where people feel safe, where bodies can relax, where old wounds can begin to mend.

The work is ongoing. There will always be setbacks, moments of fear or pain. But each act of care, each moment of awareness, is a step toward healing. Over time, these small acts add up, creating a new sense of safety, a new way of being in the world.

## Chapter 4 The Impact of White Supremacy on All Bodies

It’s tempting to think of racism as a problem that only affects those who are targeted by it. But the truth is more complicated. White supremacy—the belief that White people are superior to others—hurts everyone, though in different ways. Its effects are felt not just in laws and institutions, but in the bodies of individuals, families, and communities.

For many Black Americans, the impact is obvious: fear, anger, exhaustion, a constant sense of being on guard. But White Americans, too, are shaped by the system they inherit. Some learn to ignore or deny the pain of others, to numb themselves to suffering. Others feel guilt or shame, unsure how to respond. Still others become defensive, clinging to the illusion of innocence.

These reactions are not just mental—they are physical. A White person who feels threatened by a conversation about race may notice their heart racing, their muscles tensing, their breath quickening. They may lash out, withdraw, or shut down. These are the body’s ways of coping with discomfort, of protecting itself from pain.

Police officers, often seen as enforcers of the system, are caught in a web of expectations and fears. Many enter the profession with good intentions, but quickly learn to see the world as dangerous. Their bodies become attuned to threat, their minds trained to react quickly. Over time, this constant vigilance takes a toll. Some become hardened, others numb. Many struggle to connect with their own families, unable to turn off the alarm bells that ring in their bodies.

The effects of white supremacy are everywhere, but they are often invisible. Most people don’t notice the ways their bodies react to difference, to conflict, to uncertainty. They don’t see how old patterns—passed down through families, reinforced by society—shape their reactions. But the body knows. It remembers what the mind tries to forget.

Healing requires facing these truths, even when they are uncomfortable. It means noticing the ways white supremacy shows up in daily life—in the way people talk, the way they move, the way they react to others. It means learning to sit with discomfort, to listen to the body’s signals, to respond with care instead of defensiveness.

One man, raised in a family that never talked about race, found himself growing anxious whenever the topic came up. He noticed his jaw tightening, his breath growing shallow, his mind racing to find the right words. At first, he tried to ignore these feelings, to push them away. But over time, he learned to pause, to breathe, to let himself feel the discomfort. Slowly, he became more open, more willing to listen, more able to connect with others.

This is the work of healing—not just for individuals, but for communities. It means creating spaces where people can be honest about their pain, where bodies can relax, where old wounds can begin to mend. It means recognizing that everyone is affected by white supremacy, and that everyone has a role to play in healing.

The process is slow, and it requires courage. It means facing not just personal pain, but the pain of ancestors and the pain of a nation. It means learning to sit with discomfort, to listen to the body’s wisdom, and to trust that healing is possible—even when the wounds are deep.

## Chapter 5 Building New Patterns for Healing

Change rarely happens all at once. Most people want a quick fix, a single solution that will erase years of pain. But healing, especially from racialized trauma, is a slow, layered process. It begins with small steps—moments of awareness, acts of care, new ways of responding to old triggers.

One family, after years of tension and misunderstanding, decided to try something different. Instead of arguing or shutting down, they agreed to pause whenever things got heated. They learned to notice their bodies—tight shoulders, clenched jaws, racing hearts—and to take a few deep breaths before responding. At first, it felt awkward, even silly. But over time, these small pauses made a difference. Arguments became less explosive, misunderstandings less frequent. The family began to feel safer, more connected.

Most people underestimate the power of these small shifts. They want big changes, dramatic breakthroughs. But healing is built on tiny moments of awareness and care. It’s not about erasing the past, but about learning to live with it in a new way.

Communities, too, can create new patterns. Some schools have started teaching mindfulness and emotional awareness, helping students learn to notice and manage their feelings. Some police departments are experimenting with new training methods, focusing on de-escalation and self-awareness. These changes are small, but they add up. Over time, they create a new culture—a culture of care, of awareness, of healing.

The work is ongoing. There will always be setbacks, moments of fear or pain. But each act of care, each moment of awareness, is a step toward healing. Over time, these small acts add up, creating a new sense of safety, a new way of being in the world.

Healing from racialized trauma is not just an individual journey. It’s a collective effort, a process of building new patterns, new stories, new ways of being together. It requires courage, patience, and a willingness to face discomfort. But it is possible. With each step, each breath, each act of care, the cycle of trauma can be broken, and a new legacy can begin.

## Chapter 6 Practices for Settling the Body

There’s a quiet power in learning to settle the body, especially when the world feels unpredictable. For many, the first instinct is to push through discomfort, to ignore the racing heart or the tightness in the chest. But the body keeps score, and over time, these ignored signals build up, making it harder to feel safe or at ease.

A teacher, after a long day of managing restless students, would come home feeling drained and irritable. She noticed her jaw ached and her shoulders felt like stone. One evening, instead of collapsing in front of the TV, she tried something new. She sat quietly, closed her eyes, and placed a hand over her heart. She breathed slowly, feeling the rise and fall of her chest. At first, her mind raced with worries, but as she kept breathing, her body softened. The tension eased, just a little. It wasn’t magic, but it was a start.

Most people don’t realize how much their bodies crave this kind of attention. Simple practices—like deep breathing, humming, or gentle rocking—can signal to the nervous system that it’s safe to relax. These aren’t just relaxation techniques; they’re ways of teaching the body that it doesn’t have to stay on high alert forever.

For those carrying racialized trauma, these practices can feel unfamiliar, even uncomfortable. The body may resist, clinging to old patterns of vigilance. But with patience and repetition, new habits can form. A parent, after years of bracing for the worst, learns to pause before reacting to a child’s outburst. A police officer, used to scanning for threats, tries grounding themselves with slow, steady breaths before stepping out of the car. These small acts of settling don’t erase the past, but they create space for something new.

It became clear that healing isn’t about forcing the body to relax, but about inviting it to feel safe—one breath, one moment at a time. Over weeks and months, these moments add up, slowly shifting the body’s baseline from tension to ease. The world may not change overnight, but the way the body meets the world can.

## Chapter 7 The Power of Collective Healing

Healing doesn’t happen in isolation. While personal practices are important, the deepest wounds are often communal, shaped by shared histories and collective pain. That’s why true healing requires coming together—finding ways to support, witness, and care for one another.

In a small church basement, a group of neighbors gathered every week. Some had lived in the neighborhood for decades; others were new arrivals. At first, the meetings were awkward. People sat with arms crossed, unsure what to say. But as the weeks went by, something shifted. One person shared a story about growing up afraid of the police. Another spoke about feeling guilty for things their ancestors had done. There were tears, silences, and sometimes laughter. The group learned to listen—not to fix or judge, but to witness.

Most people underestimate the power of being seen and heard. When someone shares their pain and others hold space for it, the burden feels lighter. The body relaxes, even if just a little. Over time, trust grows. People begin to show up as their full selves, no longer hiding behind masks of strength or indifference.

Communities that practice collective healing often create rituals—shared meals, songs, or moments of silence. These rituals aren’t just symbolic; they help regulate the nervous system, reminding everyone that they’re not alone. A child who sees adults caring for each other learns that it’s safe to ask for help. An elder who shares a painful memory finds comfort in the presence of others.

It became clear that healing is contagious. When one person begins to heal, it gives others permission to do the same. The ripple effect can transform families, schools, and neighborhoods. But it takes courage to show up, to be vulnerable, to trust that others will meet you with compassion.

## Chapter 8 Navigating Conflict and Repair

No matter how much healing work is done, conflict is inevitable. Old wounds get triggered, misunderstandings happen, and people hurt each other—sometimes without meaning to. What matters most is how these moments are handled.

A father, after snapping at his teenage son during an argument, felt a familiar wave of shame. In the past, he would have withdrawn, hoping the tension would fade on its own. But this time, he remembered what he’d learned about repair. He found his son in the kitchen, took a deep breath, and said, “I’m sorry. I lost my temper. That wasn’t fair to you.” The son shrugged at first, but the father noticed his shoulders drop, just a little. The air between them softened.

Most people don’t realize that repair isn’t about erasing the hurt, but about acknowledging it. When someone takes responsibility for their actions, it helps the other person’s body relax. The nervous system, always scanning for danger, learns that it’s safe to reconnect.

In communities, repair can look like honest conversations, apologies, or changes in behavior. A school principal, after hearing from students about feeling unheard, started holding regular listening sessions. At first, the students were skeptical, but over time, they began to trust that their voices mattered.

It became clear that conflict isn’t a sign of failure, but an opportunity for growth. When handled with care, it can deepen trust and strengthen relationships. The key is to stay present, to listen, and to be willing to make amends.

## Chapter 9 The Role of Culture and Ritual

Culture shapes the way people experience and heal from trauma. For many, rituals—both old and new—offer a sense of belonging and continuity. They remind people of who they are, where they come from, and what they value.

A grandmother, every Sunday, would gather her family for a meal. She’d cook recipes passed down through generations, telling stories as she worked. The food was delicious, but it was the ritual that mattered most. The family knew that, no matter what happened during the week, they had this time together. The ritual became an anchor, a way to settle the body and the heart.

Most people don’t realize how much they rely on these small rituals. Lighting a candle, singing a song, sharing a meal—these acts help regulate the nervous system, creating a sense of safety and connection. For those whose families have been disrupted by trauma, creating new rituals can be especially healing.

Communities, too, can create rituals that foster healing. A neighborhood might hold an annual block party, a school might start each day with a moment of silence, a workplace might celebrate milestones together. These rituals aren’t just about tradition; they’re about creating spaces where people feel seen, valued, and connected.

It became clear that healing isn’t just about letting go of pain, but about building something new—cultures of care, belonging, and resilience. Rituals, both big and small, are the threads that hold these cultures together.

## Chapter 10 Moving Toward Justice and Collective Liberation

Healing the body is deeply personal, but it’s also political. The systems that create and sustain racialized trauma must be challenged if true healing is to take root. This means moving beyond individual change to collective action.

A group of friends, tired of seeing the same injustices play out in their city, decided to get involved. They attended city council meetings, organized community forums, and worked with local leaders to push for change. It was slow, frustrating work. Sometimes it felt like nothing was happening. But over time, they saw small victories—a new policy here, a safer park there. The work wasn’t just about changing laws; it was about changing hearts and minds.

Most people don’t realize that justice work is a form of healing. When communities come together to demand fairness, the body feels it. There’s a sense of agency, of hope, of possibility. The nervous system, so used to bracing for disappointment, learns that change is possible.

But the work is never finished. There will always be setbacks, moments of despair, and resistance from those who benefit from the status quo. The key is to keep going, to find support, and to celebrate every step forward.

It became clear that healing and justice are intertwined. One cannot exist without the other. As individuals and communities heal, they become more capable of imagining and creating a world where everyone can thrive.

## Conclusion

The journey through *My Grandmother’s Hands* is not just a call to understand trauma, but an invitation to transform it—within ourselves, our families, and our communities. The wisdom woven through these stories and practices is both ancient and urgently needed. Healing from racialized trauma is not a solitary path; it is a shared endeavor, built on small acts of courage, care, and connection.

**Key Takeaways:**
- Healing begins with noticing and honoring the body’s signals and needs.
- Small, consistent practices can shift the nervous system toward safety and ease.
- Collective healing is possible when communities gather to witness and support each other.
- Repairing relationships requires presence, honesty, and a willingness to make amends.
- Justice and healing are inseparable; both require ongoing, courageous action.

If you find yourself bracing against the world, know that you are not alone. The patterns you carry are not your fault, but you have the power to change them—one breath, one pause, one act of care at a time. Healing is slow, sometimes frustrating, but always possible. As you practice settling your body and reaching out to others, you become part of a larger movement—one that honors the pain of the past while building a future rooted in justice, belonging, and hope. Trust that your efforts matter, even when progress feels invisible. Every small act of healing is a gift to yourself and to the generations yet to come.
