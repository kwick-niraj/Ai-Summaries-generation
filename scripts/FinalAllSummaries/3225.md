## Chapter 1 Why Rewards and Punishments Feel So Natural—And Why They Backfire

It’s easy to picture a classroom where a teacher, hoping to keep things running smoothly, dangles a promise: “Finish your work, and you’ll get a gold star.” Or a parent, tired after a long day, offers a cookie for a quiet dinner. In offices, managers hand out bonuses for hitting targets, believing this will spark better results. These scenes feel familiar, almost automatic. Most people grow up with the idea that rewards and punishments are the levers that move us—carrots and sticks, as old as childhood itself.

But beneath the surface, something else is happening. The more these incentives are used, the more they seem to shape not just behavior, but the very reasons people do things. A child who once loved drawing now asks, “What do I get if I finish this?” An employee who used to take pride in creative solutions starts to focus only on what will earn the next bonus. The promise of a treat or the threat of a penalty becomes the main reason to act, crowding out curiosity, pride, or joy.

In one school, a teacher tried to encourage reading by giving out prizes for every book finished. At first, the students raced through pages, eager for the next reward. But soon, the questions changed. Instead of talking about the stories, they asked how many books they needed for the next prize. When the rewards stopped, so did the reading. The love of stories, once alive in the room, faded into the background.

This pattern repeats in homes, schools, and workplaces. The logic seems simple: reward what you want, punish what you don’t. But the results are often the opposite of what’s intended. Instead of building lasting motivation, these systems can make people less interested, less creative, and less willing to take risks. The promise of a reward can turn a joyful activity into a chore, and the threat of punishment can breed resentment or fear.

Most people don’t notice this shift right away. It’s subtle, like a slow change in the air. At first, rewards seem to work—behavior improves, tasks get done. But over time, the spark that made the work meaningful starts to dim. The focus moves from the activity itself to the outcome, from the process to the prize. The question becomes not “What can I learn?” but “What do I get?”

This isn’t just a problem for children. In offices, incentive plans are rolled out with fanfare, but soon, employees start to game the system, doing just enough to earn the bonus but no more. Creativity suffers, and teamwork can break down as people compete for limited rewards. The same pattern shows up in families, where praise and treats become tools for control, and in schools, where grades and stickers take center stage.

The trouble is, rewards and punishments don’t just fail to motivate in the long run—they can actually make things worse. They teach people to look outside themselves for reasons to act, rather than finding satisfaction in the work itself. They can undermine relationships, turning cooperation into competition and trust into suspicion. And they can make people less likely to take risks, try new things, or stick with a challenge when things get tough.

It’s tempting to believe that a well-designed system of incentives can solve these problems. Maybe if the rewards are bigger, or the punishments more consistent, things will improve. But the evidence tells a different story. The more people rely on external motivators, the less they develop the internal drive that leads to real growth, learning, and satisfaction.

The real question isn’t how to fine-tune the system, but whether the system itself is the problem. What if the very act of trying to control people with rewards and punishments is what’s holding them back? What if the key to motivation lies not in what’s done to people, but in what’s done with them?

## Chapter 2 The Hidden Costs of Chasing Gold Stars and Bonuses

Imagine a child who once spent hours building elaborate towers out of blocks, lost in the joy of creation. One day, a well-meaning adult offers a sticker for every tower built. At first, the child is delighted—stickers pile up, and towers rise faster than ever. But soon, the focus shifts. The towers become simpler, built quickly to earn the next sticker. The joy of building fades, replaced by the drive to collect rewards.

This story plays out in countless ways. In classrooms, students who once asked questions out of curiosity start to ask, “Will this be on the test?” At home, chores become bargaining chips, with children negotiating for treats or screen time. In offices, workers who once took pride in their craft begin to cut corners, aiming for the metrics that lead to bonuses.

The costs of this shift are easy to overlook. On the surface, things seem to be working—tasks get done, behavior improves. But underneath, something important is lost. The drive to explore, to take risks, to learn for its own sake, starts to wither. People become less willing to try new things, fearing that failure will mean no reward. Creativity suffers, as the safest path becomes the most attractive.

In one experiment, children were asked to draw pictures, an activity they enjoyed. Some were told they would receive a reward for their drawings, while others were not. The children who expected a reward spent less time drawing and produced less creative work. The promise of a prize, instead of sparking more effort, actually dampened their interest and imagination.

The same pattern shows up in adults. In workplaces where bonuses are tied to specific outcomes, employees often focus narrowly on what will earn the reward, ignoring other important aspects of their job. Teamwork can suffer, as people compete for limited incentives. The sense of shared purpose that once brought people together is replaced by a focus on individual gain.

Praise, too, can have hidden costs. When used as a tool to control behavior—“Good job!” for doing what’s expected—it can become just another form of reward. People start to seek approval, rather than doing things because they matter. The need for praise can become a kind of dependency, making it harder to act without external validation.

Most people don’t set out to undermine motivation. The intention is usually positive—to encourage, to support, to guide. But the effect is often the opposite. The more people are rewarded for doing something, the less they want to do it for its own sake. The activity becomes a means to an end, rather than an end in itself.

This isn’t just a problem for individuals. When entire systems are built around rewards and punishments, the culture shifts. In schools, learning becomes about grades and test scores, rather than understanding and growth. In businesses, innovation takes a back seat to short-term results. In families, relationships can become transactional, with love and approval tied to performance.

The real cost of chasing gold stars and bonuses isn’t just lower motivation or creativity. It’s the loss of something deeper—the sense of ownership, pride, and joy that comes from doing something because it matters. When rewards become the goal, the work itself loses its meaning.

## Chapter 3 How Rewards and Punishments Undermine Real Motivation

There’s a moment in every person’s life when the question shifts from “What do I want to do?” to “What will I get if I do it?” For some, it happens early—a parent offers a treat for a clean room, or a teacher hands out stickers for good behavior. For others, it comes later, in the form of grades, bonuses, or praise. But the effect is the same: the focus moves from the activity to the outcome, from the process to the prize.

This shift has deep roots in the way people think about motivation. The common belief is that people need to be pushed or pulled to do what’s right. Without rewards or punishments, the thinking goes, nothing would get done. But this view misses something important—the power of intrinsic motivation, the drive that comes from within.

Intrinsic motivation is what makes a child ask endless questions, or an artist lose track of time while painting. It’s the satisfaction that comes from solving a tough problem, or the pride in a job well done. It’s not about what’s earned, but about what’s experienced. When people act from this place, they don’t need to be controlled—they’re already engaged.

But rewards and punishments can erode this inner drive. When people are given external reasons to act, they start to question their own motives. “Am I doing this because I want to, or because I’ll get something?” Over time, the answer shifts. The activity becomes a means to an end, and the joy of the work fades.

In one study, adults were asked to solve puzzles. Some were paid for each puzzle completed, while others were not. The paid group lost interest in the puzzles once the payments stopped, while the unpaid group continued to work, driven by curiosity and challenge. The promise of a reward, instead of boosting motivation, actually made it more fragile.

The same thing happens with punishments. When people are threatened with consequences for not meeting expectations, they may comply in the short term. But the compliance is often shallow, driven by fear rather than understanding. The focus becomes avoiding punishment, rather than doing what’s right or meaningful.

This dynamic shows up in classrooms, where students work for grades rather than learning, and in workplaces, where employees do just enough to avoid penalties. The result is a culture of compliance, rather than engagement. People do what’s required, but little more. The spark of curiosity, the willingness to take risks, and the drive to improve are all dampened.

Praise, when used as a tool for control, can have the same effect. When people are praised for doing what’s expected, they start to seek approval rather than satisfaction. The need for praise can become a kind of addiction, making it harder to act without external validation. The focus shifts from the work to the reaction, from the process to the outcome.

Most people don’t notice this shift right away. It happens gradually, as the reasons for acting become more about what’s given or taken away, and less about what matters. The result is a loss of autonomy, a sense that someone else is in control. People become less willing to take risks, less open to new ideas, and less likely to stick with a challenge when things get tough.

The real danger of rewards and punishments isn’t just that they fail to motivate in the long run. It’s that they can actually make people less interested, less creative, and less engaged. They teach people to look outside themselves for reasons to act, rather than finding satisfaction in the work itself. The question becomes not “What can I learn?” but “What do I get?”

The path to real motivation lies not in controlling people, but in supporting their autonomy, competence, and connection to others. When people feel ownership over their work, when they’re challenged and supported, and when they feel connected to others, they don’t need to be bribed or threatened. They’re already motivated, from the inside out.

## Chapter 4 The Trap of Manipulative Praise and the Need for Authentic Encouragement

In a busy classroom, a teacher calls out, “Great job!” as a student hands in a worksheet. The words are meant to encourage, to show approval. But over time, the students start to notice a pattern. The praise comes when they do what’s expected, not when they take risks or try something new. The message is clear: do what the teacher wants, and you’ll be rewarded with approval.

At home, a parent claps and cheers when a child finishes their vegetables. The child smiles, but soon starts to expect praise for every bite. The act of eating, once routine, becomes a performance. The child looks for the parent’s reaction, rather than paying attention to hunger or taste.

In offices, managers hand out compliments for meeting targets, hoping to boost morale. But employees quickly learn that the praise is tied to specific outcomes, not to effort or growth. The compliments start to feel hollow, more about control than genuine appreciation.

This kind of praise, used as a tool to shape behavior, can become just another form of reward. It teaches people to seek approval, rather than satisfaction. The need for praise can become a kind of dependency, making it harder to act without external validation. The focus shifts from the work to the reaction, from the process to the outcome.

The problem isn’t with encouragement itself. Everyone needs to feel seen and valued. But when praise is used to control, it loses its power. It becomes a way to manipulate, rather than to support. People start to act for the sake of approval, rather than for the sake of the work.

In one study, children were praised for being “good helpers” when they cleaned up toys. Later, when no one was watching, those who had been praised were less likely to help again. The praise, instead of building a habit of helping, actually made it less likely. The children had learned to act for the sake of approval, not for the sake of helping.

The same thing happens with adults. When praise is tied to specific outcomes, people start to focus on what will earn approval, rather than on what matters. The need for praise can become a kind of addiction, making it harder to act without external validation. The focus shifts from the work to the reaction, from the process to the outcome.

Authentic encouragement is different. It’s not about controlling behavior, but about supporting growth. It’s about noticing effort, progress, and learning, rather than just outcomes. It’s about asking questions, listening, and showing interest, rather than handing out compliments. When people feel seen and valued for who they are, not just for what they do, they’re more likely to take risks, try new things, and stick with challenges.

The path to real motivation lies not in manipulating people with praise, but in building relationships based on trust, respect, and support. When people feel ownership over their work, when they’re challenged and supported, and when they feel connected to others, they don’t need to be bribed or threatened. They’re already motivated, from the inside out.

When people feel ownership over their work, when they’re challenged and supported, and when they feel connected to others, they don’t need to be bribed or threatened. ## Chapter 6 Rethinking Schools: From Compliance to Curiosity

It’s a familiar scene: a teacher stands at the front of the room, doling out points for correct answers and warnings for missed homework. The students, eyes on the scoreboard, learn quickly what matters most. But in a different kind of classroom, something else is happening. Here, the teacher sits among the students, asking what they’re curious about, inviting them to help shape the day’s lesson. The room feels different—less tense, more alive.

For many educators, the idea of letting go of rewards and punishments feels risky. There’s a fear that without these tools, chaos will follow. But in schools that have made the shift, the results are often surprising. One principal, tired of endless detentions and sticker charts, decided to try something new. She asked teachers to focus on building relationships and giving students more say in their learning. At first, there was uncertainty—students tested boundaries, and teachers worried about losing control. But over time, something changed. Students started to take more responsibility, not just for their work, but for the classroom community. They asked more questions, took more risks, and helped each other learn.

In these classrooms, mistakes aren’t punished—they’re seen as part of the process. When a student struggles, the teacher doesn’t offer a prize for improvement or a penalty for falling behind. Instead, they ask, “What do you think would help?” or “How can we work through this together?” The focus shifts from getting it right to figuring it out. Students learn to trust themselves and each other.

It became clear that when students feel ownership over their learning, they don’t need to be pushed or pulled. They’re already motivated, driven by curiosity and a sense of belonging. The teacher’s role shifts from enforcer to guide, from judge to partner. The classroom becomes a place where learning is something you do, not something that’s done to you.

Most people don’t realize how much the structure of school shapes motivation. When grades, prizes, and punishments are the main tools, students learn to play the game. But when the focus is on questions, exploration, and collaboration, they learn to love learning itself. The difference is subtle, but profound.

The challenge isn’t just to remove rewards and punishments, but to build something better in their place. That means creating classrooms where students have real choices, where their voices matter, and where mistakes are seen as opportunities. It means trusting students to care about their own learning, and supporting them as they figure out what matters to them.

## Chapter 7 Parenting Without Bribes or Threats

At the end of a long day, it’s tempting for a parent to reach for the quickest solution: “If you clean your room, you can have dessert.” Or, “If you don’t stop fighting, you’ll lose your screen time.” These bargains and threats can feel like the only way to get through the evening. But over time, something shifts. The child starts to ask, “What do I get if I do it?” or “What happens if I don’t?” The relationship becomes a series of transactions, and the joy of being together gets lost in the negotiations.

Some parents, exhausted by this cycle, decide to try a different approach. Instead of rewards and punishments, they focus on connection and understanding. When a child refuses to do a chore, the parent doesn’t threaten or bribe. Instead, they sit down and ask, “What’s going on?” or “How can we work together to solve this?” The conversation changes. The child feels heard, and the parent learns more about what’s really going on.

In one family, a parent noticed that their child was dragging their feet at bedtime. Instead of offering a treat for getting ready quickly, the parent asked, “What makes bedtime hard for you?” The child admitted to feeling lonely after lights out. Together, they came up with a new routine—reading together for a few extra minutes. The struggle faded, replaced by a sense of closeness.

It became clear that when children feel respected and involved, they’re more willing to cooperate. The focus shifts from compliance to connection, from control to collaboration. The parent’s role isn’t to manage behavior, but to build a relationship where both people feel valued.

This doesn’t mean letting children do whatever they want. Boundaries and expectations still matter. But instead of using power to enforce them, parents can invite children into the process. “What do you think is fair?” or “How can we make this work for everyone?” These questions open the door to problem-solving and empathy.

Most people don’t realize how much children crave a sense of agency. When they’re trusted to help make decisions, they rise to the challenge. The need for bribes and threats fades, replaced by a sense of partnership. The home becomes a place where everyone’s needs matter, and where love isn’t tied to performance.

The real reward is a relationship built on trust, respect, and understanding. When children feel seen and heard, they don’t need to be bribed or threatened. They’re already motivated to do their part, not for a prize, but for the sake of the family.

## Chapter 9 The Power of Questions and Conversations

There’s a quiet strength in asking the right question at the right time. In a classroom, a teacher might pause and ask, “What do you notice about this problem?” In a family, a parent might wonder aloud, “How do you feel about what happened today?” In an office, a manager might say, “What would help you do your best work?” These questions open doors that rewards and punishments can never unlock.

For many, the habit of telling—of giving instructions, feedback, or praise—comes more easily than the habit of asking. But when people are invited to reflect, to share their thoughts and feelings, something shifts. They start to take ownership, to see themselves as active participants rather than passive recipients.

In one school, a teacher replaced her usual praise with curiosity. Instead of saying, “Good job!” she asked, “What part of this project are you most proud of?” The students lit up, eager to share their thinking. They started to notice their own growth, rather than waiting for approval.

At home, a parent tried a new approach when her child was upset about a lost game. Instead of offering a reward for calming down, she asked, “What would help you feel better right now?” The child thought for a moment, then suggested a walk together. The moment became an opportunity for connection, not just compliance.

In the workplace, a manager noticed that her team was struggling with a new project. Instead of offering a bonus for meeting the deadline, she asked, “What’s getting in the way?” The team opened up about their challenges, and together they found solutions. The project moved forward, and the team felt stronger.

It became clear that questions can do what rewards and punishments cannot—they invite reflection, foster ownership, and build relationships. They show respect for people’s ideas and experiences, and create space for growth.

Most people don’t realize how powerful a simple question can be. When the focus shifts from telling to asking, from controlling to collaborating, motivation comes from within. The need for bribes and threats fades, replaced by curiosity and connection.

The real work is to build habits of conversation, to listen as much as we speak, and to trust that people have the capacity to grow when given the chance. In classrooms, homes, and workplaces, the power of questions can transform the way we motivate and support each other.
