## Introduction

The persistent legacy of white supremacy shapes societies, relationships, and personal identities in ways both overt and subtle. In Me and White Supremacy: Combat Racism, Change the World, and Become a Good Ancestor, Layla F. Saad invites a deep and honest reckoning with the ways privilege, bias, and complicity operate in everyday life. The journey begins with a call to self-examination, challenging the comfort of silence and the illusion of neutrality. Through reflection and accountability, the roots of racism are exposed—not as distant historical artifacts, but as living realities that demand conscious unlearning and active resistance.

Transformation unfolds through courageous self-inquiry and the willingness to confront uncomfortable truths. The process is not about guilt or shame, but about cultivating awareness, empathy, and responsibility. As the layers of internalized beliefs are peeled back, the possibility of genuine change emerges—change that ripples outward, influencing communities and future generations. The path toward becoming a good ancestor is illuminated by the commitment to anti-racism, the courage to disrupt harmful patterns, and the hope of building a more just and compassionate world.

## Chapter 1 Understanding the Foundations of White Supremacy and Privilege

The journey toward antiracism begins with a clear-eyed look at the world as it is, not as one wishes it to be. At the heart of this process lies the recognition that white supremacy is not just a matter of individual prejudice or overt acts of hatred. Instead, it is a deeply embedded system—a set of beliefs, practices, and social structures—that shapes daily life, often in ways that go unnoticed by those who benefit from it.

White supremacy, in this context, refers to the societal arrangement that places white people and their cultural norms at the center, granting them unearned advantages while marginalizing people of color. This system is not always visible in explicit acts; it often operates quietly, woven into laws, institutions, and everyday interactions. It is like the air in a room—unseen but always present, influencing how people move, speak, and relate to one another.

A crucial concept here is privilege. Privilege is the set of unearned benefits and immunities that come from being part of a dominant group. For those who are white, this privilege can be so normalized that it feels invisible. It is the freedom to move through the world without being questioned, stereotyped, or threatened because of skin color. Recognizing privilege is not about assigning blame for historical wrongs, but about acknowledging the reality of unequal starting points and the ongoing impact of these disparities.

To make this more tangible, consider a simple analogy: imagine a game where some players start with extra resources, better equipment, and fewer obstacles. Over time, these advantages compound, making it easier for them to succeed, even if they are unaware of the head start. The game is not fair, but those with the advantage may not notice the imbalance unless they pause to reflect.

This foundational understanding sets the stage for deeper self-examination. It asks individuals to move beyond defensiveness and to see how their own lives are shaped by systems larger than themselves. The process is not about guilt, but about responsibility—the willingness to see clearly and to act with integrity.

Key ideas introduced at this stage include:

- **White supremacy as a system:** Not just individual acts, but a network of beliefs, policies, and cultural norms that prioritize whiteness.
- **Privilege as invisibility:** The ease of not noticing the advantages one holds, and the importance of making the invisible visible.
- **Discomfort as a sign of growth:** Feeling uneasy when confronting these realities is natural and can be a sign that important learning is taking place.

This first step is about opening the door to honest reflection. It invites readers to set aside defensiveness and to approach the topic with curiosity and humility, recognizing that understanding is the first step toward meaningful change.

## Chapter 2 Examining Internalized Beliefs and Everyday Complicity

With the groundwork laid, the next phase involves turning inward to examine the beliefs, assumptions, and habits that have been absorbed over a lifetime. This is not a process of self-accusation, but of honest inventory—an exploration of how white supremacy has shaped thoughts, feelings, and behaviors, often without conscious intent.

Internalized white supremacy refers to the ways in which societal messages about race become part of one’s own worldview. These messages are absorbed from family, media, education, and culture. They can show up as subtle preferences, unspoken fears, or automatic judgments. For example, someone might notice a tendency to feel safer around people who look like them, or to assume that certain neighborhoods are “better” without questioning why.

Complicity is another key concept here. It is the recognition that benefiting from an unjust system, even passively, contributes to its continuation. This does not mean that every individual act is malicious, but that inaction and silence allow harmful patterns to persist. Complicity can look like laughing at a racist joke to avoid awkwardness, or staying silent when witnessing discrimination.

To make this more relatable, imagine a river polluted upstream. Even those who live downstream and do not add to the pollution still drink the water and are affected by its quality. Similarly, living in a society shaped by white supremacy means that everyone is touched by its effects, whether or not they actively contribute to it.

This stage encourages a practice of self-interrogation, asking questions such as:

- When have I benefited from being part of the dominant group, even if I did not ask for it?
- What stereotypes or assumptions have I internalized about people of other races?
- How have I responded when confronted with racism in my environment?

Practical tools for this reflection include:

- **Journaling prompts:** Writing about personal experiences with race, privilege, and discomfort.
- **Noticing patterns:** Paying attention to who is included or excluded in social circles, workplaces, and media.
- **Seeking feedback:** Being open to hearing from people of color about how one’s actions or words may have caused harm, even unintentionally.

This process is not about perfection, but about progress. It is about building the muscle of self-awareness, so that automatic responses can be replaced with conscious choices. The goal is to move from passive complicity to active responsibility, recognizing that small, everyday actions matter.

## Chapter 3 Confronting Barriers: Fragility, Denial, and Defensive Reactions

As self-examination deepens, it is common to encounter internal resistance. This resistance often takes the form of white fragility—a set of defensive behaviors that arise when one’s understanding of self as “good” or “not racist” is challenged. Fragility can manifest as anger, withdrawal, guilt, or a need to prove one’s innocence. It is a way of protecting oneself from discomfort, but it also serves to shut down important conversations.

Denial is another frequent barrier. It can show up as minimizing the problem (“It’s not that bad”), rationalizing (“I didn’t mean it that way”), or shifting the focus (“What about reverse racism?”). These responses are understandable, but they prevent growth by keeping the status quo intact.

To illustrate, consider a scenario where someone is told that a comment they made was hurtful. Instead of listening, they become defensive, insisting that their intentions were good. This reaction centers their own feelings and derails the conversation, making it harder for real understanding to occur.

Other common barriers include:

- **Tone policing:** Focusing on how a message is delivered rather than its content, often dismissing valid concerns because they are expressed with emotion.
- **Color blindness:** Claiming not to “see” race, which ignores the real impact of racism and erases the experiences of people of color.
- **Performative allyship:** Taking visible but shallow actions to appear antiracist, without engaging in deeper change or risk.

These barriers are not unique to any one person; they are learned responses that serve to protect comfort and avoid accountability. Recognizing them is a crucial step, because it allows individuals to pause, reflect, and choose a different path.

Practical strategies for moving through these barriers include:

- **Sitting with discomfort:** Allowing oneself to feel uneasy without rushing to fix or explain it away.
- **Listening deeply:** Prioritizing the experiences and perspectives of people of color, even when it is hard to hear.
- **Taking responsibility:** Focusing on impact rather than intent, and being willing to apologize and make amends when harm is caused.

This stage is about building resilience—the ability to stay present and engaged, even when the process is challenging. It is about shifting from a focus on personal comfort to a commitment to justice, recognizing that growth often requires moving through discomfort.

## Chapter 4 Moving from Awareness to Accountability and Action

Awareness alone is not enough to create change. The next step is to translate understanding into action, both at the personal and collective level. This involves taking responsibility for one’s role in upholding or challenging white supremacy, and making concrete choices that align with antiracist values.

Accountability is the practice of holding oneself answerable for the impact of one’s actions, not just intentions. It means being willing to acknowledge mistakes, learn from feedback, and make changes. Accountability is not about self-punishment, but about integrity—the alignment of values and behavior.

To make this practical, consider the difference between a bystander and an upstander. A bystander notices injustice but does nothing, while an upstander intervenes, speaks out, or supports those who are harmed. Becoming an upstander requires courage and a willingness to take risks, but it is essential for disrupting harmful patterns.

Key elements of accountability and action include:

- **Ongoing self-reflection:** Regularly examining one’s beliefs, behaviors, and impact on others.
- **Seeking education:** Taking responsibility for learning about racism, privilege, and history, rather than relying on people of color to provide explanations.
- **Speaking up:** Challenging racist comments, jokes, or policies in personal and professional settings.
- **Supporting change:** Using one’s resources—time, money, influence—to support antiracist organizations, policies, and leaders.

Accountability also involves recognizing that mistakes are inevitable. What matters is the willingness to learn, apologize, and do better. This process is not linear; it requires patience, humility, and persistence.

To support this journey, practical tools might include:

- **Accountability partners:** Connecting with others who are committed to antiracism, to share progress, challenges, and encouragement.
- **Action plans:** Setting specific, measurable goals for learning, advocacy, and support.
- **Reflection check-ins:** Regularly reviewing progress and adjusting actions as needed.

This stage is about moving from intention to impact. It is about making antiracism a lived practice, not just a set of beliefs. The goal is to become someone who not only understands the problem, but who is actively working to create a more just and equitable world.

## Chapter 5 Building Lifelong Antiracist Habits and Considering Legacy

The final step in this part of the journey is to recognize that antiracism is not a destination, but a lifelong practice. It is not something to be checked off a list, but a way of being that requires ongoing attention, learning, and adaptation.

Building antiracist habits means integrating reflection, accountability, and action into daily life. It involves staying curious, open, and willing to be challenged. It also means recognizing that the work is never finished—there will always be more to learn, more to unlearn, and more to do.

A key aspect of this stage is considering legacy. The choices made today shape the world that future generations will inherit. Becoming a “good ancestor” means acting with an awareness of the long-term impact of one’s actions, and striving to leave a more just and equitable world for those who come after.

Practical ways to build lifelong habits include:

- **Continuous learning:** Reading, listening, and engaging with diverse perspectives on race and justice.
- **Community involvement:** Participating in groups, organizations, or movements that are working for change.
- **Mentoring and supporting others:** Sharing knowledge, resources, and encouragement with those who are newer to the journey.
- **Reflecting on legacy:** Regularly asking, “What kind of world do I want to help create? What will I be remembered for?”

This stage is about sustaining momentum and avoiding complacency. It is about recognizing that antiracism is a collective effort, and that individual actions, when multiplied, can create powerful change.

The journey outlined so far is not easy, but it is necessary. It calls for honesty, courage, and compassion—for oneself and for others. By committing to this ongoing process, individuals can help dismantle the systems that perpetuate injustice, and contribute to a world where everyone can thrive.

## Chapter 6 Deepening Collective Responsibility: Community, Relationships, and Shared Power

With a foundation of self-awareness and personal accountability, the next step is to look outward—toward the communities and relationships that shape daily life. Antiracism is not a solitary pursuit; it is most powerful when practiced in connection with others. This chapter explores how to move from individual change to collective responsibility, recognizing that transformation happens in the spaces between people as much as within them.

The principle here is that white supremacy is upheld not just by individuals, but by groups—families, workplaces, schools, faith communities, and neighborhoods. These are the places where norms are set, behaviors are modeled, and values are reinforced. To create lasting change, it is necessary to engage these communities, challenging old patterns and building new ones together.

A helpful metaphor is that of a garden: one person can tend their own plot, but the health of the whole garden depends on everyone working together—pulling weeds, planting seeds, and sharing the harvest. In the same way, antiracist work flourishes when it is rooted in shared effort and mutual support.

Practical ways to foster collective responsibility include:

- **Initiating conversations:** Gently bringing up topics of race and equity in group settings, even when it feels uncomfortable.
- **Modeling vulnerability:** Sharing personal reflections and mistakes, which invites others to do the same.
- **Building alliances:** Connecting with others who are committed to justice, and forming networks of support and accountability.
- **Redistributing power:** Actively seeking out and amplifying the voices of people of color in decision-making spaces.

For example, in a workplace, this might look like advocating for more inclusive hiring practices, or supporting colleagues who raise concerns about bias. In a family, it could mean discussing the history of racism with children, or challenging relatives who make harmful comments.

The key is to remember that change is rarely immediate or easy. Resistance is natural, and setbacks are part of the process. What matters is the willingness to stay engaged, to listen deeply, and to keep moving forward together.

## Chapter 7 Navigating Mistakes, Repair, and Recommitment

No journey of growth is free from missteps. In antiracist practice, mistakes are inevitable—words will be misspoken, actions will fall short, and harm may be caused despite good intentions. This chapter focuses on how to respond when things go wrong, emphasizing the importance of repair and recommitment.

The guiding principle is that mistakes are not the end of the story; they are opportunities for learning and deeper connection. The real test is not whether one avoids error, but how one responds when it happens.

A useful analogy is that of learning a new language: at first, mistakes are frequent and sometimes embarrassing, but each one is a chance to improve. The same is true for antiracism—missteps are part of the process, not a reason to give up.

When harm occurs, the following steps can help guide the process of repair:

- **Acknowledge the impact:** Focus on how actions affected others, rather than defending intent.
- **Apologize sincerely:** Offer a genuine apology without excuses or justifications.
- **Listen and learn:** Be open to feedback, and seek to understand what went wrong.
- **Make amends:** Take concrete steps to address the harm and prevent it in the future.
- **Recommit to the work:** Use the experience as motivation to deepen understanding and action.

For instance, if someone is told that a comment they made was offensive, the first impulse might be to explain or minimize. Instead, pausing to listen, apologizing, and asking how to do better can turn a painful moment into a bridge for trust and growth.

This chapter also emphasizes the importance of self-compassion. Shame and guilt can be paralyzing, but they are not productive. The goal is to stay engaged, to keep learning, and to remember that the work of antiracism is a lifelong journey, not a test of perfection.

## Chapter 8 Sustaining Momentum: Avoiding Burnout and Cultivating Resilience

As the work deepens, it is common to encounter fatigue, discouragement, or a sense of overwhelm. The scale of injustice can feel daunting, and the process of change is often slow. This chapter addresses the need for sustainability—how to maintain energy, hope, and commitment over the long haul.

The central idea is that antiracist practice is a marathon, not a sprint. Just as athletes need rest, nourishment, and support to keep going, so too do those engaged in social change. Burnout is a real risk, especially for those who are new to the work or who take on too much at once.

To sustain momentum, consider these practices:

- **Pace yourself:** Set realistic goals and recognize that change takes time.
- **Celebrate progress:** Acknowledge small wins and moments of growth, both personal and collective.
- **Seek community:** Connect with others who share your values, to share burdens and encouragement.
- **Practice self-care:** Attend to your own emotional, physical, and spiritual needs, so you can show up fully for others.
- **Stay connected to purpose:** Remind yourself why this work matters, and what kind of world you hope to help create.

A real-life example might be someone who, after a period of intense activism, feels exhausted and disillusioned. Rather than withdrawing completely, they might take a step back to rest, reflect, and reconnect with supportive friends. This allows them to return to the work with renewed energy and perspective.

Resilience is not about never feeling tired or discouraged; it is about finding ways to recover, adapt, and keep going. By caring for oneself and for each other, it is possible to sustain the work for the long term.

## Chapter 9 Engaging Systems and Institutions: Advocacy, Policy, and Structural Change

While personal and community transformation are essential, the roots of white supremacy run deep in the structures and institutions of society. This chapter explores how to engage with these larger systems, using advocacy and policy change to address the root causes of inequality.

The principle here is that individual actions, while important, are not enough to dismantle systemic racism. Laws, policies, and institutional practices must also be challenged and changed. This requires moving beyond personal relationships to engage with the broader forces that shape society.

A helpful metaphor is that of a river: while it is important to help those struggling in the current, it is also necessary to go upstream and address the source of the pollution. In the same way, antiracist work must include efforts to change the systems that produce and perpetuate injustice.

Practical steps for engaging systems include:

- **Educating yourself about policy:** Learn how laws and institutional practices create and maintain racial disparities.
- **Advocating for change:** Support policies and leaders that promote equity and justice.
- **Participating in civic life:** Vote, attend meetings, and use your voice to influence decisions.
- **Holding institutions accountable:** Demand transparency, fairness, and representation in organizations you are part of.
- **Supporting grassroots movements:** Amplify the work of those most affected by injustice, and follow their leadership.

For example, this might look like joining a local campaign to reform policing, advocating for equitable funding in schools, or supporting efforts to increase diversity in leadership positions.

The key is to recognize that systems are made up of people, and that collective action can create powerful change. By working together to challenge unjust structures, it is possible to create a society that is more fair and inclusive for all.

## Chapter 10 Embodying Antiracism: Everyday Practice and Becoming a Good Ancestor

The final chapter brings the journey full circle, returning to the idea of legacy and the ongoing practice of antiracism. The work is not about achieving a final state of “wokeness,” but about living in alignment with values of justice, compassion, and equity every day.

The guiding principle is that antiracism is a way of being, not just a set of actions. It is about showing up, again and again, with humility, courage, and a willingness to learn. It is about making choices—large and small—that reflect a commitment to a more just world.

To embody antiracism in daily life:

- **Stay curious:** Continue to seek out new perspectives and challenge your own assumptions.
- **Practice humility:** Recognize that there is always more to learn, and be open to correction.
- **Act with integrity:** Align your actions with your values, even when it is difficult.
- **Support others:** Encourage and mentor those who are newer to the work, and share resources generously.
- **Reflect on your legacy:** Regularly ask how your choices are shaping the world for future generations.

A real-life example might be someone who, after years of learning and activism, continues to listen, adapt, and support others—never seeing themselves as “finished,” but always as a work in progress.

The journey of antiracism is ongoing, and the impact of one’s actions may not always be visible or immediate. But by committing to the practice, day after day, it is possible to help create a world where everyone can thrive.

## Conclusion

The path of antiracism is not a straight line, nor is it a solitary endeavor. It is a lifelong practice that calls for honesty, courage, and compassion—toward oneself and others. The steps outlined here offer a framework for moving from awareness to action, from individual reflection to collective transformation, and from momentary discomfort to sustained change.

**Key takeaways for real-world practice:**

- Recognize white supremacy as a system, not just individual prejudice or bias.
- Examine internalized beliefs and everyday complicity with honesty and humility.
- Move through discomfort, fragility, and denial to build resilience and accountability.
- Translate awareness into concrete action, both personally and within communities.
- Engage with systems and institutions to address the root causes of inequality.
- Sustain momentum by practicing self-care, seeking support, and celebrating progress.
- Embrace mistakes as opportunities for repair, learning, and recommitment.
- Embody antiracism as a daily practice, shaping a legacy of justice and equity.

Remember, this work is not about achieving perfection or earning a badge of virtue. It is about showing up, again and again, with a willingness to see clearly, to listen deeply, and to act with integrity. There will be moments of discomfort, fatigue, and doubt—but these are signs of growth, not failure. Each step, no matter how small, contributes to a larger movement for justice. By tending to your own learning, supporting others, and engaging with the world around you, you help to create ripples of change that extend far beyond your own life. The journey is long, but you are not alone. With patience, persistence, and compassion, it is possible to become not just a witness to history, but an active participant in building a more just and loving world.
