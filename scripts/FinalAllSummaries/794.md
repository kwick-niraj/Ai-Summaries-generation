## Introduction

Across the landscape of American education, stark contrasts emerge between schools separated by little more than a few miles. Savage Inequalities: Children in America's Schools by Jonathan Kozol brings into sharp focus the deep chasms that divide the educational experiences of children based on the neighborhoods in which they live. The realities of underfunded classrooms, crumbling facilities, and limited resources stand in jarring opposition to the privilege and abundance found in wealthier districts. These disparities are not merely about budgets or buildings—they reflect a broader societal neglect and a persistent failure to address the roots of systemic inequality.

Within this world, the daily lives of students and teachers are shaped by forces far beyond their control, revealing how race, class, and geography intertwine to determine opportunity and hope. The stories and observations woven throughout highlight the resilience and aspirations of children who strive for more, even as they confront barriers that seem insurmountable. The journey through these schools is not just an exploration of policy or statistics, but a powerful testament to the enduring impact of inequality on the youngest members of society, setting the stage for a deeper understanding of what is at stake when educational justice is denied.

---

## Chapter 1 The Uneven Starting Line: Entering America’s Divided Schools

It’s easy to imagine that all children in a country as wealthy as the United States would begin their school years with similar hopes and opportunities. Yet, stepping into the world of public education, the reality quickly unravels that assumption. The first day of school, for some, means walking into a bright, well-equipped classroom, sunlight streaming through clean windows, shelves lined with new books, and teachers who have the time and resources to nurture every question. For others, the same day begins with a walk past crumbling buildings, through metal detectors, into overcrowded rooms where supplies are scarce and hope feels rationed.

In cities like East St. Louis, the contrast is especially sharp. Here, the air is thick with the smell of nearby factories and sewage plants. The streets are pocked with potholes, and the schools themselves seem to bear the weight of neglect. A child might sit at a desk with a broken leg, trying to focus while rainwater drips from a stained ceiling. The textbooks are battered, sometimes decades old, and the teachers—though often deeply committed—are stretched thin, managing classes that spill over with students.

A parent in this neighborhood, juggling multiple jobs, worries about sending their child to a school where the bathrooms don’t always work and the playground is a patch of cracked asphalt. The sense of being left behind is not just a feeling; it’s a daily reality. The children notice, too. They see the difference when they watch television or hear stories from relatives in other towns. Some ask why their school looks so different, why the paint peels and the heat doesn’t work in winter.

Travel just a few miles away, and the scene changes dramatically. In a nearby suburb, the school grounds are lush and green. The buildings are modern, the hallways quiet and orderly. Here, students have access to computers, art supplies, and a wide range of extracurricular activities. The teachers have manageable class sizes and the support of well-funded programs. Parents in these communities expect—and receive—a high standard of education for their children.

The gap between these two worlds is not an accident. It’s the result of a system that ties school funding to local property taxes. In neighborhoods where homes are valuable and businesses thrive, schools benefit from a steady stream of resources. In places where poverty is entrenched and property values are low, schools struggle to provide even the basics. This system, though rarely discussed in everyday conversation, quietly shapes the futures of millions of children.

Most people don’t see these differences up close. They might hear about “bad schools” or “troubled districts” on the news, but the day-to-day reality is far more personal and painful. For the children who grow up in underfunded schools, the message is clear: their education—and by extension, their lives—are valued less. The inequalities are not hidden; they are written into the very walls of the schools themselves.

As the school year unfolds, the consequences of these disparities become more apparent. Students in struggling districts face constant obstacles: outdated materials, frequent teacher turnover, and a lack of enrichment opportunities. The sense of being shortchanged seeps into their sense of self-worth. Meanwhile, their peers in wealthier areas are encouraged to dream big, supported by a system that seems designed for their success.

The divide is not just about money. It’s about the message that some children are worth more than others. This realization, once seen, is hard to forget. It lingers in the mind, raising uncomfortable questions about fairness, justice, and the true meaning of equal opportunity.

## Chapter 2 The Roots of Inequality: How School Funding Deepens the Divide

Looking beneath the surface, the reasons for these stark differences in school quality become clearer. The way public schools are funded in the United States is both simple and devastating in its effects. Most of the money for schools comes from local property taxes. This means that communities with expensive homes and thriving businesses can pour money into their schools, while those with low property values are left scrambling for scraps.

In practice, this system locks in inequality. A child born in a poor neighborhood is almost guaranteed to attend an underfunded school, no matter how hard their parents work or how much they care about education. The schools in these areas often have to make impossible choices: fix the leaking roof or buy new science equipment, hire another teacher or keep the library open after school. There is never enough to go around.

The numbers tell a stark story. In some districts, spending per student is double or even triple that of neighboring areas. This isn’t just about nicer buildings or more field trips. It’s about the basics: enough teachers to keep class sizes reasonable, up-to-date textbooks, safe and clean facilities, and access to counselors and special education services. In wealthier districts, these things are taken for granted. In poorer ones, they are luxuries.

The impact of this funding gap is felt most acutely in cities with large populations of Black and Latino children. Decades of housing discrimination and economic segregation have concentrated poverty in certain neighborhoods, and the schools there reflect the hardships of the community. The children who attend these schools are often aware, even at a young age, that they are being shortchanged. They see the difference when they visit other schools for sports or academic competitions. The sense of unfairness is palpable.

A teacher in a struggling district might spend their own money on classroom supplies, knowing that the school budget won’t stretch far enough. They might stay late to help students who are falling behind, but the challenges are overwhelming. The building itself can feel like an enemy—cold in the winter, stifling in the summer, with broken windows and unreliable plumbing. The message to students is clear: their education is not a priority.

Meanwhile, in affluent suburbs, the schools are showcases of what is possible when resources are abundant. Students have access to advanced classes, well-stocked libraries, and a wide range of extracurricular activities. The schools are clean, safe, and welcoming. Parents are involved, not just because they care, but because they have the time and energy to participate. The children here are encouraged to aim high, and the system is designed to support their ambitions.

The contrast is not lost on anyone who spends time in both types of schools. The differences are not just in the physical environment, but in the expectations and opportunities available to students. In underfunded schools, the focus is often on survival—getting through the day, keeping order, and doing the best with what little is available. In well-funded schools, the focus is on growth, exploration, and achievement.

This system of funding, though rarely questioned by those who benefit from it, perpetuates a cycle of inequality. Children who start out with less are given fewer opportunities to succeed, making it harder for them to break out of poverty. The promise of equal opportunity, so often repeated in American life, rings hollow in the face of such disparities.

The roots of this system run deep. Attempts to reform school funding have often been met with resistance, especially from those who fear losing the advantages they have come to expect. The result is a patchwork of laws and policies that do little to address the underlying problem. For the children caught in the middle, the consequences are all too real.

## Chapter 3 Life Inside Underfunded Schools: Struggles, Hopes, and Daily Realities

Walking through the halls of an underfunded school, the challenges are impossible to ignore. The walls are often marked by years of wear, the floors scuffed and uneven. In some classrooms, the heat doesn’t work in winter, and the air conditioning is broken in the summer. The bathrooms are sometimes out of order, and the playground is little more than a fenced-in patch of dirt.

For the students, these conditions are a daily reminder of what they lack. A child might try to concentrate on a math lesson while shivering in a drafty room, or struggle to read from a textbook that is missing pages. The teachers do their best, but the obstacles are relentless. Supplies run out quickly, and there is never enough money for field trips or special projects.

One day, a group of students gathers in the cafeteria, their voices echoing in the large, mostly empty space. They talk about their dreams—becoming doctors, teachers, or engineers—but the path ahead seems uncertain. They know that their school doesn’t offer the advanced classes or college prep programs available in other districts. Some have never been on a real science field trip or used a computer in class.

A teacher, exhausted but determined, stays after school to help a student who is struggling with reading. The teacher knows that this child is bright and capable, but the lack of resources makes it hard to provide the support needed. The teacher worries about burnout, about the students who slip through the cracks, about the future.

Parents, too, feel the strain. Many work long hours at low-wage jobs, doing their best to support their children’s education. They attend school meetings when they can, but often feel powerless to change the system. Some worry about safety, about the influence of gangs or drugs, about the lack of after-school programs to keep their children engaged and out of trouble.

Despite these challenges, there is a sense of resilience in the community. Students form close bonds with each other and with the teachers who care for them. They celebrate small victories—a good grade, a successful school play, a sports team that makes it to the playoffs. These moments of joy are hard-won, and they matter all the more because of the obstacles faced.

Still, the sense of being left behind is hard to shake. When students from these schools visit wealthier districts for competitions or events, the differences are glaring. The other schools have new equipment, spacious gyms, and a sense of order and calm. The students from underfunded schools notice the looks they get, the subtle ways they are made to feel out of place.

The impact of these conditions goes beyond academics. It shapes the way students see themselves and their place in the world. Some internalize the message that they are less deserving, that their dreams are out of reach. Others become angry or withdrawn, acting out in ways that reflect their frustration and sense of injustice.

The teachers, for their part, do what they can to shield their students from the harshest realities. They create moments of warmth and encouragement, trying to instill a sense of hope. But the limits of what they can do are always present, a constant reminder of the larger forces at work.

## Chapter 4 The Other Side: Privilege and Opportunity in Wealthy Districts

A short drive from the struggling schools of the inner city, a different world unfolds. Here, the school buildings are modern and well-maintained, surrounded by manicured lawns and playgrounds filled with new equipment. The classrooms are bright and spacious, with enough desks for every student and shelves lined with the latest books and technology.

The students in these schools arrive each morning with a sense of security. They know that their teachers are well-trained and supported, that their classrooms will be warm in winter and cool in summer. They have access to a wide range of programs—music, art, sports, and advanced academic courses. The school day is filled with opportunities to explore, to create, to learn.

Parents in these communities are deeply involved in their children’s education. They volunteer in classrooms, organize fundraisers, and advocate for new programs. Many have the time and resources to support their children’s learning at home, providing books, computers, and enrichment activities. The expectation is that every child will succeed, and the system is designed to make that possible.

A student in this environment might spend the afternoon in a well-equipped science lab, conducting experiments with the latest technology. Later, they might join a club or sports team, or work on a project in the school’s media center. The possibilities seem endless, and the support is always there.

The teachers, too, benefit from the abundance of resources. They have manageable class sizes, access to professional development, and the support of counselors and specialists. They can focus on teaching, knowing that their students’ basic needs are met. The atmosphere is one of calm and order, with high expectations and a sense of shared purpose.

The contrast with underfunded schools is stark. Here, students are encouraged to dream big, to set ambitious goals, and to believe that anything is possible. The path to college and career is clear, with guidance counselors and mentors to help every step of the way. The sense of possibility is woven into the fabric of the school.

Yet, beneath the surface, there is an awareness—sometimes unspoken—of the advantages that come with privilege. Some students notice the differences when they visit other schools or hear stories from friends in less fortunate districts. They may feel a sense of guilt or confusion, wondering why the world is so uneven.

For the most part, though, the inequalities remain invisible to those who benefit from them. The system works as intended, providing a high-quality education to those who can afford to live in the right neighborhoods. The children who grow up in these communities are given every opportunity to succeed, often without realizing how rare and precious those opportunities are.

The result is a society where the gap between rich and poor is reinforced by the very institutions that are supposed to promote equality. The schools become engines of inequality, passing on advantages and disadvantages from one generation to the next. The promise of equal opportunity is undermined by a system that rewards privilege and punishes poverty.

For those who see both sides of the divide, the question is inescapable: how can a society that values fairness and justice allow such disparities to persist? The answer is complex, rooted in history, politics, and deeply held beliefs about merit and responsibility. But for the children who pass through these schools, the consequences are immediate and profound.

## Chapter 5 The Cycle of Segregation: Race, Poverty, and the Persistence of Inequality

The story of America’s schools is not just about money; it’s also about race and the long shadow of segregation. Decades after the Supreme Court declared that separate schools are inherently unequal, many children still attend schools that are deeply divided along racial and economic lines.

In cities across the country, neighborhoods remain segregated by race and income. The schools reflect these divisions, with Black and Latino children concentrated in underfunded districts, while white and affluent students attend well-resourced schools. The reasons for this are complex—rooted in housing policies, economic inequality, and the legacy of discrimination—but the result is clear: the promise of integration remains unfulfilled.

A family moving into a new city might quickly learn which neighborhoods have the “good schools.” Real estate agents, neighbors, and even local officials steer families toward certain areas, reinforcing patterns of segregation. The schools in these neighborhoods become magnets for privilege, while those in poorer areas are left to struggle.

For the children who attend segregated, underfunded schools, the impact is profound. They are often taught by less experienced teachers, in overcrowded classrooms, with fewer resources and opportunities. The sense of being cut off from the mainstream is reinforced every day, in ways both large and small.

A student in a segregated school might notice that their textbooks are old and worn, that the science lab is missing equipment, that the art room is used for storage. They might hear about programs and opportunities available in other schools, but know that those doors are closed to them. The sense of exclusion is not just academic; it shapes their sense of self and their place in the world.

The teachers in these schools face their own challenges. Many are committed and passionate, but the constant struggle for resources takes its toll. Teacher turnover is high, and the lack of support makes it hard to build lasting relationships with students. The sense of frustration is palpable, as teachers try to do more with less, year after year.

The cycle of segregation is self-perpetuating. As schools in poor, minority neighborhoods struggle, families who can afford to leave do so, taking their resources and support with them. The schools are left with the most vulnerable students, and the challenges only grow. Attempts to integrate schools have often been met with resistance, both overt and subtle. The result is a system that continues to sort children by race and class, despite the rhetoric of equality.

The consequences of this segregation are far-reaching. Students in underfunded, segregated schools are less likely to graduate, less likely to attend college, and more likely to face limited job prospects. The cycle of poverty and disadvantage is reinforced, making it harder for each new generation to break free.

Yet, even in the face of these challenges, there are moments of hope. Some schools find ways to create a sense of community and belonging, despite the odds. Teachers and students form strong bonds, and parents organize to demand better conditions. These efforts are often small and hard-won, but they matter.

The persistence of segregation and inequality in America’s schools is a reminder that the struggle for justice is ongoing. The barriers are not just legal or political; they are woven into the fabric of everyday life. Changing the system requires more than good intentions—it demands a willingness to confront uncomfortable truths and to imagine a different future.

## Chapter 6 The Human Cost: Dreams Deferred and the Weight of Injustice

When you listen to the stories of children growing up in neglected schools, the statistics and policy debates fade into the background. What remains are the quiet moments—a kid staring out a cracked window, wondering if college is even possible; a teacher’s voice trembling as she tells a parent she can’t offer the extra help their child needs. These are the moments where the true cost of inequality is felt, not in numbers, but in lives shaped by what’s missing.

There’s a boy who loves science, always asking questions about how things work. But his school’s lab is a closet with a few broken beakers and a single outdated microscope. He tries to make do, improvising experiments with whatever he can find, but the spark of curiosity dims a little each time he’s told, “We don’t have the materials for that.” His teacher, who sees his potential, feels a pang of guilt every time she has to say no.

A girl in another classroom dreams of being a writer. She fills notebooks with stories, but the school library is mostly empty shelves, and the only computer is always broken. She writes anyway, but she wonders if her words will ever matter beyond these walls. Her friends tease her for dreaming so big, and sometimes she believes them.

For many students, the daily grind of scarcity becomes a kind of background noise. They get used to sharing textbooks, to skipping lunch because the cafeteria ran out of food, to teachers who come and go. But every so often, something breaks through—a visit to a better-funded school, a news story about a student their age winning a scholarship, a teacher who refuses to give up. In those moments, the weight of what’s been lost is almost too much to bear.

The adults in these schools carry their own burdens. A principal spends her evenings writing grant applications, hoping for a few thousand dollars to fix the roof. A counselor juggles a caseload of hundreds, trying to help kids navigate trauma, hunger, and the pressure to drop out and work. They do what they can, but the system is stacked against them.

Most people outside these neighborhoods never see this side of America. They might hear about “failing schools” or “achievement gaps,” but the reality is more intimate and more painful. It’s the slow erosion of hope, the quiet resignation that comes from being told, over and over, that you don’t matter as much as others.

Yet, even in the midst of all this, there are moments of defiance. A student wins a citywide essay contest, surprising everyone but her teacher. A group of parents organizes a fundraiser, raising enough money for new uniforms for the basketball team. These victories are small, but they are real. They are reminders that, even in the harshest conditions, people find ways to push back against the odds.

The cost of inequality is not just measured in test scores or graduation rates. It’s measured in dreams deferred, in talents left undeveloped, in the quiet ache of knowing you could have done more if only you’d been given the chance. For every child who manages to break through, there are many more whose potential is quietly lost. That is the true tragedy of America’s divided schools.

## Chapter 7 Attempts at Reform: Hope, Resistance, and the Limits of Change

Every few years, a new wave of reform sweeps through the world of public education. Politicians promise to “fix our schools,” philanthropists launch ambitious initiatives, and communities rally around the hope that things can be different. For a while, there’s a sense of possibility—a belief that, this time, real change might be within reach.

In one city, a new superintendent arrives with bold plans. She talks about equity, about closing the gap, about making sure every child has what they need. Teachers are hopeful, parents are cautiously optimistic. For a while, things seem to improve: new textbooks arrive, after-school programs are expanded, and the buildings get a fresh coat of paint.

But change is hard, and the forces that created the inequalities in the first place are stubborn. Funding dries up when the economy falters. Political support shifts with each election. Some parents in wealthier neighborhoods push back, worried that their own schools will lose resources. The superintendent faces criticism from all sides, and the momentum slows.

A teacher who was once excited about the reforms finds herself frustrated again. The new programs are underfunded, and the extra paperwork takes time away from her students. She wonders if anything will ever really change.

In another district, a group of parents files a lawsuit, arguing that their children are being denied a basic education. The case drags on for years, winding its way through the courts. There are moments of hope—a judge rules in their favor, the state promises more funding—but the changes are slow and uneven. Some schools see real improvements, while others are left behind.

The story repeats itself across the country. Well-intentioned reforms bump up against the realities of politics, economics, and deeply held beliefs about who deserves what. Some changes stick, but many fade away, leaving students and teachers feeling whiplashed by the constant churn.

Still, there are bright spots. In some places, community organizing leads to real gains—new playgrounds, smaller class sizes, more counselors. A principal who refuses to give up manages to turn her school around, not with flashy programs, but with relentless attention to the needs of her students. These successes are hard-won, and they rarely make the headlines, but they matter deeply to the people involved.

The lesson that emerges is both sobering and hopeful. Real change is possible, but it’s rarely quick or easy. It requires persistence, creativity, and a willingness to confront uncomfortable truths. Most of all, it requires a belief that every child is worth fighting for, no matter where they live or what they look like.

## Chapter 8 The Power of Community: Grassroots Efforts and Everyday Heroes

In the face of overwhelming odds, it’s often the people closest to the problem who find the most creative solutions. Across the country, parents, teachers, and students come together to make their schools better, one small step at a time.

In a city where the school budget has been slashed again, a group of parents starts a weekend tutoring program in the basement of a local church. They bring snacks, old laptops, and a lot of determination. The kids show up, some shy at first, but soon the room is buzzing with energy. A retired teacher volunteers to help, and before long, the program grows.

Elsewhere, a high school student notices that her classmates are struggling with college applications. She organizes a workshop, inviting recent graduates to share their experiences. The event is a hit, and soon the school counselor is working with her to make it a regular part of the school year.

A principal in a struggling district decides to open the school library on weekends, giving kids a safe place to read and study. She recruits volunteers from the community, and the library becomes a hub of activity. For some students, it’s the only quiet place they have to do homework.

These efforts don’t solve all the problems, but they make a difference. They show what’s possible when people refuse to accept the status quo. The work is often exhausting, and the victories are sometimes small, but the impact is real. Kids who might have fallen through the cracks find support. Teachers who feel isolated discover allies. Parents who once felt powerless realize they can make a difference.

There’s a kind of magic in these grassroots efforts—a sense of possibility that comes from working together. The problems are big, but so is the capacity for hope and resilience. Most people don’t notice these quiet acts of heroism, but they are the threads that hold communities together.

Over time, these small changes can add up. A school that once felt hopeless becomes a place where kids want to be. A neighborhood that was written off finds new energy. The lesson is clear: while the system may be slow to change, people are not powerless. When they come together, they can create something better, even in the face of daunting odds.

## Chapter 9 The National Conversation: Myths, Realities, and the Struggle for Justice

Step outside the daily life of America’s schools, and you find a country wrestling with big questions about fairness, opportunity, and what it means to care for all its children. The national conversation is often loud and confusing, filled with competing stories about why some schools succeed and others fail.

Some people argue that the problem is a lack of effort—that if students and families just worked harder, things would improve. Others point to the system itself, to the way resources are distributed and the barriers that keep some kids from getting what they need. The truth, as always, is more complicated.

There’s a persistent myth that America’s schools are a great equalizer, that anyone can succeed if they try hard enough. But for those who have seen the realities up close, this story rings hollow. The playing field is not level, and the obstacles are not the same for everyone.

A parent in a wealthy suburb may worry about their child’s grades or college prospects, but they rarely have to worry about whether the school roof will leak or if the heat will work in January. A student in a poor neighborhood faces challenges that most people never even think about—hunger, violence, the constant stress of scarcity.

The national debate often misses these nuances. Politicians talk about “school choice” and “accountability,” but the voices of those most affected are rarely heard. The conversation is shaped by those with power and privilege, while the people living with the consequences are left on the margins.

Yet, there are moments when the truth breaks through. A news story about a school in crisis sparks outrage. A student’s essay about her dreams goes viral. For a brief moment, the country pays attention. But the attention fades, and the system remains.

The struggle for justice in America’s schools is ongoing. It’s a battle not just over budgets and policies, but over the very idea of what kind of country we want to be. Do we believe that every child deserves a fair shot, or are we content to let the gap between rich and poor keep growing?

The answer is not just a matter of policy. It’s a question of values, of empathy, of whether we are willing to see the world through someone else’s eyes. The fight for better schools is, at its heart, a fight for a more just and compassionate society.

## Chapter 10 Enduring Hope: Children, Teachers, and the Possibility of Change

Despite everything—the broken buildings, the empty budgets, the weight of history—there is a stubborn hope that refuses to die. It lives in the laughter of children playing on a cracked playground, in the determination of a teacher who stays late to help a struggling student, in the quiet pride of a parent who believes in their child’s future.

A boy who once doubted himself wins a scholarship to college. His teacher, who never stopped believing in him, cheers from the audience at graduation. A girl who grew up in a neighborhood everyone else had written off becomes the first in her family to graduate high school. Her mother cries tears of joy, remembering all the nights she spent worrying about the future.

These stories are not rare. They happen every day, in every corner of the country. They are reminders that, even in the face of overwhelming odds, people can and do rise above their circumstances. The system may be broken, but the human spirit is not.

Teachers, especially, are the quiet heroes of this story. They show up every day, often with little support, and give everything they have to their students. They find ways to create moments of joy and discovery, even when the resources are lacking. They are the glue that holds schools together, the champions who refuse to give up.

Parents, too, play a vital role. They fight for their children, advocate for better schools, and build communities of support. Their love and determination are powerful forces for change.

And the children themselves—so often underestimated—are the heart of it all. They dream, they strive, they hope. They remind us of what’s possible, if only we are willing to invest in their futures.

The story of America’s schools is not just one of failure and injustice. It is also a story of resilience, of courage, of the enduring belief that things can be better. The path forward is not easy, but it is possible. It begins with seeing the truth, with refusing to accept the unacceptable, and with working—together—for a future where every child has a real chance to succeed.

## Conclusion: What Savage Inequalities Teaches Us—And What We Can Do

Reading the stories and realities woven through America’s schools, it’s impossible not to feel both anger and hope. The inequalities are not hidden—they are in plain sight, written into the very walls and routines of our schools. Yet, the book’s greatest gift is its insistence that these injustices are not inevitable. They are the result of choices, policies, and priorities that can be changed.

If you take one thing from this journey, let it be this: the fate of children in America’s schools is not sealed. Every day, in every community, people are working to make things better. The work is hard, and the progress is slow, but it matters. The future is not fixed—it is shaped by what we choose to see, to value, and to fight for.

**Key Takeaways:**

- School funding tied to property taxes locks in cycles of inequality and segregation.
- The daily realities of underfunded schools erode hope and limit children’s futures.
- Attempts at reform often falter without sustained commitment and community support.
- Grassroots efforts and everyday heroes can create real, lasting change in schools.
- The struggle for educational justice is ongoing, requiring empathy and collective action.

The path forward is not about waiting for a perfect solution or a single leader to fix everything. It’s about showing up—again and again—for the children who need us most. It’s about listening to those whose voices are too often ignored, and refusing to accept a world where a child’s zip code determines their destiny. Change begins with seeing the truth, with asking hard questions, and with believing that every child is worth the fight. If you carry that belief into your own life—whether as a parent, a neighbor, a voter, or a friend—you become part of the story of hope and possibility. The work is not easy, but it is necessary. And it is, in the end, the work of building a more just and loving world.
