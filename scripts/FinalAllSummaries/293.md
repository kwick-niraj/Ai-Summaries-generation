## Introduction

In the dynamic world of leadership, the difference between a thriving team and a stagnant one often comes down to the daily habits and mindsets of those at the helm. The journey through Lift: Five Practices Great Managers Do Consistently by Raymond Wheeler reveals how exceptional managers elevate both performance and morale, creating environments where employees not only meet expectations but exceed them. Central to this exploration is the understanding that great management is not a matter of innate talent, but of consistent, intentional practices that foster trust, engagement, and growth.

As the narrative unfolds, the focus sharpens on five core practices that set outstanding managers apart. These practices are not abstract theories, but actionable approaches that can be woven into the fabric of any workplace. Through real-world examples and practical insights, the path to becoming a manager who inspires, motivates, and empowers becomes clear. The emphasis remains on the transformative power of consistency—how small, repeated actions can lead to remarkable results, both for individuals and the teams they lead. With this foundation, the stage is set for a deeper dive into the principles that drive high-performing, fulfilled teams.

## Chapter 1 The Foundation of Consistent Leadership

Every workplace, regardless of industry or size, is shaped by the quality of its leadership. At the heart of effective management lies a simple but powerful idea: consistently great managers do not rely on occasional bursts of inspiration or charisma. Instead, they practice a set of reliable behaviors that, over time, create an environment where people can do their best work and feel valued. This approach is less about grand gestures and more about steady, day-to-day actions that build trust and momentum.

The concept of “lifting” others forms the backbone of this philosophy. In practical terms, lifting means helping people rise above their current level—whether that’s in skill, confidence, or engagement. It’s not about carrying the team on one’s shoulders, but about creating the conditions where everyone can stand taller. This mindset shifts the focus from managing tasks to nurturing people.

A key insight is that consistency is more important than perfection. Employees do not expect their managers to have all the answers or to never make mistakes. What matters most is that leaders show up in a predictable, supportive way. This reliability becomes a kind of psychological safety net, allowing team members to take risks, share ideas, and recover from setbacks without fear of unpredictable reactions.

Imagine a manager as the steady current beneath a boat. The water doesn’t need to be dramatic or turbulent to move the vessel forward; it just needs to flow in a dependable direction. In the same way, consistent leadership provides a stable force that helps teams navigate challenges and opportunities alike.

This foundation is built on several core beliefs:

- People want to do meaningful work and be recognized for it.
- Trust is earned through repeated, authentic actions.
- Growth happens when individuals feel both supported and challenged.

When these beliefs are put into practice, the workplace transforms from a place of mere compliance to one of genuine engagement. Employees begin to see their manager not as a distant authority figure, but as a partner in their success.

A real-world example can be seen in a mid-sized technology company where a team leader made it a habit to check in with each team member every week. These conversations were not about micromanaging tasks, but about understanding what each person needed to succeed. Over time, the team’s morale improved, turnover decreased, and performance metrics rose—not because of any single dramatic intervention, but because of the leader’s steady, caring presence.

The journey toward becoming a consistently great manager starts with a commitment to these foundational ideas. It’s about showing up, listening, and acting with integrity, day after day. This sets the stage for the more specific practices that follow, each designed to further lift individuals and teams.

## Chapter 2 Listening Deeply to Build Trust and Engagement

One of the most powerful tools a manager has is the ability to listen—not just to words, but to the underlying emotions, concerns, and aspirations of team members. Deep listening goes beyond simply hearing updates or status reports. It involves being fully present, setting aside distractions, and making space for honest dialogue.

When employees feel truly heard, several positive things happen. Trust grows, because people sense that their perspectives matter. Engagement increases, as individuals become more invested in their work and the team’s goals. Innovation flourishes, since people are more likely to share creative ideas when they know they won’t be dismissed or ignored.

Deep listening can be visualized as a bridge between manager and employee. Each conversation adds another plank, making it easier for both sides to cross over and understand each other’s world. This bridge is built not with grand speeches, but with small, consistent acts of attention.

Some practical ways to practice deep listening include:

- Setting aside regular, distraction-free time for one-on-one conversations.
- Asking open-ended questions that invite reflection and honesty.
- Reflecting back what has been heard, to ensure understanding and show empathy.
- Avoiding the urge to immediately solve problems or offer advice, instead allowing space for the employee to express themselves fully.

Consider the experience of a retail store supervisor who noticed that one of the team members had become withdrawn and less enthusiastic. Instead of jumping to conclusions or issuing warnings, the supervisor invited the employee for a private chat, simply asking how things were going. Through patient listening, it emerged that the employee was struggling with a personal issue that was affecting their work. The supervisor offered support and flexibility, which not only helped the employee recover but also strengthened the bond of trust within the team.

Listening deeply is not a one-time event, but a habit that must be cultivated. It requires patience, humility, and a willingness to be changed by what is heard. Over time, this practice creates a culture where people feel safe to speak up, share concerns, and contribute their best ideas.

The benefits of deep listening extend beyond individual relationships. Teams led by managers who listen well tend to have higher morale, better collaboration, and fewer misunderstandings. Problems are identified earlier, and solutions are more creative and inclusive.

In summary, deep listening is the first and perhaps most essential practice for lifting others. It lays the groundwork for all other aspects of effective management, from giving feedback to supporting growth. By making listening a daily priority, managers create the conditions for trust, engagement, and lasting success.

## Chapter 3 Giving Clear Feedback and Recognizing Strengths

Once a foundation of trust has been established through consistent presence and deep listening, the next step is to provide clear, constructive feedback. Feedback is often misunderstood as criticism, but in reality, it is a tool for growth and affirmation. When delivered thoughtfully, feedback helps individuals understand where they stand, what they are doing well, and where they can improve.

Effective feedback is both specific and timely. Vague praise or criticism leaves people confused and uncertain about what to repeat or change. Instead, feedback should focus on observable behaviors and outcomes, making it easier for employees to connect actions with results.

A helpful way to think about feedback is as a mirror. A good mirror reflects reality without distortion, allowing the person to see themselves clearly. In the same way, feedback should provide an accurate, balanced view—highlighting strengths as well as areas for development.

Key elements of effective feedback include:

- Focusing on behaviors, not personal traits.
- Being specific about what was observed and its impact.
- Balancing positive recognition with constructive suggestions.
- Delivering feedback in a timely manner, close to the event.

Recognition of strengths is just as important as pointing out areas for improvement. People are more likely to repeat behaviors that are noticed and appreciated. Public acknowledgment of achievements can boost morale and motivate others, while private recognition can deepen individual relationships.

For example, in a healthcare setting, a nurse manager made it a point to recognize team members who went above and beyond in patient care. This recognition was not limited to formal awards, but included simple gestures like handwritten notes or a few words of appreciation during team meetings. Over time, this culture of recognition led to higher job satisfaction and lower turnover.

At the same time, managers must not shy away from difficult conversations. When performance falls short, it is important to address the issue directly, but with empathy and a focus on solutions. The goal is not to punish, but to help the individual get back on track.

A practical approach to feedback might look like this:

- Begin with a positive observation to set a supportive tone.
- Clearly describe the behavior or outcome that needs attention.
- Invite the employee to share their perspective.
- Collaboratively discuss steps for improvement or continued success.

By making feedback a regular part of team interactions, rather than a rare or dreaded event, managers normalize the process of learning and growth. Employees come to see feedback as a gift, not a threat.

In summary, clear feedback and recognition are essential tools for lifting others. They help people understand their value, see a path forward, and feel motivated to contribute their best. When combined with deep listening, these practices create a powerful engine for individual and team development.

## Chapter 4 Supporting Growth and Ownership

Beyond listening and feedback, great managers are distinguished by their commitment to supporting the growth and ownership of their team members. This means creating opportunities for people to stretch, learn, and take responsibility for meaningful work. Rather than holding tightly to control, effective leaders empower others to step up and lead in their own right.

Supporting growth begins with understanding each person’s aspirations, strengths, and areas for development. This requires ongoing dialogue and a willingness to see potential, even when it is not yet fully realized. Managers act as coaches, helping individuals set goals, identify resources, and navigate challenges.

Ownership, in this context, refers to the sense of agency and accountability that comes from being trusted with important tasks. When people are given the chance to make decisions and see the impact of their work, they become more invested in the team’s success.

Some practical ways to foster growth and ownership include:

- Delegating meaningful projects, not just routine tasks.
- Encouraging employees to propose solutions and take initiative.
- Providing access to training, mentorship, or new experiences.
- Celebrating progress and learning, even when outcomes are not perfect.

Consider the example of a marketing manager who noticed that one team member had a knack for creative problem-solving. Instead of keeping this person in a narrow role, the manager invited them to lead a new campaign, offering guidance but allowing room for independent decision-making. The employee rose to the challenge, developing new skills and gaining confidence. The team benefited from fresh ideas, and the manager was able to focus on higher-level strategy.

Supporting growth also means being there during setbacks. When people stumble, managers can help them reflect on what went wrong, extract lessons, and try again. This approach turns mistakes into opportunities for learning, rather than sources of shame or frustration.

A culture of growth and ownership does not happen by accident. It requires intentional effort and a willingness to let go of the need to control every detail. Managers who embrace this mindset find that their teams become more resilient, adaptable, and innovative.

In summary, supporting growth and ownership is a key practice for lifting others. It transforms the manager’s role from gatekeeper to enabler, unlocking the full potential of each team member. When people are trusted to lead, they rise to the occasion—and the entire organization benefits.

## Chapter 5 Creating a Positive and Sustainable Team Culture

The final core practice that distinguishes consistently great managers is the intentional creation of a positive and sustainable team culture. Culture is often described as “the way we do things around here,” but it is more than just habits or routines. It is the invisible force that shapes how people feel, interact, and perform.

A positive culture does not emerge by chance. It is the result of deliberate choices and consistent actions by leaders at every level. Managers play a crucial role in setting the tone, modeling desired behaviors, and reinforcing shared values.

Key elements of a healthy team culture include:

- Psychological safety, where people feel comfortable speaking up and taking risks.
- Shared purpose, so everyone understands how their work contributes to larger goals.
- Mutual respect, with differences valued and conflicts handled constructively.
- Celebration of both individual and collective achievements.

Building such a culture requires attention to both the big picture and the small details. Rituals, such as regular team check-ins or informal celebrations, help reinforce a sense of belonging. Clear communication about expectations and values ensures that everyone is aligned.

For instance, a project manager in a construction firm made it a habit to start each week with a brief team huddle, where successes were acknowledged and challenges discussed openly. This simple ritual fostered camaraderie and transparency, making it easier to address problems before they escalated.

Sustainability is also a key consideration. High performance cannot be maintained if people are burned out or disengaged. Managers must pay attention to workload, encourage healthy boundaries, and model self-care. This might mean insisting on reasonable work hours, providing flexibility, or simply checking in on how people are doing beyond their tasks.

A sustainable culture is one where people can thrive over the long term, not just hit short-term targets. It balances ambition with well-being, competition with collaboration, and results with relationships.

In summary, creating a positive and sustainable team culture is the capstone of effective management. It brings together all the previous practices—consistency, listening, feedback, growth, and ownership—into a cohesive whole. When culture is strong, teams are more resilient, creative, and committed. The workplace becomes not just a place to earn a living, but a community where people can grow and succeed together.

## Chapter 6 Integrating the Five Practices—Building a Management Flywheel

With the five core practices established, the next step is to understand how they work together as a self-reinforcing system. Rather than isolated actions, these practices form a kind of management flywheel—a cycle where each element strengthens the others, creating ongoing momentum for team growth and resilience.

Picture a flywheel in motion: once it’s spinning, each push makes it easier to keep going. In the same way, when a manager consistently listens, gives feedback, supports growth, and nurtures culture, the team’s energy and trust build naturally. This momentum makes it easier to handle challenges, adapt to change, and sustain high performance.

The flywheel effect emerges through:

- **Repetition:** Small, positive actions repeated over time become habits that shape team norms.
- **Reinforcement:** Each practice supports the others—listening makes feedback more relevant, feedback clarifies growth opportunities, and a strong culture amplifies all efforts.
- **Resilience:** When setbacks occur, the established trust and routines help the team recover quickly.

For example, consider a nonprofit team facing a sudden funding cut. Because the manager had already built trust through regular check-ins and open feedback, the team felt safe discussing tough options. Growth opportunities were reframed as new challenges, and the positive culture helped everyone stay focused on shared values. The flywheel of good management practices kept the team moving forward, even in uncertainty.

Managers can visualize this system as a circle, not a ladder—there’s no final step, just ongoing cycles of support and improvement. The key is to keep the flywheel turning, especially during stressful times, by returning to the basics: show up, listen, give feedback, support growth, and nurture culture.

## Chapter 7 Navigating Common Challenges and Setbacks

Even the most consistent managers encounter obstacles—conflict, disengagement, or unexpected change. The difference lies in how these challenges are approached. Rather than seeing setbacks as failures, effective managers treat them as opportunities to reinforce the five practices and model resilience.

Some common challenges and practical responses include:

- **Team Conflict:** When disagreements arise, managers can draw on deep listening and psychological safety. By inviting all voices and focusing on shared goals, conflict becomes a source of creative problem-solving rather than division.
- **Low Morale or Burnout:** If energy dips, it’s time to revisit recognition and workload balance. Small gestures of appreciation, honest conversations about stress, and adjustments to expectations can help restore well-being.
- **Performance Issues:** When someone struggles, clear feedback and growth support are essential. Address issues directly but compassionately, focusing on solutions and learning rather than blame.
- **Change and Uncertainty:** During transitions, consistency and communication matter most. Reiterate shared values, keep routines where possible, and invite input on navigating new realities.

A real-life example: a customer service manager noticed rising tension after a company reorganization. Instead of ignoring the discomfort, she held a team meeting to surface concerns, listened without judgment, and worked with the group to co-create new norms. By leaning into the five practices, she helped the team regain stability and trust.

The lesson is that challenges are not interruptions to good management—they are the moments when it matters most. Returning to the core practices provides both a compass and a toolkit for navigating uncertainty.

## Chapter 8 Adapting the Practices to Different People and Situations

No two teams—or individuals—are exactly alike. Great managers recognize that the five practices must be adapted to fit the unique needs, personalities, and contexts of their people. This flexibility is not about abandoning consistency, but about personalizing the approach within a stable framework.

Some ways to tailor the practices include:

- **Listening Styles:** Some team members open up in one-on-ones, while others prefer written check-ins or group discussions. Adjust the format to match comfort levels.
- **Feedback Preferences:** While some thrive on public recognition, others value private, specific praise. Learn what motivates each person and deliver feedback accordingly.
- **Growth Opportunities:** Not everyone wants the same kind of challenge. Offer a range of projects, training, or responsibilities to match individual aspirations.
- **Cultural Sensitivity:** Be mindful of cultural backgrounds, communication norms, and values that may shape how people experience trust, feedback, and ownership.

For instance, a global project manager found that her European team valued direct feedback, while her Asian colleagues preferred a more nuanced approach. By learning and respecting these differences, she built stronger relationships and avoided misunderstandings.

The key is to stay curious and open, asking team members what works best for them and being willing to adjust. This adaptability, grounded in the five core practices, ensures that everyone feels seen, respected, and supported.

## Chapter 9 Sustaining Your Own Energy and Growth as a Manager

Lifting others is demanding work. To sustain these practices over the long haul, managers must also care for their own well-being and development. Just as flight attendants remind us to put on our own oxygen mask first, leaders need to recharge and grow in order to support their teams effectively.

Some practical self-sustaining habits include:

- **Regular Reflection:** Set aside time to review what’s working, where you’re struggling, and what you’re learning. Journaling or peer conversations can help clarify insights.
- **Seeking Feedback:** Invite honest input from your team, peers, or mentors. This not only models humility but also uncovers blind spots and growth areas.
- **Setting Boundaries:** Protect time for rest, family, and personal interests. Model healthy work-life balance so your team feels permission to do the same.
- **Continuous Learning:** Invest in your own development—read, attend workshops, or connect with other managers to share ideas and challenges.

A manager in a fast-paced startup found herself on the edge of burnout. By blocking out one hour each week for reflection and learning, she regained perspective and energy. Her renewed presence benefited both her and her team.

Remember, the practices that lift others also apply to yourself. By nurturing your own growth and well-being, you become a more resilient, empathetic, and effective leader.

## Chapter 10 Embedding the Practices for Long-Term Impact

The final step is to make these practices part of the team’s DNA—woven into daily routines, rituals, and systems so they endure beyond any one manager or moment. This is how a culture of lifting others becomes self-sustaining and scalable.

Ways to embed the practices include:

- **Ritualizing Check-Ins:** Make regular one-on-ones, team huddles, or feedback sessions a non-negotiable part of the calendar.
- **Documenting Values and Norms:** Co-create a simple team charter or set of agreements that reflect the five practices.
- **Peer Recognition:** Encourage team members to recognize each other’s strengths and contributions, not just rely on the manager.
- **Onboarding and Training:** Introduce new hires to the team’s culture and expectations from day one, modeling the practices in action.

For example, a software company created a “culture playbook” that outlined how the team listens, gives feedback, supports growth, and celebrates wins. This living document helped new and existing members stay aligned, even as the team grew and changed.

Over time, these embedded practices become self-reinforcing. New leaders emerge, team members hold each other accountable, and the culture remains strong through transitions. The manager’s role shifts from sole driver to steward—guiding, supporting, and trusting the team to carry the torch.

## Conclusion: Bringing the Practices to Life

**Key Takeaways:**

- Consistency in small actions builds trust and psychological safety over time.
- Deep listening is the foundation for engagement, innovation, and honest feedback.
- Clear, timely feedback and recognition help people grow and feel valued.
- Supporting growth and ownership unlocks individual and team potential.
- A positive, sustainable culture is built through daily rituals and shared values.
- The five practices work as a flywheel, reinforcing and sustaining each other.
- Adaptation and self-care are essential for long-term leadership effectiveness.
- Embedding these habits ensures the culture endures beyond any one manager.

As you move forward, remember that great management is not about perfection or grand gestures. It’s about showing up, day after day, with intention and care. The five practices—listening, feedback, growth, ownership, and culture—are not a checklist to complete, but a set of habits to live. Some days will be easier than others. There will be setbacks, misunderstandings, and moments of doubt. But each time you return to these core actions, you reinforce the foundation for trust, resilience, and shared success.

If you ever feel overwhelmed, pause and focus on just one practice—perhaps a quiet conversation, a word of appreciation, or a small act of support. These moments, repeated and shared, are what truly lift teams and organizations. Over time, you’ll find that the energy you invest in others comes back to you, multiplied in the form of loyalty, creativity, and collective achievement.

You don’t have to do it all at once. Start where you are, with what you have, and let the practices grow with you. Leadership is a journey, not a destination—and every step you take to lift others lifts you, too.
