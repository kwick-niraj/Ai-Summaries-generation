## Introduction

Desires shape destinies, often more powerfully than conscious beliefs or intellectual convictions. Beneath the surface of daily routines, habits quietly mold the heart, steering affections and aspirations in directions that may go unnoticed. In You Are What You Love: The Spiritual Power of Habit, James K.A. Smith delves into the profound connection between spiritual formation and the rhythms of everyday life, revealing how the things people repeatedly do are not just actions, but formative practices that sculpt the very core of identity. The question shifts from "What do you think?" to "What do you love?"—inviting a deeper exploration of how love, more than knowledge, directs the course of a life.

Within this framework, the ordinary becomes sacred, and the mundane routines of work, worship, and relationships are recast as powerful liturgies that shape the soul. Smith’s insights illuminate the subtle ways in which culture, community, and personal rituals influence what is truly valued, often beneath the level of conscious choice. The journey unfolds as an invitation to reexamine the habits that guide daily existence, offering a vision of spiritual growth rooted not in mere willpower, but in the intentional cultivation of love and desire. This perspective opens a path toward transformation, where the alignment of heart and habit becomes the foundation for a flourishing, purpose-driven life.

## Chapter 1 What Shapes a Life: The Power of Desire Beneath the Surface

Most people imagine that what they know or believe is what truly defines them. It’s easy to picture a person as a mind, collecting ideas and making choices based on what seems right. But beneath the surface, something deeper is at work. The real engine of a life isn’t just thought—it’s desire. The things people long for, sometimes without even realizing it, quietly steer their days and decisions.

Picture a college student, eager to make a difference in the world. She fills her shelves with books, attends lectures, and debates ideas late into the night. She’s convinced that if she just learns enough, she’ll become the person she wants to be. But as the weeks pass, she notices something unsettling. Despite all her knowledge, she keeps falling into the same patterns—scrolling through her phone for hours, chasing approval, feeling restless. It’s as if her mind and her actions are out of sync.

This gap between what people think and what they actually do is familiar to many. It’s not just about willpower or discipline. There’s a hidden current, a set of habits and loves, that quietly shapes the direction of a life. Most don’t notice it happening. They assume that if they believe the right things, everything else will fall into place. But over time, it becomes clear: what the heart wants, the life follows.

In everyday routines, this truth shows up in small, telling ways. A parent, exhausted after a long day, finds comfort in a favorite TV show, even though she wishes she spent more time reading to her kids. A young professional, determined to save money, can’t resist the pull of online shopping. These aren’t just slips in judgment—they’re signs of deeper desires at work, desires that have been shaped by years of practice.

The world is full of messages about what’s worth loving. Advertisements, social media, even the layout of a shopping mall—all are designed to capture attention and steer desire. Without realizing it, people are trained to want certain things, to chase after comfort, status, or excitement. These aren’t just passing influences; they’re powerful habits that form the heart.

It’s tempting to think that changing a life is just a matter of making better choices. But the truth is more complicated. The things people do over and over—their routines, rituals, and habits—are what shape what they love. And what they love, in turn, shapes who they become.

This realization can be unsettling. It means that good intentions aren’t enough. It’s possible to believe all the right things and still find life pulled in a different direction. But it’s also freeing. If desire can be shaped, then it can be reshaped. The journey of spiritual growth isn’t just about learning new ideas—it’s about forming new loves, one small habit at a time.

## Chapter 2 Habits That Form the Heart: How Everyday Practices Shape Desire

Most people underestimate the quiet power of habit. It’s easy to see routines as mindless or unimportant, but in reality, they’re the training ground for the heart. The things people do without thinking—their morning rituals, the way they spend their free time, the patterns of their days—are constantly shaping what they care about most.

Consider a teenager who starts every morning by checking social media. At first, it’s just a way to catch up with friends. But over time, it becomes a reflex. The phone is the first thing she reaches for, even before getting out of bed. She doesn’t notice how this habit is shaping her desires—how it’s teaching her to crave attention, to measure her worth by likes and comments. The habit isn’t just a routine; it’s a lesson about what matters.

In another home, a family gathers around the dinner table every evening. They light a candle, say a simple prayer, and share stories from their day. The ritual is small, but it leaves a mark. Over the years, the children learn that meals are about more than food—they’re about connection, gratitude, and belonging. The habit forms their hearts, teaching them what to love.

Most people don’t choose their habits on purpose. They pick them up from the world around them, often without realizing it. The culture is full of “secular liturgies”—practices that shape desire in subtle but powerful ways. A trip to the mall, for example, isn’t just about shopping. The music, the displays, the promise of newness—all are designed to stir up longing, to make people want more. The mall becomes a kind of temple, training people to love consumption.

These everyday liturgies are everywhere. The rhythm of the workweek, the rituals of entertainment, even the way people greet each other—all carry messages about what’s valuable. Over time, these practices sink in, forming the heart’s deepest loves.

It’s easy to overlook the formative power of these habits. People assume that as long as they believe the right things, their lives will follow. But the heart is shaped by what it does, not just what it thinks. The things people repeat, day after day, become the grooves along which their desires run.

This is why spiritual formation isn’t just about ideas. It’s about practices—habits that aim the heart toward what’s truly good. In the Christian tradition, worship is more than an expression of belief. It’s a way of training desire, of forming the heart to love God and neighbor.

But this kind of formation doesn’t happen by accident. It requires intention—a willingness to pay attention to the habits that are shaping life, and to choose practices that point the heart in a better direction. It’s a slow process, full of small steps and quiet routines. But over time, these habits become the foundation of a life well-lived.

## Chapter 3 Competing Stories: How Culture’s Rituals Shape What People Love

Every day, people are surrounded by stories about what matters most. These stories aren’t always told with words. Sometimes, they’re woven into the routines and rituals of daily life. Without realizing it, people are shaped by these cultural liturgies—practices that teach them what to love, what to chase, and what to fear.

Imagine a young professional walking into a high-end store. The lighting is soft, the music carefully chosen, the displays arranged to catch the eye. There’s a sense of possibility in the air—the promise that buying something new will bring happiness, confidence, or belonging. The experience is carefully crafted, designed to stir up desire. It’s not just about the products on the shelves; it’s about the story being told: “You are what you own. Fulfillment is just one purchase away.”

Or think of a sports fan, swept up in the energy of a packed stadium. The crowd moves as one, chanting, cheering, celebrating every victory. There’s a sense of unity, of purpose, of belonging to something bigger. The ritual of the game becomes a kind of worship, shaping what the heart longs for—excitement, triumph, community.

These aren’t just harmless diversions. They’re powerful habits that form the heart. The world is full of these secular liturgies—practices that compete with spiritual formation, offering their own visions of the good life. They promise meaning, identity, and satisfaction, but often leave people restless and unsatisfied.

Most people don’t notice how these rituals are shaping them. They move through their days, following routines that seem normal, even necessary. But over time, these habits form deep grooves in the heart, teaching people to love things that can never truly satisfy.

It’s not just the obvious rituals that matter. Even the way people use technology, the rhythms of work and rest, the patterns of entertainment—all carry messages about what’s worth loving. A person who spends hours each day scrolling through news feeds or chasing the next big thing is being formed, whether they realize it or not.

The challenge is that these cultural liturgies are everywhere. They’re easy to slip into, hard to resist. They offer quick rewards, instant gratification, and the comfort of fitting in. But they also shape desire in ways that can pull people away from what’s truly good.

Recognizing these competing stories is the first step toward spiritual formation. It means paying attention to the habits that are shaping the heart, and asking hard questions about what they’re teaching. Are these routines forming love for what matters, or are they training the heart to chase after lesser things?

It’s not about rejecting culture or withdrawing from the world. It’s about becoming wise to the ways desire is shaped, and choosing practices that point the heart in a better direction. It’s a slow, sometimes difficult process, but it’s the only way to become the kind of person who loves what’s truly worth loving.

## Chapter 4 Worship as Formation: How Christian Practices Redirect Desire

For many, worship is something done on Sundays—a time to sing, pray, and listen to familiar words. It’s easy to see it as an expression of belief, a way to show what’s in the heart. But worship is more than that. It’s a practice that shapes desire, forming the heart to love what’s truly good.

Picture a small congregation gathered in a simple church. The pews are worn, the music unpolished, but there’s a quiet rhythm to the service. People stand and sit, recite ancient words, share bread and wine. These actions might seem routine, even boring at times. But over the years, they leave a mark. The repetition forms the heart, teaching it to long for God, to care for neighbor, to find hope in something bigger than the self.

In another home, a family lights a candle before dinner, says a prayer, and shares a moment of silence. The ritual is small, but it shapes the evening. It’s a reminder that life is a gift, that gratitude matters, that love is found in simple things. Over time, these habits form the heart, teaching it to love what’s good and true.

Worship isn’t just about expressing what people already believe. It’s about forming new loves, redirecting desire toward what’s truly life-giving. The practices of worship—singing, praying, confessing, sharing a meal—are like exercises for the heart. They train people to love God and neighbor, to find joy in service, to hope in the face of disappointment.

This kind of formation doesn’t happen overnight. It’s the result of small, repeated actions, done week after week, year after year. The habits of worship sink in, shaping the heart in ways that words alone cannot.

But worship isn’t just for Sundays. The practices that form the heart can be woven into daily life—at the dinner table, in moments of silence, in acts of kindness. These habits become anchors, reminders of what matters most.

It’s easy to underestimate the power of these practices. They seem ordinary, even forgettable. But over time, they form the heart, teaching it to love what’s truly worth loving. They offer a counter-story to the messages of the world, a different vision of the good life.

Spiritual formation is about more than ideas. It’s about habits—practices that aim the heart toward what’s truly good. Worship is the training ground for this kind of formation, a place where desire is shaped, redirected, and renewed.

## Chapter 5 Intentional Practices: Building Habits That Aim the Heart Toward God

Most people drift through life, shaped by habits they never chose. The routines of work, the pull of technology, the rhythms of culture—all form the heart in ways that often go unnoticed. But spiritual growth requires intention—a willingness to choose practices that aim desire in a better direction.

Consider a busy parent, overwhelmed by the demands of work and family. She longs for peace, for a sense of purpose, but finds herself caught in a cycle of exhaustion and distraction. One day, she decides to set aside a few minutes each morning for silence and prayer. At first, it feels awkward, even pointless. But over time, the habit becomes a lifeline—a moment of stillness that shapes the rest of the day.

In another setting, a group of friends commits to sharing a meal once a week. They turn off their phones, light a candle, and take turns sharing what they’re grateful for. The ritual is simple, but it forms a bond. It teaches them to slow down, to listen, to care for one another. The habit shapes their hearts, teaching them to love community over busyness.

These intentional practices aren’t about earning favor or checking boxes. They’re about forming the heart, training it to love what’s truly good. The habits of worship, prayer, gratitude, and service become the grooves along which desire runs.

But building new habits isn’t easy. It requires patience, persistence, and grace. There will be days when the old routines feel stronger, when the heart longs for comfort or distraction. But over time, the new practices sink in, forming new loves.

Spiritual formation is a journey, not a destination. It’s a process of becoming, of learning to love what’s truly worth loving. The habits people choose—at home, at work, in community—are the tools for this journey. They shape the heart, one small step at a time.

The world is full of competing stories, each offering its own vision of the good life. But through intentional practices, it’s possible to form a different kind of heart—a heart that loves God, cares for neighbor, and finds joy in the ordinary. This is the quiet power of habit, the spiritual strength that comes from loving what’s truly good.

## Chapter 6 The Home as a Habitation of Love: Family Rituals and the Formation of Desire

There’s a certain warmth that fills a home when its rhythms are shaped by love. Not the kind of love that’s loud or showy, but the quiet, steady kind that’s woven into the fabric of daily life. In the rush of school runs, work deadlines, and endless chores, it’s easy to overlook how much these small, repeated actions matter. But the home is where the heart first learns what to love.

Think of a household where mornings begin with a gentle greeting, a shared breakfast, and a moment of gratitude. The children may not remember every word spoken, but they feel the security of belonging. Over time, these rituals become the background music of their lives, teaching them that love is found in presence, not perfection.

In another home, the evening is marked by a simple ritual: everyone gathers in the living room, phones tucked away, and each person shares a high and a low from their day. There’s laughter, sometimes tears, but always a sense of being seen. The habit isn’t grand, but it’s formative. It teaches that every story matters, that vulnerability is safe, and that love listens.

Most families don’t set out to create these rituals. They emerge from the needs and quirks of daily life. But when practiced with intention, they become powerful tools for shaping desire. The way a family celebrates birthdays, handles conflict, or marks the change of seasons—all these moments are opportunities to form the heart.

It’s easy to underestimate the impact of these habits. Parents may worry that their efforts are too small, too ordinary to make a difference. But over time, the routines of home become the soil in which love grows. Children learn what to value, how to forgive, and where to find hope—not from lectures, but from the lived example of those around them.

Of course, no family is perfect. There are days when tempers flare, when routines fall apart, when love feels stretched thin. But even in the mess, there’s room for grace. The willingness to apologize, to start again, to keep showing up—these are the habits that form a resilient heart.

The home is the first school of love. Its rituals, both big and small, shape what people desire and who they become. In a world that pulls hearts in a thousand directions, the quiet practices of family life offer a different story—a story where love is patient, where belonging is secure, and where the heart is gently formed, one day at a time.

## Chapter 7 Community and the Shared Life: How Friendship and Belonging Shape the Heart

There’s a kind of magic that happens when people gather—not just for a purpose, but for the simple joy of being together. In a world that often prizes independence and self-sufficiency, the power of community can be easy to overlook. But the truth is, no one is formed in isolation. The heart is shaped in the company of others.

Imagine a group of neighbors who meet every month for a potluck dinner. The food is homemade, the conversation meanders, and the laughter is easy. Over time, these gatherings become more than just meals—they become a lifeline. When someone loses a job or faces an illness, the group rallies around, offering meals, rides, or just a listening ear. The habit of gathering forms a web of care, teaching everyone that they are not alone.

In another setting, a circle of friends commits to reading and discussing a book together. The topics are sometimes challenging, but the real gift is the space to be honest, to wrestle with doubts, to share hopes and fears. The ritual of meeting, of listening and being heard, shapes the heart to value truth, empathy, and growth.

Community isn’t always easy. There are misunderstandings, disappointments, and moments of awkwardness. But these challenges are part of the formation process. Learning to forgive, to ask for help, to celebrate another’s joy—these are habits that stretch the heart, making it more spacious and resilient.

Most people long for belonging, but few realize that it’s built through small, repeated acts. The way a group welcomes a newcomer, the willingness to show up even when it’s inconvenient, the habit of sharing both burdens and joys—these are the practices that form a community of love.

In a culture that often encourages competition and comparison, true community offers a different vision. It teaches that worth isn’t measured by achievement, but by presence. It reminds people that they are seen, known, and valued—not for what they do, but for who they are.

The shared life is a school of love. Its rituals, both formal and informal, shape the heart to desire connection, generosity, and grace. In the company of others, people learn to love more deeply, to forgive more freely, and to hope more boldly. Community is not just a support system—it’s a place of transformation, where the heart is formed for the good of all.

## Chapter 8 Work, Vocation, and the Habits of Meaning

For many, work is a source of both pride and frustration. It’s where people spend much of their time, where they chase dreams, and where they often measure their worth. But beneath the deadlines and to-do lists, work is also a place where the heart is formed.

Picture someone who approaches their job as a calling, not just a paycheck. Each morning, they pause before starting, offering a quiet prayer for wisdom and patience. Throughout the day, they look for small ways to serve—a kind word to a colleague, a moment of encouragement, a willingness to go the extra mile. The habit isn’t flashy, but it shapes the way they see their work and themselves.

In another office, a team begins each week with a moment of silence, inviting everyone to set an intention for the days ahead. The ritual is brief, but it changes the atmosphere. People feel seen, valued, and connected to something larger than their individual tasks. Over time, the habit forms a culture of respect, collaboration, and purpose.

Work can easily become a place of anxiety, competition, and burnout. The pressure to perform, to climb the ladder, to prove oneself—these are habits that shape the heart in ways that can be hard to undo. But it’s possible to choose different practices, to form habits that aim the heart toward meaning rather than mere achievement.

This doesn’t mean ignoring ambition or settling for mediocrity. It means paying attention to the habits that shape desire at work. The way people start and end their day, the rituals of gratitude and celebration, the willingness to rest—these are practices that form the heart for the long haul.

Most people don’t realize how much their work habits shape their sense of self. The drive to be productive, the fear of failure, the longing for recognition—these are desires that are formed, not fixed. By choosing practices that honor dignity, service, and rest, it’s possible to reshape what the heart loves.

Work is more than a means to an end. It’s a place of formation, where the habits of meaning are learned and lived. In the routines of the workday, the heart is shaped—sometimes for good, sometimes for ill. The invitation is to choose practices that form the heart for what truly matters, making work a place of growth, purpose, and love.

## Chapter 9 The Wider World: Public Life, Justice, and the Habits of Hope

Beyond the walls of home and the routines of work, people are shaped by the world they inhabit. The news, the neighborhood, the city streets—all these places form the heart in ways that are easy to miss. In a world marked by injustice, division, and fear, the habits people practice in public life matter more than ever.

Imagine a group of neighbors who decide to clean up a local park. At first, it’s just a way to make the space more beautiful. But as they work side by side, picking up trash and planting flowers, something shifts. They begin to notice each other, to share stories, to dream about what their neighborhood could become. The habit of showing up, of caring for a shared space, forms a sense of hope and responsibility.

In another city, a congregation partners with a local shelter to serve meals to those in need. The work is sometimes messy, often humbling. But over time, the habit of service changes both the servers and the served. It teaches that every person has dignity, that justice is not just an idea but a practice, and that hope is built through small, faithful acts.

Public life is full of competing stories—stories of scarcity, fear, and self-protection. It’s easy to be shaped by these narratives, to retreat into cynicism or apathy. But the habits people practice in the wider world can form the heart for hope, courage, and justice.

This kind of formation doesn’t happen by accident. It requires intention—a willingness to show up, to listen, to act. The way people vote, the causes they support, the way they treat strangers—these are habits that shape the heart for the common good.

Most people underestimate the power of these small acts. They assume that real change requires grand gestures or heroic effort. But over time, the quiet habits of justice and hope form a different kind of heart—a heart that believes change is possible, that refuses to give in to despair, and that finds joy in working for the good of all.

The wider world is a place of formation. Its routines, its challenges, its opportunities—all shape what people love and who they become. By choosing habits that aim the heart toward justice, mercy, and hope, it’s possible to be formed for the good of the world.

## Chapter 10 The Long Journey: Grace, Failure, and the Ongoing Formation of Desire

No one’s journey is a straight line. Even with the best intentions, people stumble, fall back into old habits, and lose sight of what matters. The process of being formed by love is slow, sometimes frustrating, and always unfinished. But it’s in the messiness of real life that the deepest lessons are learned.

Picture someone who sets out to build new habits—morning prayer, weekly service, intentional rest. For a while, the practices feel life-giving. But then comes a season of stress, loss, or disappointment. The routines slip, the old desires resurface, and shame creeps in. It’s tempting to give up, to believe that change is impossible.

But the story doesn’t end there. In the midst of failure, there’s an invitation to grace. The willingness to start again, to forgive oneself, to receive help from others—these are habits that form a resilient heart. Over time, it becomes clear that formation isn’t about perfection, but about persistence.

Most people carry wounds—old patterns, regrets, fears that shape what they love. The journey of spiritual formation is not about erasing these scars, but about learning to love in and through them. The habits of confession, forgiveness, and gratitude become tools for healing, reminders that the heart can be reshaped, no matter how many times it’s been broken.

It’s easy to focus on the visible results—the changed behavior, the new routines, the outward signs of growth. But the real work happens beneath the surface, in the slow, hidden process of desire being reshaped. The journey is long, and there will be setbacks. But with grace, patience, and community, the heart can be formed for love.

The invitation is to keep going—to trust that every small act, every new habit, every moment of grace is part of the story. The journey is not about arriving, but about becoming. And in the end, it’s love that shapes the heart, one day at a time.

## Conclusion: Living a Life Shaped by Love

If you’ve followed this journey, you know by now that the heart is not formed by accident. The things you love, the habits you keep, the stories you live—all are shaping you, day by day. The good news is that you are not powerless in this process. With intention, patience, and grace, you can choose practices that aim your heart toward what’s truly good.

**Key Takeaways:**
- The heart is shaped more by habits than by ideas or intentions.
- Everyday rituals at home, work, and in community form what you love most.
- Cultural routines compete for your desire—notice which stories you’re living.
- Worship and spiritual practices are training grounds for reshaping your heart.
- Grace and persistence matter more than perfection on the journey of formation.

Remember, you don’t have to overhaul your life overnight. Start small. Light a candle at dinner, pause for a moment of gratitude, reach out to a friend, or serve in your neighborhood. These simple acts, repeated over time, will quietly reshape what you love and who you become. When you stumble—and you will—let grace be your companion. The journey is long, but you are not alone. Every day is a new chance to choose love, to form your heart for what matters, and to become the person you were made to be. The world needs hearts shaped by love—yours included. So step forward, gently and bravely, and let your habits tell a better story.
