## Introduction

History often shapes not only collective memory but also the very identity of a nation, weaving together stories of triumph, struggle, and transformation. Yet, beneath the surface of familiar narratives, uncomfortable truths and overlooked perspectives linger, challenging the comfort of accepted wisdom. Lies My Teacher Told Me: Everything Your American History Textbook Got Wrong by James W. Loewen delves into the heart of these hidden realities, questioning the sanitized and sometimes misleading versions of American history that have long been presented in classrooms. The journey through America’s past becomes a search for authenticity, where myths are confronted and the complexity of historical events is brought to light.

As the layers of conventional storytelling are peeled back, a more nuanced and inclusive portrait of the nation emerges—one that acknowledges the voices and experiences often left out of mainstream accounts. The exploration moves beyond rote memorization of dates and names, inviting a deeper engagement with the forces that have shaped society, culture, and power. Through this lens, the past is not a static record but a living dialogue, urging a reconsideration of what has been taught and why. The path forward is illuminated by a willingness to question, to seek understanding, and to embrace the full spectrum of America’s historical legacy.

## Chapter 1 The Hidden Patterns in School History Lessons

It’s a familiar scene: a classroom filled with restless students, eyes drifting toward the clock as a teacher recites the day’s history lesson. The words on the page seem settled, final—names, dates, and events lined up in neat order. For many, history class feels like a parade of facts, each one presented as if it’s the only way the story could be told. But beneath the surface, something more complicated is happening. The stories chosen for these textbooks, and the way they’re told, shape how people see themselves and their country. Most never realize how much is left out, or how much is quietly rearranged to fit a certain picture.

In countless schools, the first big story students encounter is about a man who sailed across the ocean centuries ago. The tale of Christopher Columbus is often the opening act, setting the tone for what’s to come. The story usually begins with a bold explorer, driven by curiosity and courage, who “discovers” a new world. The details are familiar: three ships, a long voyage, and a moment of triumph as land appears on the horizon. The lesson seems simple—Columbus was a hero, and his journey changed everything.

But for one student, sitting in the back row, something doesn’t add up. The textbook says Columbus was the first to reach the Americas, but a quick glance at a library book hints that others may have arrived before him. The textbook describes friendly meetings with the people already living there, but the library book mentions violence and disease. The student wonders why the stories don’t match.

This quiet confusion is more common than most realize. The way history is taught often leaves out the messier parts—the mistakes, the conflicts, the voices of those who lost as well as those who won. The story of Columbus, for example, rarely mentions the suffering of the indigenous people he encountered, or the consequences of his arrival. Instead, the focus stays on his bravery and the “discovery” itself, as if nothing and no one existed before he arrived.

It’s not just Columbus. Throughout the curriculum, certain patterns repeat. Textbooks tend to highlight the achievements of a few well-known figures, while downplaying or ignoring the experiences of others. The stories are smoothed out, made safe and easy to digest. Controversial topics—like slavery, racism, or the struggles of ordinary people—are often glossed over or left out entirely. The result is a version of history that feels settled and unchanging, as if the past was always meant to unfold this way.

Most students accept these stories without question. After all, the textbook is supposed to be the authority. But for those who look a little closer, it becomes clear that history is not just a collection of facts. It’s a series of choices—about what to include, what to leave out, and how to frame the events that shaped the world. The stories told in school are not the only stories that could be told. They are, in many ways, a reflection of the values and priorities of those who write them.

Over time, this selective storytelling shapes how people see their country and themselves. It creates a sense of national identity, built on a foundation of shared myths and half-remembered facts. The stories become part of the culture, passed down from one generation to the next. Most never stop to ask whose voices are missing, or what truths have been hidden in the margins.

For the student in the back row, the realization is unsettling. If the story of Columbus is incomplete, what else might be missing? What other parts of history have been smoothed over or left out? The questions linger, quietly challenging the certainty of the textbook’s version of events.

This is where the real journey begins—not with the arrival of a ship on a distant shore, but with the decision to look beyond the surface and ask what lies beneath. The past is not as simple as it seems, and the stories told in school are only one piece of a much larger puzzle.

## Chapter 2 The Making of American Heroes and the Stories We Tell

In the world of school history, certain names stand out like monuments. These are the figures whose faces appear in portraits, whose deeds are recited in classrooms, and whose lives are held up as examples to follow. The stories of these heroes are told again and again, each time a little more polished, a little more certain. But behind the familiar tales, there are deeper truths—stories of complexity, contradiction, and sometimes, uncomfortable realities.

Take the story of Helen Keller. Most people remember her as the determined young woman who overcame the challenges of being blind and deaf, learning to communicate with the help of a patient teacher. The textbook version ends there, with a message of perseverance and hope. But for those who look further, another side of Keller’s life emerges. As she grew older, she became a passionate advocate for social justice, speaking out against poverty, inequality, and war. She challenged the status quo, aligning herself with causes that made many uncomfortable. Yet, these parts of her story rarely make it into the classroom. The focus stays on her early triumphs, leaving out the ways she questioned the world around her.

This pattern repeats with other figures. Woodrow Wilson is often remembered as a champion of democracy, the president who led the country through World War I and helped shape the League of Nations. But the textbooks tend to skip over his record on race—his support for segregation and his willingness to turn a blind eye to injustice. The story is simplified, the rough edges smoothed away.

For many students, these omissions go unnoticed. The stories are presented as settled fact, with little room for doubt or debate. But for those who pay attention, the gaps become hard to ignore. The heroes of history are not as simple as they seem. They are human—capable of greatness, but also of failure and contradiction.

In one classroom, a teacher tries to spark a conversation about these complexities. She asks her students to think about what makes someone a hero. Is it their achievements, or the values they stand for? Can a person be both admirable and flawed? The discussion is hesitant at first, but soon the students begin to share their own thoughts. Some are surprised to learn about the parts of Keller’s life that were left out of the textbook. Others struggle with the idea that a president celebrated for his leadership could also have supported policies that harmed so many.

As the conversation unfolds, it becomes clear that the stories told in school are only part of the picture. The real lives of these figures are more complicated, more interesting, and sometimes more troubling than the versions found in textbooks. By focusing only on their achievements, the curriculum misses an opportunity to teach something deeper—that history is made by real people, with all their strengths and weaknesses.

This realization can be unsettling. It challenges the idea that the past is a simple story of progress and heroism. But it also opens the door to a more honest and meaningful understanding of history. When students are encouraged to look beyond the surface, to ask questions and seek out the full story, they begin to see the past in a new light. They learn that history is not just about memorizing names and dates, but about understanding the choices people made, and the consequences of those choices.

## Chapter 3 The Stories Left Out—Race, Conflict, and the Silences in History

There’s a quiet tension that runs through many history lessons—a sense that some topics are too difficult, too controversial, or too painful to discuss. In classrooms across the country, the stories of race and conflict are often pushed to the margins, if they’re mentioned at all. The result is a version of history that feels safe and comfortable, but also incomplete.

For one group of students, the lesson on the Civil War is a turning point. The textbook describes the conflict as a struggle over states’ rights, with only a passing mention of slavery. The words are carefully chosen, avoiding anything that might spark discomfort or debate. But for some in the room, the omissions are glaring. They know from family stories and community history that the war was about much more than abstract principles. The reality of slavery, the violence of racism, and the long struggle for freedom are all part of their own heritage. The textbook’s silence feels like a denial of their experience.

This kind of erasure is not accidental. Over the years, textbook writers and publishers have faced pressure to avoid topics that might upset parents, school boards, or politicians. The result is a curriculum that tiptoes around the most difficult parts of the past. The stories of indigenous people, enslaved Africans, immigrants, and others who faced discrimination are often reduced to a few paragraphs, or left out entirely. The focus stays on the achievements of the powerful, while the struggles of the marginalized are pushed aside.

In one school, a teacher tries to fill in the gaps. She brings in stories from outside the textbook—accounts of resistance, survival, and resilience. She asks her students to imagine what it would have been like to live through these events, to see the world through the eyes of those whose voices are rarely heard. The students are hesitant at first, unsure how to respond. But as they listen, something shifts. The past becomes more real, more immediate. The stories of struggle and injustice are no longer abstract—they are part of the world they live in.

For many, this is the first time they’ve been asked to think about history in this way. The experience is both challenging and empowering. It becomes clear that the stories left out of the textbook are just as important as the ones that are included. The silences in history are not empty—they are filled with meaning, with the lives and experiences of real people.

The decision to avoid difficult topics has consequences. It shapes how students see themselves and their place in the world. When the stories of conflict and injustice are left out, it becomes easier to believe that the past was simpler, more harmonious than it really was. It becomes harder to understand the roots of the problems that still exist today.

But when the silences are broken, something powerful happens. Students begin to see the connections between past and present. They realize that history is not just a story about other people, in another time. It is a living thing, shaped by the choices and actions of those who came before. The classroom becomes a place for honest conversation, for grappling with the complexities of the world.

## Chapter 4 The Power of Myths and the Shaping of National Identity

Every country tells stories about itself. These stories are more than just a collection of facts—they are the myths that shape how people see their nation, their community, and themselves. In the United States, the stories told in school history classes play a powerful role in creating a sense of national identity. But these stories are not always as straightforward as they seem.

In one small town, a group of students gathers for a lesson on the founding of the country. The textbook describes the early settlers as brave pioneers, building a new society in the wilderness. The story is familiar—hard work, determination, and a belief in freedom. The students are encouraged to see themselves as the heirs of this tradition, part of a long line of people who overcame adversity to build a better life.

But as the lesson continues, some begin to notice what’s missing. The experiences of indigenous people, whose lands were taken and whose cultures were disrupted, are barely mentioned. The struggles of enslaved Africans, whose labor made much of the country’s wealth possible, are reduced to a few lines. The story is told from a single point of view, with little room for other perspectives.

This selective storytelling is not unique to one classroom. Across the country, textbooks tend to present a version of history that emphasizes unity, progress, and shared values. The conflicts, contradictions, and injustices that shaped the nation are often downplayed or ignored. The result is a sense of national identity built on a foundation of myths—stories that are comforting, but not always true.

For many, these myths are a source of pride. They offer a sense of belonging, a feeling of connection to something larger than themselves. But for others, the stories ring hollow. The gaps and omissions are too big to ignore. The reality of the past is more complicated, more painful, and more interesting than the myths suggest.

In one family, the stories passed down from generation to generation tell a different version of history. They speak of struggle and survival, of dreams deferred and promises broken. These stories are not found in the textbook, but they are just as real, just as important. For those who carry these memories, the myths of national identity can feel like a denial of their own experience.

The power of these myths is hard to overstate. They shape how people see themselves and their place in the world. They influence how communities respond to new challenges, and how they remember the past. When the stories told in school are incomplete or misleading, it becomes harder to understand the true nature of the country’s history.

But myths are not unchangeable. They can be questioned, revised, and expanded. When students are encouraged to look beyond the surface, to seek out the stories that have been left out, they begin to see the past in a new light. The myths of national identity become more complex, more honest, and more inclusive.

## Chapter 5 Why Honest History Matters—Critical Thinking and Civic Responsibility

There’s a moment in every classroom when a student asks a question that doesn’t have an easy answer. Maybe it’s about a contradiction in the textbook, or a story that doesn’t quite make sense. The teacher pauses, unsure how to respond. The rest of the class waits, sensing that something important is at stake.

This is the heart of what it means to study history—not just to memorize facts, but to think deeply about the world and one’s place in it. When students are encouraged to ask questions, to challenge the stories they’ve been told, they begin to develop the skills they need to be active, engaged citizens.

In one school, a group of students works on a project about the civil rights movement. They start with the textbook, but soon realize that the story is incomplete. The book mentions a few famous leaders, but says little about the thousands of ordinary people who risked everything for justice. The students decide to interview members of their own community, to hear the stories that aren’t found in the official version of history. The experience is transformative. They learn that history is not just something that happened long ago—it is alive, shaped by the choices and actions of real people.

This kind of learning is not always easy. It can be uncomfortable to confront the darker parts of the past, or to realize that the stories told in school are not the whole truth. But it is also empowering. When students learn to think critically, to question sources, and to seek out multiple perspectives, they become more than just passive recipients of information. They become active participants in the ongoing conversation about what kind of society they want to create.

The importance of honest history goes beyond the classroom. It shapes how people understand the world, how they relate to others, and how they respond to the challenges of the present. When the past is presented as a simple story of progress and heroism, it becomes harder to recognize the problems that still exist. But when the full truth is faced—the conflicts, the injustices, the struggles for justice—people are better equipped to make sense of the world and to work for change.

In one community, a group of parents comes together to talk about what their children are learning in school. They share their own experiences, their hopes and fears for the future. Some worry that teaching the full truth about the past will divide the community, or make students feel ashamed. Others argue that honest history is the only way to build a more just and inclusive society. The conversation is difficult, but necessary. It becomes clear that the stories told in school matter—not just for students, but for everyone.

## Chapter 6 The Machinery Behind the Textbooks—Who Decides What Gets Told

It’s easy to imagine that the stories in schoolbooks are simply the truth, handed down from the past. But behind every page, there’s a web of decisions, negotiations, and sometimes, quiet battles. The process of creating a history textbook is less like writing a diary and more like assembling a puzzle—one where some pieces are left out on purpose.

Picture a group of editors gathered around a long table, stacks of drafts and notes in front of them. They’re not just historians; they’re also businesspeople, marketers, and sometimes, politicians in disguise. Each one brings their own priorities. Some want to avoid controversy, others want to appeal to the largest number of schools, and a few are genuinely passionate about accuracy. But the loudest voices often belong to those who worry about sales and public reaction.

In one publishing house, a new textbook is up for review. The editors debate how to describe the Vietnam War. Should they mention the protests, the draft, the stories of those who refused to fight? One editor worries that including too much detail will upset parents in certain states. Another points out that some school boards have already rejected books for being “unpatriotic.” The final version ends up watered down—just enough to pass through the gauntlet of approval committees, but missing the rawness of what really happened.

Most students never see this side of things. They open their books and trust that what’s inside is the whole story. But the reality is, every chapter is shaped by invisible hands—by the pressures of politics, economics, and public opinion. In some states, a single committee can decide what millions of children will learn. In others, a vocal group of parents can have a chapter rewritten or removed.

For teachers, this can be frustrating. One high school teacher, passionate about teaching the truth, finds herself tiptoeing around certain topics. She wants her students to understand the full story, but the textbook is her only official resource. She brings in outside articles, primary sources, and even invites guest speakers, but she knows not every teacher has the freedom or support to do the same.

The machinery behind textbooks is rarely discussed, but it shapes the way generations understand their world. The stories that make it into the classroom are not just about the past—they’re about what a society is willing to face, and what it would rather forget. The process is messy, imperfect, and often driven by fear of backlash rather than a love of truth.

Over time, this system creates a kind of quiet consensus. The most difficult stories are softened, the most controversial figures are made safe, and the rough edges of history are sanded down. The result is a version of the past that feels comfortable, but not quite real.

For those who care about honest history, this is a call to action. The stories in textbooks matter, but so do the stories that don’t make it in. By understanding how these decisions are made, students and teachers can begin to ask better questions—and demand better answers.

## Chapter 7 The Cost of Silence—What Happens When We Don’t Tell the Whole Story

There’s a weight that comes with silence—a heaviness that settles in when important truths are left unspoken. In classrooms across the country, this silence is felt most deeply by those whose stories are missing from the official narrative. The cost isn’t just academic; it’s personal, emotional, and sometimes, generational.

Imagine a student who never sees their family’s history reflected in the lessons. Each year, the same names and faces appear in the textbook, while the struggles and triumphs of their own community are nowhere to be found. Over time, this absence sends a message: some stories matter more than others. Some people belong in the story of the nation, while others are left in the shadows.

For many, this silence leads to a quiet sense of alienation. A young person might wonder why their ancestors’ contributions are ignored, or why the pain their family endured is never acknowledged. The classroom becomes a place of distance, not connection. The lessons feel incomplete, and the student’s sense of belonging begins to fray.

But the cost of silence isn’t limited to those left out. Even students whose stories are front and center in the textbook pay a price. They grow up with a version of history that is neat and tidy, free from the messiness of real life. They miss the chance to grapple with complexity, to understand the struggles that shaped the world they live in. When difficult truths are hidden, empathy and understanding are lost.

In one school, a teacher decides to break the silence. She shares a story about a local event that never made it into the textbook—a protest, a tragedy, a moment of courage. The students listen, at first surprised, then moved. For some, it’s the first time they’ve heard their own history spoken aloud. For others, it’s a window into a world they never knew existed. The classroom feels different—more honest, more alive.

The cost of silence is also felt in the wider world. When a society refuses to face its past, old wounds fester. Misunderstandings grow, and the same mistakes are repeated. The stories we don’t tell become the ghosts that haunt our present.

But silence is not inevitable. It can be broken, one story at a time. When teachers and students dare to speak the truth, the classroom becomes a place of healing and discovery. The cost of silence is high, but the rewards of honesty are even greater.

## Chapter 8 The Ripple Effect—How Textbook History Shapes Society

The stories told in school don’t just stay in the classroom. They ripple outward, shaping the way people see themselves, their neighbors, and the world. The version of history learned as a child becomes the lens through which the present is understood—and the future imagined.

In one community, a heated debate breaks out over a new monument in the town square. Some see it as a tribute to local heritage; others see it as a painful reminder of injustice. The arguments go back and forth, but beneath the surface, something deeper is at play. The stories people learned in school—about who counts as a hero, what counts as progress, and whose suffering matters—shape every word of the debate.

For many, the lessons of the past feel settled. They remember what they were taught: the nation was built by brave pioneers, the struggles of the marginalized were unfortunate but necessary, and progress is always forward. But for others, the gaps in the story are impossible to ignore. They know that the past is more complicated, that the pain of injustice lingers, and that progress is never guaranteed.

The ripple effect of textbook history can be seen in the way people vote, the policies they support, and the way they treat one another. When the stories are incomplete, misunderstandings grow. Stereotypes take root, and empathy withers. It becomes easier to dismiss the struggles of others, or to believe that the problems of the past are long gone.

But the ripple effect can also work in the other direction. When students learn to question the stories they’ve been told, to seek out new perspectives, and to listen to voices that have been silenced, something shifts. The community becomes more open, more willing to face difficult truths. The debates become richer, the solutions more creative.

In one town, a group of students organizes a project to collect oral histories from elders in the community. They record stories of migration, of struggle, of hope. The project brings people together, bridging divides that once seemed insurmountable. The ripple effect of honest history is felt in every conversation, every new connection.

The stories told in school matter—not just for the individual, but for the whole society. They shape the way people see themselves and each other. When the stories are honest, the ripples spread outward, creating a world that is more just, more compassionate, and more real.

## Chapter 9 Facing the Past—Reckoning with Injustice and Moving Forward

There comes a moment when a community must decide whether to look away from its past or to face it head-on. This reckoning is never easy. It means confronting painful truths, acknowledging harm, and asking what it would take to make things right.

In one city, a group of high school students learns about a long-ago event—a riot, a massacre, a moment of violence that was never mentioned in their textbooks. The discovery is shocking. Some feel angry, others ashamed, and a few are simply confused. Why was this story hidden? What does it mean for their community today?

The teacher encourages them to dig deeper. They visit the local library, talk to elders, and read old newspaper clippings. The more they learn, the more complicated the story becomes. There are no easy answers, no simple heroes or villains. The past is messy, filled with pain and resilience, cruelty and courage.

As the students share what they’ve learned, the classroom becomes a place of reckoning. Some struggle with guilt, others with anger. But through the discomfort, something new emerges—a sense of responsibility. The students realize that facing the past is not about assigning blame, but about understanding how the choices of yesterday shape the world of today.

In the wider community, the conversation is difficult. Some want to move on, to forget. Others demand justice, reparations, or at least an honest acknowledgment of what happened. The debate is raw, but necessary. It becomes clear that healing is only possible when the truth is faced.

Reckoning with injustice is not just about the past. It’s about the future. When a community dares to tell the whole story, it opens the door to new possibilities. Old wounds can begin to heal, and new relationships can be built. The process is never easy, but it is the only way forward.

For the students in the classroom, the lesson is clear. History is not just something to be learned—it is something to be lived. By facing the past, they become part of the ongoing story of their community, shaping a future that is more honest, more just, and more hopeful.

## Chapter 10 The Courage to Change—Teachers, Students, and the New Storytellers

Change rarely comes from the top down. More often, it begins with a single voice—a teacher who refuses to stick to the script, a student who asks a difficult question, a parent who demands more from the curriculum. These are the new storytellers, the ones who dare to imagine a different way of teaching and learning history.

In one school, a young teacher decides to rewrite her lesson plans. She knows the textbook is incomplete, so she brings in stories from her own family, from the community, from voices that have been left out. The students are skeptical at first, but soon they are drawn in. They begin to see themselves in the stories, to ask questions, to challenge what they thought they knew.

One student, inspired by a lesson on civil rights, decides to interview her grandmother about growing up in the segregated South. The conversation is difficult, but eye-opening. The student shares what she learns with the class, and suddenly, history feels real—alive, urgent, and deeply personal.

In another classroom, a teacher invites students to create their own history projects. Some choose to research local events, others to explore their family’s journey to the country. The projects are messy, imperfect, and sometimes controversial. But they are also honest, creative, and full of life.

The courage to change is not just about rewriting lesson plans. It’s about creating a culture where questions are welcomed, where difficult truths are faced, and where every student feels seen and heard. It’s about recognizing that history is not just something to be memorized, but something to be made.

The new storytellers are everywhere—teachers, students, parents, and community members who refuse to accept the old myths. They are building a new kind of classroom, one where the full story is told, and where every voice matters.

## Chapter 11 Reimagining the Curriculum—Toward a More Honest and Inclusive History

The journey toward honest history is not a straight line. It’s a process of reimagining, of questioning old assumptions, and of daring to tell new stories. In schools across the country, this work is already underway.

In one district, a group of teachers comes together to review the curriculum. They ask hard questions: Whose stories are missing? What voices have been silenced? How can the lessons be made more inclusive, more honest, more real? The process is slow, sometimes frustrating, but also inspiring. New materials are brought in, old lessons are revised, and students are invited to share their own experiences.

In another school, students lead the way. They organize a history fair, inviting community members to share stories from their own lives. The event is a celebration of diversity, a reminder that history is not just about famous names and big events, but about the everyday lives of ordinary people.

The work of reimagining the curriculum is never finished. There will always be new stories to tell, new voices to hear, new truths to face. But each step forward brings the classroom closer to a place of honesty, empathy, and understanding.

For teachers, the challenge is to balance the demands of the curriculum with the needs of their students. For students, the challenge is to ask questions, to seek out new perspectives, and to never stop learning. For communities, the challenge is to support honest history, even when it is uncomfortable.

The journey is long, but the rewards are great. A more honest and inclusive history is not just good for the classroom—it is good for the world.

## Chapter 12 The Ongoing Conversation—History as a Living Dialogue

History is not a finished story. It is a living conversation, shaped by the questions we ask, the stories we tell, and the truths we are willing to face. In every classroom, every community, the conversation continues.

In one town, a group of students organizes a public forum to discuss the legacy of a controversial local figure. The event is tense, with strong opinions on all sides. But as people share their stories, something shifts. The conversation becomes less about winning and more about understanding. Old wounds are acknowledged, new connections are made.

In another city, a teacher starts a history club, inviting students to explore topics not covered in the textbook. The club becomes a space for curiosity, for debate, for discovery. Students learn that history is not just about the past—it is about the present, and the future.

The ongoing conversation is not always easy. It requires courage, humility, and a willingness to listen. But it is also a source of hope. When people come together to share their stories, to face difficult truths, and to imagine new possibilities, history comes alive.

The classroom is just the beginning. The real work happens in the world—at the dinner table, in the community center, on the streets. The conversation continues, shaping the way people see themselves, their neighbors, and their world.

## Conclusion: Carrying the Torch—How to Live and Learn with Honest History

The journey through the stories and silences of history is not just an academic exercise—it’s a call to action. The lessons of *Lies My Teacher Told Me* are not meant to leave us cynical or defeated, but to inspire us to become more thoughtful, engaged, and compassionate citizens. Honest history is not about tearing down heroes or dwelling on the past’s darkest moments. It’s about seeing the full picture, with all its complexity, and using that understanding to build a better future.

**Key Takeaways:**
- The stories in textbooks are shaped by choices, pressures, and omissions.
- Silence about difficult truths creates distance, misunderstanding, and lost empathy.
- Honest history empowers students to think critically and act with compassion.
- Teachers, students, and communities can all be storytellers and truth-seekers.
- Facing the past is the first step toward healing and building a just society.

If you’re reading this, you’re already part of the ongoing conversation. The work of honest history is never finished, and it doesn’t belong to experts alone. It belongs to everyone who cares enough to ask questions, to listen, and to share their own story. You don’t have to be a teacher to make a difference. Start with your own family, your own community, your own curiosity. Seek out the stories that have been left out, and be willing to face the discomfort that comes with the truth. Remember, history is not just about what happened—it’s about what we choose to remember, and what we choose to do next. The torch is in your hands. Carry it forward, with courage and care, and help shape a world where every story matters.
