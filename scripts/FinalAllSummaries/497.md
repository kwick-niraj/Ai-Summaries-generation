## Introduction

A world shaped by systemic injustice and the relentless pursuit of dignity comes alive through the lived experiences of those who refuse to be silenced. The journey of Patrisse Khan-Cullors, co-founder of the Black Lives Matter movement, unfolds against a backdrop of racial inequality, state violence, and the enduring hope for transformation. The realities of growing up Black in America—navigating poverty, police brutality, and the constant threat of being misunderstood or maligned—reveal the personal cost of activism and the courage required to demand change. The story is not just one of struggle, but of love, resilience, and the unbreakable bonds of family and community that sustain the fight for justice.

When They Call You a Terrorist: A Black Lives Matter Memoir traces the evolution of a movement born from pain and necessity, illuminating the humanity behind headlines and hashtags. The narrative weaves together moments of vulnerability and strength, exploring how personal trauma intersects with collective resistance. Through the lens of Khan-Cullors’ life, the complexities of identity, belonging, and the power of speaking truth to power are brought into sharp focus. The call for justice becomes both intimate and universal, inviting reflection on what it means to be seen, heard, and valued in a world that too often denies those very things. The path forward emerges through solidarity, hope, and the unwavering belief in the possibility of a more just future.

## Chapter 1 Growing Up in a World Shaped by Injustice

Childhood memories often carry the weight of the world, even when the world seems too big to understand. In a neighborhood where helicopters buzzed overhead and police cars were as common as ice cream trucks, a young girl learned early that safety was something other people took for granted. The streets of her Los Angeles community were alive with laughter and play, but also with the constant hum of worry—worry that someone you loved might not come home, worry that the world outside your door could turn dangerous in a heartbeat.

Family was the anchor in this uncertain world. A mother, always working, juggled multiple jobs to keep the lights on and food on the table. She was a force of nature, determined to shield her children from the harshest blows of poverty and discrimination. The father, though not always present, left a mark—his absence and presence both shaping the way his children saw themselves and the world. Siblings became confidants and protectors, each one learning to navigate the maze of school, friendships, and the unspoken rules of survival.

In this environment, the lines between right and wrong often blurred. The local schools, underfunded and overcrowded, seemed more interested in control than in nurturing curiosity. Teachers sometimes looked through students rather than at them, missing the spark of potential hidden behind tired eyes. The police, meant to serve and protect, were a constant presence, but not a comforting one. Their arrival often signaled trouble, not help.

It became clear, even to a child, that the world was not built for everyone equally. Some families seemed to glide through life, untouched by the daily struggles that defined others. The reasons for this were never explained in school, but they were felt in every interaction—with neighbors, with authority figures, with the world at large. The message was subtle but persistent: some lives mattered more than others.

Despite these challenges, there was joy. Family gatherings, music, and moments of laughter offered relief from the daily grind. The community, though battered by poverty and systemic neglect, found ways to celebrate life. People looked out for each other, sharing what little they had and offering comfort in times of need. These moments of connection became a lifeline, a reminder that love and resilience could flourish even in the harshest conditions.

As the years passed, the realities of systemic racism became impossible to ignore. Friends and family members were swept up by the criminal justice system, often for minor infractions that seemed to carry outsized consequences. The fear of losing someone to violence or incarceration was ever-present, shaping decisions and dreams. Yet, through it all, the belief in a better future persisted—a quiet hope that things could change, that justice was possible, even if it seemed out of reach.

## Chapter 2 Family Bonds and the Struggle for Dignity

Every family has its own rhythm, a unique way of moving through the world. In this household, love was expressed through small acts—meals cooked after long shifts, stories shared at the end of a hard day, a gentle touch on the shoulder when words failed. The mother, a pillar of strength, carried the weight of the family’s hopes and fears. She worked tirelessly, often sacrificing her own needs to ensure her children had what they needed to survive.

The siblings, each with their own dreams and struggles, leaned on each other for support. One brother, sensitive and creative, struggled to fit into a world that seemed determined to misunderstand him. His encounters with the police were frequent and often traumatic, leaving scars that were slow to heal. Another sibling, fiercely protective, took on the role of guardian, determined to shield the family from harm.

The father’s absence was a wound that never fully healed. When he was present, his love was evident, but his struggles with addiction and the criminal justice system often pulled him away. The family learned to adapt, finding strength in each other and in the extended network of relatives and friends who stepped in to help.

Poverty was a constant companion, shaping every decision and limiting opportunities. The family moved frequently, chasing affordable rent and safer neighborhoods. Each move brought new challenges—new schools, new friends, new threats. The children learned to be adaptable, to find joy in small victories and to hold onto hope even when the odds seemed stacked against them.

Despite these hardships, the family refused to be defined by their circumstances. They celebrated birthdays and holidays with whatever resources they had, turning even the simplest meal into a feast. Music and laughter filled the home, offering a respite from the outside world. The mother’s unwavering belief in her children’s potential became a source of strength, inspiring them to dream beyond the limitations imposed by society.

The struggle for dignity was ongoing. Encounters with the welfare system, the police, and the schools were often dehumanizing, reducing the family to statistics and stereotypes. Yet, through it all, they held onto their sense of self-worth, refusing to let the world’s judgments define them. The bonds of family, forged in adversity, became a source of resilience—a reminder that love could survive even the harshest conditions.

## Chapter 3 Discovering Identity and the Power of Community

Adolescence is a time of searching, of trying on different identities to see what fits. For a young person growing up in a world that often felt hostile, this search was both urgent and fraught. The question of who you are becomes tangled with the question of who the world allows you to be. In a community where difference was often punished, finding a sense of belonging was both a challenge and a necessity.

School offered little refuge. The curriculum rarely reflected the realities of students’ lives, and the rules seemed designed to control rather than inspire. For those who didn’t fit the mold—who were too loud, too quiet, too different—the consequences could be severe. Suspensions and expulsions were common, and the path from the classroom to the juvenile justice system was well-worn.

Yet, even in this environment, there were moments of connection. A teacher who took the time to listen, a friend who offered a shoulder to cry on, a community center that provided a safe space to explore new interests. These small acts of kindness became lifelines, reminders that not everyone saw you as a problem to be solved.

The search for identity extended beyond the classroom. Questions about sexuality, gender, and self-worth loomed large. In a world that often demanded conformity, embracing difference became an act of resistance. Finding others who shared similar experiences—who understood the pain of being misunderstood—offered a sense of belonging that had long been missing.

Community organizing became a way to channel frustration into action. Local groups, often led by women and queer people of color, provided a blueprint for what collective power could look like. Through workshops, protests, and mutual aid, the community learned to advocate for itself, to demand respect and resources from those in power.

It became clear that the personal was political. The struggles faced by individuals were not isolated incidents, but part of a larger pattern of systemic injustice. By coming together, the community could challenge the narratives that painted them as criminals or threats. They could demand to be seen as fully human, deserving of dignity and respect.

The journey toward self-acceptance was ongoing. There were setbacks and moments of doubt, but also breakthroughs and moments of joy. Embracing one’s identity, in all its complexity, became a source of strength—a reminder that difference could be a source of power, not just pain.

## Chapter 4 The Impact of Mass Incarceration and Police Violence

The shadow of the criminal justice system loomed large over the community. For many families, the threat of losing a loved one to incarceration was a constant worry. The reasons were often arbitrary—a missed court date, a minor infraction, a case of mistaken identity. The consequences, however, were devastating. Families were torn apart, children left without parents, dreams derailed by a system that seemed designed to punish rather than rehabilitate.

One brother’s struggles with mental health made him especially vulnerable. Encounters with the police were frequent and often escalated quickly. Instead of receiving care and support, he was met with suspicion and force. The family fought tirelessly to advocate for him, navigating a maze of bureaucracy and indifference. Each setback was a reminder of how little value the system placed on Black lives, especially those living with mental illness.

The impact of incarceration extended beyond the individual. Families were forced to navigate the stigma and financial strain that came with having a loved one behind bars. Visits to jails and courtrooms became a regular part of life, each one a painful reminder of the system’s reach. The emotional toll was immense, but so was the determination to fight back.

Stories of police violence were all too common. Friends and neighbors recounted encounters that ended in injury or humiliation. The fear of being stopped by the police was ever-present, shaping the way people moved through the world. Simple acts—walking to the store, driving to work—became fraught with danger.

It became clear that these experiences were not isolated incidents, but part of a larger pattern of systemic racism. The criminal justice system, far from being a neutral arbiter, was deeply implicated in the perpetuation of inequality. The language of “law and order” was often used to justify the targeting of Black communities, painting them as inherently dangerous or criminal.

Despite these challenges, the community refused to be silenced. Organizing efforts intensified, with families and activists demanding accountability and reform. Protests, petitions, and public forums became tools for raising awareness and building solidarity. The fight for justice was exhausting, but it was also empowering—a reminder that collective action could make a difference.

Through it all, the importance of healing and self-care became evident. The trauma inflicted by the system was real, and addressing it required more than just policy changes. It required a commitment to supporting each other, to building spaces where people could process their pain and find hope for the future.

## Chapter 5 The Birth of a Movement Rooted in Love and Resistance

Sometimes, a single moment can spark a movement. The news of yet another unarmed Black person killed by police sent shockwaves through the community. The grief was overwhelming, but so was the anger. People gathered in living rooms, on street corners, and online, searching for ways to channel their pain into action. The question on everyone’s mind was simple: How much longer would this be allowed to continue?

Social media became a powerful tool for organizing. Messages of solidarity and outrage spread quickly, connecting people across cities and states. The phrase “Black Lives Matter” emerged as a rallying cry—a simple but profound assertion of humanity in the face of relentless dehumanization. It was a declaration that Black lives, so often treated as disposable, were worthy of love, respect, and protection.

The movement was built on a foundation of love—love for self, for family, for community. It was a love that refused to be passive, that demanded justice and accountability. Organizers drew on the lessons of their own lives, channeling their experiences of loss and resilience into a vision for a better world.

The work was not easy. Activists faced backlash from the media, from politicians, and even from within their own communities. The label of “terrorist” was used to delegitimize their efforts, to paint them as threats rather than as people fighting for justice. But the movement persisted, grounded in the belief that change was possible.

Collective action became a source of healing. Protests and rallies were not just about demanding justice—they were about reclaiming space, about asserting the right to exist and to be heard. The movement created new possibilities for solidarity, bringing together people from different backgrounds and experiences.

It became clear that the fight for justice was not just about changing laws or policies. It was about transforming the way people saw each other, about building a world where everyone’s humanity was recognized and valued. The movement was a testament to the power of ordinary people to create extraordinary change, even in the face of overwhelming odds.

As the movement grew, so did the understanding that activism required care—not just for the cause, but for the people involved. Burnout and trauma were real risks, and sustaining the work meant prioritizing healing and self-care. The movement became a space for both resistance and renewal, a place where people could find strength in each other and in the shared vision of a more just world.

## Chapter 6 The Movement Grows: Facing the World’s Gaze

When a movement begins to catch fire, the world starts to watch. Suddenly, what once felt like a private pain—shared in whispered conversations and late-night texts—becomes a public spectacle. The phrase “Black Lives Matter” echoed far beyond the neighborhoods where it was born, carried by voices across the country and around the globe. For those at the heart of the movement, this new attention was both exhilarating and daunting.

There’s a strange vulnerability in being seen. One organizer, who had always felt invisible in the eyes of authority, now found her face on television screens and magazine covers. Strangers reached out with messages of support, but also with threats and accusations. The label of “terrorist” stung, not just because it was false, but because it twisted the meaning of the work—turning a call for love and justice into something dangerous in the public imagination.

The media, hungry for stories, often missed the heart of the movement. They focused on moments of conflict, on broken windows and raised voices, rather than on the quiet acts of care that sustained the work. It became clear that telling your own story was essential. If you didn’t, someone else would, and they might get it all wrong.

Inside the movement, there were growing pains. People disagreed about tactics, about messaging, about how to respond to criticism. Some wanted to move quickly, to seize the moment; others urged caution, worried about burnout and backlash. The pressure was intense, and the stakes felt impossibly high.

But through it all, the sense of purpose remained. Organizers met in kitchens and community centers, planning marches and vigils, writing statements and press releases. They leaned on each other, sharing meals and laughter, holding space for grief and frustration. The work was exhausting, but it was also a lifeline—a way to turn pain into power.

As the movement grew, so did the understanding that real change would take time. Laws could be rewritten, but hearts and minds changed more slowly. Still, every protest, every conversation, every act of solidarity was a step forward. The world was watching, and for the first time, it felt like maybe—just maybe—things could be different.

## Chapter 7 The Toll of Activism: Burnout, Loss, and the Need for Healing

There’s a cost to carrying the weight of a movement. For many, the work of activism became all-consuming. Days blurred into nights, and the boundaries between personal life and public struggle grew thin. The constant barrage of news—another shooting, another injustice—made it hard to rest, hard to breathe.

One organizer, who had always been the first to show up and the last to leave, began to feel the cracks. Sleep became elusive, and the joy that once fueled her work was replaced by exhaustion and doubt. Friends noticed the change, urging her to slow down, to take care of herself. But the needs of the movement felt urgent, and stepping back seemed impossible.

Loss was a constant companion. The movement was born out of grief, and that grief never fully went away. Each new tragedy reopened old wounds, reminding everyone of what was at stake. Funerals and memorials became part of the rhythm of life, spaces where sorrow and solidarity mingled.

The pressure to be strong, to keep going no matter what, was immense. But over time, it became clear that no one could do this work alone. Healing was not a luxury—it was a necessity. Organizers began to carve out spaces for rest and reflection, to share their struggles and support each other. Therapy, meditation, and simple acts of self-care became tools for survival.

The movement learned that caring for each other was as important as any protest or petition. A meal shared after a long day, a phone call to check in, a moment of laughter in the midst of chaos—these small acts of kindness became the glue that held everything together.

It became clear that the fight for justice was not just about changing the world, but about changing the way people treated themselves and each other. Healing was part of the work, not separate from it. In caring for themselves, organizers found the strength to keep going, to keep dreaming of a better world.

## Chapter 8 Reclaiming the Narrative: Art, Storytelling, and the Power of Voice

When the world tries to define you, there’s power in telling your own story. For those at the heart of the movement, art and storytelling became tools for survival and resistance. Murals appeared on city walls, songs filled the air at rallies, and poetry gave voice to feelings that were hard to name.

One young artist, who had always felt out of place in school, found her calling painting portraits of those lost to violence. Each brushstroke was an act of remembrance, a way to say: you mattered, you are not forgotten. Her work became a gathering place for the community, a space to mourn and to celebrate.

Social media, once just a way to keep in touch with friends, became a platform for truth-telling. Videos and essays spread stories that mainstream media ignored. People shared their experiences—of fear, of hope, of everyday life in the shadow of injustice. These stories built bridges, connecting people who might never have met otherwise.

It became clear that the movement was about more than protest—it was about culture, about reclaiming the right to define what it meant to be Black, to be human. Art and storytelling offered a way to heal, to imagine new possibilities, to remind each other that joy and beauty could exist even in the midst of struggle.

The power of voice was undeniable. When people spoke their truth—whether in a speech, a song, or a simple conversation—they challenged the narratives that had long been used to justify oppression. They insisted on being seen, not as problems or threats, but as people with dreams, with families, with stories worth telling.

In reclaiming the narrative, the movement found new strength. It became a space where everyone’s voice mattered, where difference was celebrated, and where the future could be imagined together.

## Chapter 9 Building a Future: Policy, Progress, and the Limits of Reform

As the movement matured, the focus shifted from protest to policy. The question became: how do you turn outrage into lasting change? Meetings with lawmakers, city officials, and community leaders became more frequent. The language of demands—defund, invest, transform—entered the mainstream.

Some victories came quickly. Cities pledged to review police practices, to invest in mental health services, to create civilian oversight boards. For a moment, it felt like the world was listening. But progress was uneven, and backlash was swift. Promises were made, then quietly forgotten. Old habits proved hard to break.

One organizer, who had spent years fighting for change, found herself frustrated by the slow pace. She watched as politicians offered words of support, but little action. It became clear that reform was not enough. The roots of injustice ran deep, and real change would require more than new policies—it would require a transformation of values.

Still, there were reasons for hope. Young people, inspired by the movement, ran for office and won. Community groups built new institutions—schools, clinics, mutual aid networks—that reflected their values. The work was slow, often invisible, but it was happening.

The movement learned to celebrate small victories, to find joy in progress even when it fell short of the dream. Change was possible, but it would take time, persistence, and a willingness to imagine something radically different.

It became clear that the fight for justice was not a sprint, but a marathon. The work would continue, carried forward by new voices, new leaders, new dreams.

## Chapter 10 Legacy and the Ongoing Journey Toward Justice

Looking back, it’s easy to see the movement as a series of headlines and hashtags. But for those who lived it, the story is more complicated, more human. It’s a story of families and friendships, of heartbreak and hope, of ordinary people doing extraordinary things.

The label of “terrorist” never fit. What the movement built was rooted in love—in the belief that everyone deserves dignity, safety, and joy. The work was messy, imperfect, and unfinished. But it changed lives, and it changed the world.

The journey toward justice is ongoing. New challenges arise, new injustices demand attention. But the lessons of the movement endure: the power of community, the importance of self-care, the necessity of telling your own story.

For those who come after, the path is clearer. The movement showed what’s possible when people refuse to accept the world as it is, when they insist on dreaming of what could be. The work continues, carried forward by those who believe that justice is not just a word, but a way of life.

## Conclusion: Carrying the Torch Forward

If you’ve walked through these pages, you’ve seen how a single life—shaped by love, loss, and the relentless pursuit of dignity—can spark a movement that changes the world. The story of “When They Call You a Terrorist” is not just about one person or one moment; it’s about the power of ordinary people to demand extraordinary change, even when the odds seem insurmountable.

As you move forward, keep these lessons close:

- Real change begins with honest storytelling and the courage to be seen.
- Community care and self-healing are as vital as public protest.
- Progress is slow, uneven, and often invisible—but every step matters.
- Art, culture, and voice are powerful tools for reclaiming dignity and hope.
- The fight for justice is a marathon, not a sprint—rest and renewal are essential.

Remember, the work of justice is not reserved for the few. It belongs to all of us, in the choices we make, the stories we share, and the ways we care for each other. You don’t have to be perfect or fearless to make a difference. You just have to begin—where you are, with what you have, and with the people around you. The world will not change overnight, but every act of love, every moment of truth, every refusal to accept injustice moves us closer to the world we deserve. Carry the torch forward, knowing that you are not alone, and that your story, too, can be a spark for change.
