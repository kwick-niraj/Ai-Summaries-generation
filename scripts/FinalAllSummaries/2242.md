## Introduction

Ambition and talent often propel careers forward, yet subtle habits and ingrained behaviors can quietly undermine progress, especially for women navigating the complexities of professional advancement. In How Women Rise: Break the 12 Habits Holding You Back from Your Next Raise, Promotion, or Job by Sally Helgesen, the spotlight turns to the unique challenges women face in the workplace—challenges that are not always about skill or effort, but about patterns of thinking and acting that can inadvertently stall growth. The journey toward leadership and recognition is shaped not only by external barriers but also by internalized habits that may have once served a purpose but now stand in the way of new opportunities.

Within this landscape, the focus shifts to twelve specific habits that often hold women back, from reluctance to claim achievements to the tendency to overvalue expertise at the expense of visibility. Each habit is explored with empathy and insight, revealing how even the most well-intentioned behaviors can become obstacles to advancement. The path to breaking these patterns is not about wholesale change, but about recognizing strengths, reframing self-perceptions, and embracing new strategies for influence and growth. As the narrative unfolds, the possibility of transformation emerges—not just for individual careers, but for the broader culture of work itself.

## Chapter 1 Understanding the Unique Barriers Women Face in Career Advancement

The journey toward professional growth is rarely a straight line, and for women, the path is often shaped by a distinct set of challenges. These challenges are not always the result of external discrimination or overt bias; more often, they are woven into the fabric of workplace culture and personal habits developed over years. Recognizing these unique barriers is the first step toward meaningful change.

Many women begin their careers with a strong sense of diligence, reliability, and a desire to contribute meaningfully. These qualities are often rewarded early on, leading to positive feedback and a sense of accomplishment. However, as responsibilities grow and the stakes rise, the very habits that once propelled progress can become obstacles. The workplace, with its unwritten rules and shifting expectations, often rewards visibility, self-advocacy, and strategic networking—skills that may not come naturally to those who have relied on hard work alone.

Imagine a professional environment as a complex game. The rules are not always clear, and sometimes, the strategies that work in the early rounds are not the ones that secure victory in the later stages. For women, the game can feel especially opaque. There is often an expectation that good work will speak for itself, that recognition will follow naturally from effort, and that relationships should be built on mutual support rather than strategic intent. These beliefs, while rooted in positive values, can inadvertently limit advancement.

The first foundational idea is that awareness is essential. Without recognizing the specific habits and mindsets that may be holding them back, women can find themselves stuck, wondering why their careers have plateaued despite their dedication. This sense of being “stuck” is not a reflection of capability or ambition, but rather a sign that it is time to examine the underlying patterns at play.

A common scenario involves a talented professional who consistently delivers high-quality work but hesitates to share her achievements. She may feel uncomfortable drawing attention to herself, believing that humility is a virtue and that self-promotion is boastful. Meanwhile, colleagues who are more vocal about their successes are noticed and rewarded. Over time, this dynamic can lead to frustration and self-doubt.

Another frequent challenge is the tendency to overvalue expertise. Many women invest significant energy in becoming subject matter experts, assuming that mastery will naturally lead to advancement. While expertise is important, it is not always the primary factor in promotion decisions. Leadership roles often require broader skills, such as influencing others, building alliances, and navigating organizational politics.

The workplace is also shaped by relationships. Early in their careers, women may focus on building strong, supportive connections, but as they move up, the need to leverage these relationships for strategic purposes becomes more important. This shift can feel uncomfortable, as it may seem transactional or self-serving. However, understanding that relationships are a two-way street—offering support and seeking it in return—is crucial for growth.

In summary, the first step toward breaking through professional barriers is to recognize that the habits and beliefs that served well in the past may need to be reexamined. By developing a clear understanding of the unique challenges faced, it becomes possible to identify which patterns are helpful and which may be holding progress back. This awareness sets the stage for intentional change and opens the door to new possibilities.

## Chapter 2 The Twelve Habits That Hold Women Back

Building on the foundation of awareness, the next step is to identify the specific habits that can limit professional growth. These habits are not flaws or weaknesses; rather, they are learned behaviors that often develop in response to early career experiences and cultural expectations. Understanding them is like shining a light on the hidden corners of one’s professional life, making it possible to choose new ways of operating.

The twelve habits that commonly hold women back can be grouped into several broad themes: reluctance to claim achievements, expecting hard work to be noticed without self-advocacy, overvaluing expertise, building rather than leveraging relationships, and failing to enlist allies. Each habit is rooted in positive intentions but can have unintended consequences.

Consider the habit of minimizing achievements. Many women are taught from a young age to be modest and to avoid drawing attention to themselves. In the workplace, this can translate into downplaying successes or attributing them to luck or teamwork rather than personal effort. While humility is admirable, it can lead to being overlooked for promotions or new opportunities.

Another common pattern is the expectation that hard work will be automatically recognized and rewarded. This belief is often reinforced in school, where effort and results are closely linked. However, in the professional world, visibility and self-advocacy play a significant role in advancement. Those who wait for others to notice their contributions may find themselves passed over in favor of those who are more proactive in sharing their accomplishments.

Overvaluing expertise is another habit that can become a stumbling block. Many women invest heavily in becoming experts in their field, believing that mastery will guarantee advancement. While expertise is valuable, leadership roles often require a broader set of skills, including the ability to delegate, influence others, and see the bigger picture. Focusing exclusively on technical skills can limit opportunities for growth.

Relationship-building is a strength for many women, but there is a tendency to focus on creating supportive, nurturing connections rather than leveraging relationships for strategic purposes. This can lead to being seen as a reliable team player rather than a leader. Learning to use relationships as a source of influence and support is essential for moving into higher-level roles.

Finally, there is the habit of failing to enlist allies. Many women try to go it alone, believing that asking for help is a sign of weakness or that they should be able to handle challenges independently. In reality, building a network of supporters and mentors is a key factor in career advancement.

To make these habits more tangible, here are some of the most common patterns that can hold women back:

- Downplaying achievements or deflecting praise
- Waiting for others to notice and reward hard work
- Focusing on expertise at the expense of broader leadership skills
- Building relationships but not leveraging them for advancement
- Avoiding self-promotion due to discomfort or fear of being seen as boastful
- Failing to enlist allies or ask for support
- Putting job before career by prioritizing current tasks over long-term goals
- The perfection trap—believing that everything must be done flawlessly
- The disease to please—saying yes to every request to avoid disappointing others
- Minimizing—using language or body language that makes one seem smaller or less important
- Ruminating—dwelling on past mistakes or perceived failures
- Letting loyalty turn into self-sacrifice—staying in roles or organizations out of a sense of obligation rather than pursuing growth

Each of these habits is understandable and often reinforced by cultural messages about how women “should” behave. However, recognizing them is the first step toward change. By bringing these patterns into the open, it becomes possible to make conscious choices about which habits to keep and which to let go.

## Chapter 3 The Cost of Reluctance to Claim Achievements and the Power of Self-Advocacy

One of the most persistent barriers to advancement is the reluctance to claim achievements. This habit is often rooted in early socialization, where modesty and humility are encouraged, and self-promotion is discouraged. In the workplace, however, the ability to articulate accomplishments is essential for visibility and recognition.

Imagine a meeting where a team has just completed a successful project. While some members are quick to share their contributions, others remain silent, hoping their work will be noticed without having to speak up. Over time, those who consistently highlight their achievements are more likely to be considered for new opportunities, while those who remain quiet may be overlooked.

The discomfort with self-promotion is not unique to women, but it is often more pronounced due to cultural expectations. There is a fear of being seen as arrogant or self-serving, which can lead to a pattern of deflecting praise or attributing success to others. This habit, while well-intentioned, can have significant consequences for career growth.

The workplace often operates on the principle of “out of sight, out of mind.” Leaders and decision-makers are busy, and they may not be aware of all the contributions being made. Those who are able to clearly and confidently communicate their achievements are more likely to be considered for promotions, raises, and new projects.

To overcome this barrier, it is helpful to reframe self-advocacy as a form of communication rather than self-aggrandizement. Sharing achievements is not about boasting; it is about providing information that others need to make informed decisions. It is also about modeling confidence and encouraging others to do the same.

Practical strategies for developing self-advocacy include:

- Keeping a record of accomplishments and contributions, making it easier to recall specific examples when needed
- Practicing concise, factual statements about achievements, focusing on outcomes and impact
- Seeking feedback from trusted colleagues to gain perspective on strengths and areas for growth
- Reframing self-promotion as a way to support the team and organization by highlighting valuable work

By adopting these practices, it becomes possible to build confidence in sharing achievements and to create a culture where contributions are recognized and valued. This shift not only benefits the individual but also sets a positive example for others.

The power of self-advocacy extends beyond individual recognition. It also plays a role in shaping perceptions of leadership potential. Those who are able to articulate their successes are more likely to be seen as capable and ready for greater responsibility. This, in turn, opens the door to new opportunities and career growth.

## Chapter 4 Moving Beyond the Expectation That Hard Work Alone Will Be Rewarded

Another deeply ingrained habit is the belief that hard work will naturally lead to recognition and advancement. This expectation is often formed in school, where effort and results are closely linked, and reinforced by early career experiences. However, as roles become more complex and competition increases, the link between effort and reward becomes less direct.

In many organizations, the most visible contributors are the ones who are considered for promotions and new opportunities. This does not mean that hard work is unimportant, but rather that it is only one piece of the puzzle. Visibility, strategic communication, and relationship-building are equally important.

Consider the analogy of a garden. Tending to the plants—doing the work—is essential, but if the garden is hidden behind a fence, no one will see the results. Opening the gate and inviting others to see the garden is just as important as the work itself.

The expectation that hard work will be noticed can lead to frustration and disappointment when recognition does not follow. It can also result in burnout, as individuals take on more and more tasks in the hope that their efforts will eventually be rewarded. This cycle can be difficult to break without a conscious shift in mindset.

To move beyond this habit, it is important to:

- Recognize that visibility is a key factor in advancement
- Develop the ability to communicate contributions and results to decision-makers
- Seek out opportunities to showcase work, such as presentations, reports, or informal updates
- Build relationships with those who have influence over career progression

This does not mean abandoning the value of hard work, but rather complementing it with strategic actions that increase visibility and influence. By doing so, it becomes possible to create a more balanced approach to career growth.

Another aspect of this habit is the tendency to focus on current tasks at the expense of long-term career goals. It is easy to become absorbed in day-to-day responsibilities and lose sight of the bigger picture. Taking time to reflect on career aspirations and to set intentional goals is essential for sustained growth.

Practical steps for aligning daily work with long-term goals include:

- Regularly reviewing career objectives and assessing progress
- Seeking feedback and guidance from mentors or sponsors
- Identifying opportunities for growth and development, such as new projects or training
- Being proactive in seeking out new challenges and responsibilities

By integrating these practices, it becomes possible to move beyond the expectation that hard work alone will be rewarded and to take a more active role in shaping one’s career.

## Chapter 5 Overcoming the Perfection Trap and the Urge to Please

Two additional habits that can limit professional growth are the pursuit of perfection and the desire to please others. Both are rooted in positive intentions but can have unintended consequences.

The perfection trap is the belief that everything must be done flawlessly. This habit often develops in response to high expectations and a desire to avoid criticism. While striving for excellence is admirable, perfectionism can lead to overwork, stress, and missed opportunities. It can also make it difficult to delegate tasks or to take risks, as there is a fear of making mistakes.

In the workplace, perfectionism can be a double-edged sword. On one hand, it can lead to high-quality work and a reputation for reliability. On the other hand, it can result in missed deadlines, reluctance to take on new challenges, and difficulty moving into leadership roles. Leaders are often required to make decisions with incomplete information and to balance competing priorities. The ability to tolerate ambiguity and to learn from mistakes is essential.

To move beyond the perfection trap, it is helpful to:

- Set realistic standards for performance, recognizing that not every task requires the same level of attention to detail
- Prioritize tasks based on importance and impact
- Practice self-compassion and recognize that mistakes are opportunities for learning
- Delegate tasks and trust others to deliver results

The urge to please is another common habit. Many women are socialized to be accommodating and to avoid disappointing others. In the workplace, this can lead to saying yes to every request, taking on too many responsibilities, and neglecting personal needs and goals. Over time, this pattern can result in burnout and resentment.

Learning to set boundaries and to say no when necessary is a critical skill. This does not mean becoming uncooperative or indifferent, but rather making intentional choices about where to invest time and energy. It is also about recognizing that it is not possible to please everyone and that prioritizing one’s own goals is essential for growth.

Practical strategies for overcoming the urge to please include:

- Clarifying personal and professional priorities
- Practicing assertive communication, expressing needs and limits clearly and respectfully
- Seeking feedback from trusted colleagues to gain perspective on when to say yes and when to decline
- Reflecting on the impact of overcommitting and making adjustments as needed

By addressing these habits, it becomes possible to create a more sustainable and fulfilling approach to work. Letting go of perfectionism and the need to please opens the door to new opportunities, greater resilience, and a stronger sense of agency.

## Chapter 6 Releasing the Habit of Minimizing and Embracing a Stronger Presence

As the journey continues, another subtle but powerful habit comes into focus: minimizing. This is the tendency to shrink one’s contributions, ideas, or even physical presence—often unconsciously. It can show up in language (“I just wanted to add…” or “This might be a silly question, but…”), in body language (hunched shoulders, avoiding eye contact), or in the way one occupies space in meetings.

Minimizing is often a learned response to environments where assertiveness is discouraged or where standing out feels risky. Over time, these small acts of self-effacement can add up, sending a message to others—and to oneself—that one’s ideas are less valuable.

To counteract this, it’s helpful to practice what might be called “occupying your space.” This doesn’t mean dominating conversations or disregarding others, but rather allowing yourself to be fully present and to communicate with clarity and confidence. Imagine walking into a room and taking a seat at the table, rather than hovering at the edge. Or, when sharing an idea, stating it simply and directly, without unnecessary qualifiers.

Some gentle shifts to try:

- Replace minimizing language with clear, direct statements (“I recommend…” instead of “I just think…”).
- Practice open, grounded body language—shoulders back, steady eye contact, relaxed posture.
- Prepare for meetings by identifying one or two points you want to contribute, and commit to sharing them.
- Notice when you feel the urge to shrink or apologize, and pause to reset.

These small changes, repeated over time, help build a stronger presence. They also signal to others that your contributions matter, which can shift the dynamics of meetings and collaborations. Overcoming minimizing is not about becoming someone you’re not, but about allowing your authentic self to be seen and heard.

## Chapter 7 Breaking the Cycle of Rumination and Building Forward Momentum

Another habit that can quietly erode confidence and progress is rumination—the tendency to dwell on past mistakes, perceived failures, or negative feedback. While reflection is valuable, rumination is like a mental loop that keeps replaying the same scene, often amplifying self-doubt and anxiety.

This habit can be especially strong in high-achieving professionals who hold themselves to high standards. After a challenging meeting or a critical comment, it’s easy to replay the event, searching for what could have been done differently. Over time, this can sap energy and make it harder to take risks or move forward.

To break the cycle of rumination, it’s helpful to develop practices that encourage learning and forward movement:

- When a mistake happens, ask: “What can I learn from this?” rather than “Why did I do that?”
- Set a time limit for reflection—give yourself permission to process, then consciously shift focus.
- Use grounding techniques, such as deep breathing or a short walk, to reset after a stressful event.
- Talk with a trusted colleague or mentor to gain perspective and reality-check your thoughts.

Imagine rumination as being stuck in a revolving door. The goal is to step out of the loop and onto the path ahead. By focusing on learning and action, rather than self-criticism, it becomes possible to build resilience and maintain momentum.

## Chapter 8 Transforming Loyalty from Self-Sacrifice to Strategic Commitment

Loyalty is a deeply valued trait, and many professionals take pride in being dedicated to their teams, leaders, or organizations. However, loyalty can sometimes morph into self-sacrifice—staying in roles that no longer serve growth, or putting organizational needs ahead of personal aspirations for too long.

This habit often arises from a sense of responsibility or gratitude, but it can lead to stagnation or burnout. The key is to distinguish between healthy commitment and self-limiting loyalty.

A helpful metaphor is the oxygen mask on an airplane: you must secure your own before assisting others. In a career context, this means ensuring that your own growth and well-being are not consistently sacrificed for the sake of others.

To recalibrate loyalty:

- Regularly assess whether your current role aligns with your long-term goals and values.
- Have open conversations with leaders about your aspirations and development needs.
- Set boundaries around time and energy, especially when asked to take on extra work out of obligation.
- Remember that moving on from a role or organization can be an act of integrity, not betrayal.

By transforming loyalty into strategic commitment, you honor both your own needs and those of your organization. This shift creates space for growth, new challenges, and a more sustainable career path.

## Chapter 9 Building a Support Network and Enlisting Allies

A recurring theme in career advancement is the importance of relationships—not just for camaraderie, but as a source of support, advocacy, and opportunity. Many women are skilled at building strong, supportive connections, but may hesitate to leverage these relationships for strategic purposes.

Enlisting allies is not about transactional networking, but about cultivating genuine, reciprocal relationships. Allies can be mentors, sponsors, peers, or even those outside your immediate field. They offer perspective, open doors, and advocate on your behalf when you’re not in the room.

To build and activate a support network:

- Identify individuals who have influence or insight, and seek opportunities to connect authentically.
- Offer support and value to others, creating a foundation of trust and reciprocity.
- Be clear about your goals and aspirations, so allies know how to help.
- Ask for introductions, feedback, or advocacy when appropriate—remember, most people are willing to help if asked directly.

Think of your network as a garden: it requires tending, diversity, and time to flourish. By investing in relationships and enlisting allies, you create a web of support that can sustain and accelerate your growth.

## Chapter 10 Practical Tools for Changing Habits and Sustaining Progress

Recognizing unhelpful habits is only the beginning; lasting change requires practical tools and ongoing support. Changing ingrained patterns can feel daunting, but small, consistent actions can lead to meaningful transformation.

A simple framework for habit change includes:

- Awareness: Notice when a habit is triggered and how it shows up.
- Intention: Decide what new behavior you want to practice instead.
- Experimentation: Try out new approaches in low-stakes situations, and reflect on what works.
- Support: Enlist accountability partners, mentors, or coaches to encourage and guide you.

For example, if you tend to minimize your contributions in meetings, set an intention to share one idea per meeting, no matter how small. If rumination is a challenge, practice a “reset ritual” after stressful events. Over time, these small experiments build confidence and create new neural pathways.

It’s also helpful to track progress and celebrate wins, no matter how minor. Change is rarely linear, and setbacks are part of the process. By focusing on growth rather than perfection, you create a more compassionate and sustainable path forward.

**Conclusion: Integrating New Habits for Lasting Growth**

The journey to professional fulfillment and leadership is not about becoming someone else, but about uncovering and amplifying your authentic strengths. By recognizing the habits that may have served you in the past but now hold you back, you open the door to new possibilities.

Key takeaways to remember:

- Awareness of habits is the first step toward intentional change and growth.
- Self-advocacy and visibility are essential for recognition and advancement.
- Strategic relationships and allies provide support, perspective, and opportunity.
- Letting go of perfectionism and the urge to please creates space for resilience.
- Small, consistent actions are the building blocks of lasting transformation.

As you move forward, remember that change is a process, not a destination. There will be moments of discomfort, uncertainty, and even setbacks. This is natural and part of the learning curve. The most important thing is to keep returning to your intentions, to seek support when needed, and to celebrate progress along the way.

You are not alone on this path. Many have walked it before you, and many will walk it after. By embracing your strengths, challenging old patterns, and inviting others to support your journey, you create a ripple effect that extends beyond your own career. Each step you take—no matter how small—contributes to a more inclusive, dynamic, and empowering professional world. Trust in your capacity to grow, and give yourself permission to lead with both confidence and compassion.
