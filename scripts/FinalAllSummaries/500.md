## Introduction

Freedom and responsibility intertwine at the heart of human existence, shaping every choice and action. The world of Existentialism is a Humanism by Jean-Paul Sartre explores the profound implications of living without predetermined essence or divine blueprint. In a universe where meaning is not handed down from above, each individual stands alone, tasked with forging identity and purpose through acts of will. The anxiety and exhilaration of this freedom become central, as the absence of external authority compels a radical honesty about motives, values, and the consequences of one’s decisions.

Sartre’s vision challenges the comfort of excuses and the safety of conformity, insisting that to exist is to be condemned to be free. Every action, no matter how small, becomes a declaration of values not just for oneself, but for all humanity. The weight of this responsibility can feel overwhelming, yet it also opens the possibility for authentic living—where meaning is created, not discovered. Through this lens, the ordinary becomes extraordinary, and the struggle to live sincerely in a world without guarantees becomes the ultimate human project.

## Chapter 1 Facing Criticism and Misunderstanding: Why Existentialism Needs Defending

It’s a chilly evening in postwar Paris, and a crowd gathers in a lecture hall, restless and expectant. The air is thick with the weight of recent history—war, occupation, liberation—and the hunger for new ideas. Many in the audience have come with questions, some with skepticism, others with hope. The word “existentialism” has been tossed around in cafés and newspapers, often with a sneer or a shrug. Some call it a philosophy of despair, others accuse it of encouraging selfishness or moral chaos. The time has come to clear the air.

The room is filled with people from all walks of life: students eager for clarity, older listeners wary of new trends, and a handful of critics ready to pounce. The mood is tense but electric. The speaker, aware of the misunderstandings swirling around existentialism, begins not with abstract theory but with the lived reality of being misunderstood. It’s a feeling most people know well—being judged for what one is not, or having one’s intentions twisted by others.

Existentialism, the speaker explains, has been accused of many things. Some say it’s too gloomy, obsessed with anxiety and meaninglessness. Others claim it’s too individualistic, encouraging people to ignore the needs of society. There are even those who argue that it’s dangerous, undermining morality and tradition. These criticisms sting, not just because they’re harsh, but because they miss the point entirely.

The heart of the matter is this: existentialism is not about wallowing in despair or rejecting all values. Instead, it’s a response to a world where old certainties have crumbled. The war has shown that the foundations people once relied on—religion, tradition, authority—can be shaken or destroyed. In this new landscape, the question is not whether life has meaning, but how meaning can be created when the old answers no longer satisfy.

The speaker looks out at the audience, sensing their unease. Many are searching for something solid to hold onto, a way to make sense of their lives after so much upheaval. Existentialism, far from being a philosophy of hopelessness, offers a way forward. It insists that people are not defined by their circumstances or by what others say about them. Instead, each person is responsible for shaping their own existence, for making choices that matter.

This responsibility is both liberating and daunting. It means that no one can hide behind excuses or blame fate for their actions. At the same time, it opens up the possibility of genuine freedom—a freedom rooted in the reality of everyday life, not in abstract ideals. The critics, it turns out, have misunderstood existentialism because they see only its challenges, not its promise.

As the evening unfolds, the speaker promises to address these misunderstandings head-on. The goal is not to win an argument, but to invite the audience into a deeper conversation about what it means to be human in a world without guarantees. The journey will not be easy, but it will be honest. And in that honesty, there is hope.

## Chapter 2 Existence Before Essence: How People Define Themselves Through Choices

Imagine a child sitting at a desk, pencil in hand, staring at a blank page. There’s no instruction, no template, no one telling them what to draw. The possibilities are endless, but so is the uncertainty. This is the starting point for understanding existentialism’s most famous idea: existence comes before essence.

Most people grow up believing that things have a fixed nature—a chair is made to be sat on, a knife is made to cut. Their purpose is built in from the start. For centuries, many assumed that humans were the same: born with a set purpose, a soul designed by a higher power, a destiny waiting to be fulfilled. But what if that’s not true? What if, instead, people are more like that child with the blank page—thrown into the world without a script, forced to invent themselves as they go?

This is the radical claim at the heart of existentialism. People exist first—they are born, they act, they make choices. Only afterward do they become something, shaping their identity through what they do. There’s no blueprint handed down from above, no essence that determines who someone must be. Instead, each person is a work in progress, defined by the sum of their actions.

It’s a dizzying thought, and not everyone welcomes it. Some find comfort in the idea that their path is laid out for them, that they are part of a grand design. But existentialism asks people to let go of that comfort and face the reality of their freedom. There’s no one else to blame, no fate to hide behind. The meaning of a life is not given—it must be created, moment by moment, through choices big and small.

Consider the story of a young adult, torn between staying home to care for a sick parent and joining the resistance against an occupying force. There’s no rulebook to consult, no authority to dictate the right answer. The decision is agonizing, and whatever choice is made, it will shape not just the future, but the very identity of the person making it. In that moment, it becomes clear: people are not defined by what they are told to do, but by what they choose to do.

Most people don’t notice this process as it happens. They drift through routines, following habits or expectations, rarely stopping to ask why they do what they do. But existentialism insists that every action counts, that even the smallest decision is a brushstroke on the canvas of a life. There’s no escaping this responsibility, no way to opt out. To be human is to be free, and to be free is to be responsible for what one becomes.

This freedom can feel overwhelming, even frightening. It’s easier to believe that someone else is in charge, that there’s a plan to follow. But existentialism offers a different kind of hope—the hope that, in the absence of guarantees, people can still create meaning for themselves. The blank page is not a curse, but an opportunity.

## Chapter 3 The Weight of Freedom: Anguish, Responsibility, and the Search for Meaning

There’s a moment that comes to everyone, sooner or later, when the full weight of freedom becomes impossible to ignore. It might happen late at night, lying awake and replaying the day’s choices. Or in the middle of a conversation, realizing that words spoken in haste can’t be taken back. For some, it arrives in a crisis, when the usual supports fall away and the next step is uncertain. In these moments, the reality of being responsible for one’s own life hits hard.

Existentialism calls this feeling “anguish,” but it’s not just anxiety or fear. It’s the deep, unsettling awareness that every choice matters—not just for oneself, but for others as well. When someone decides how to act, they are, in a sense, setting an example for all of humanity. The stakes are high, and there’s no one else to take the blame if things go wrong.

Picture a teacher, standing in front of a classroom, faced with a difficult decision. Should they stick to the curriculum, even if it feels empty, or take a risk and teach something that might truly matter to the students? There’s no guarantee of success, no promise that the choice will be the right one. The teacher feels the pressure—not just to do their job, but to live up to their own ideals. In that moment, the burden of freedom is real and heavy.

Most people try to avoid this feeling. They look for ways to shift responsibility onto others—parents, bosses, traditions, even fate. It’s tempting to believe that someone else knows best, that there’s a right answer waiting to be discovered. But existentialism insists that this is an illusion. There’s no escape from freedom, no way to hand off the task of living to someone else.

This realization can lead to a sense of “forlornness”—a word that captures the loneliness of being truly free. Without a higher power to provide guidance, people are left to make their own way. It’s a sobering thought, but it’s also the starting point for genuine authenticity. Only by accepting the reality of their situation can people begin to live honestly, without hiding behind excuses or false hopes.

Despair is another consequence of freedom, but it’s not the same as giving up. Instead, it’s the recognition that people can only control their own actions, not the outcome. A parent might do everything possible to raise a child well, but there’s no guarantee of success. A friend might offer support, but can’t force someone else to change. This doesn’t mean that effort is pointless—only that it must be grounded in the reality of what’s possible.

## Chapter 4 Living Without God: Creating Values and Meaning in a Secular World

For centuries, many found comfort in the idea that life’s meaning came from a higher power. There was a sense of order, a belief that right and wrong were written into the fabric of the universe. But in the aftermath of war and upheaval, that certainty has faded for many. The question now is not what God wants, but what people are to do when there is no divine plan to follow.

This is the challenge of living in a secular world. Without God, the old sources of meaning and morality no longer hold sway. Some see this as a recipe for chaos—a world where anything goes, where values are up for grabs. But existentialism offers a different perspective. It suggests that, in the absence of external authority, people are free to create their own values. This freedom is not a license for selfishness, but a call to responsibility.

Imagine someone facing a moral dilemma—perhaps a worker tempted to cut corners for personal gain, or a friend deciding whether to tell a painful truth. There’s no commandment to consult, no cosmic judge to weigh the options. The decision must be made in the here and now, with only one’s own conscience as a guide. This is both liberating and terrifying. There’s no one to blame if things go wrong, but also no one to take credit if they go right.

Most people, when confronted with this freedom, feel a sense of vertigo. It’s easier to cling to old rules, to do what’s expected, to avoid the risk of making a mistake. But existentialism insists that this is not enough. To live authentically is to accept the challenge of creating meaning in a world without guarantees. It means taking responsibility for one’s own values, and for the impact those values have on others.

This process is not about inventing values out of thin air, or acting on a whim. It’s about recognizing that every choice is a statement about what matters, not just for oneself, but for everyone. When someone chooses honesty over deceit, or courage over cowardice, they are saying, in effect, “This is what I believe a human being should do.” The stakes are high, and the responsibility is real.

Some worry that this approach leads to moral relativism, where anything is permitted and nothing matters. But existentialism rejects this idea. The freedom to create values is not the freedom to do whatever one wants, but the freedom to take responsibility for the consequences of one’s actions. It’s a demanding standard, but also a hopeful one. In a world without God, people are not lost—they are free to become who they choose to be.

## Chapter 5 Authenticity and the Universal Impact of Personal Choices

In the quiet moments between decisions, a realization often dawns: the choices made in private ripple outward, touching lives far beyond what can be seen. This is the heart of existentialism’s claim that to choose for oneself is, in a sense, to choose for all humanity. Every action, no matter how small, carries a kind of universal weight.

Consider a person deciding whether to speak up against an injustice at work. The temptation to stay silent is strong—after all, it’s easier to avoid conflict, to blend in, to let someone else take the risk. But in that moment, the decision is not just about personal comfort. It’s a statement about what kind of world is acceptable, about what it means to be human. The choice sets an example, whether intended or not.

Most people underestimate the impact of their actions. They see themselves as isolated, their choices as private matters. But existentialism challenges this view. It insists that every decision is a kind of declaration, a way of saying, “This is what I believe is right.” Others are watching, consciously or not, and are influenced by what they see.

This is where the idea of authenticity comes in. To live authentically is to act in accordance with one’s own values, not out of fear or conformity. It means refusing to hide behind excuses or to blame others for one’s choices. It’s a demanding way to live, requiring honesty and courage. But it’s also the only way to create a life that is truly one’s own.

Authenticity is not about perfection. Everyone makes mistakes, and no one can predict the outcome of every decision. What matters is the willingness to take responsibility, to learn from failure, and to keep striving for integrity. In a world where there are no guarantees, this is the closest thing to certainty that can be found.

The universal impact of personal choices is both a burden and a gift. It means that no one is insignificant, that every life has the power to shape the world in some small way. It’s a call to live with intention, to recognize the interconnectedness of all people, and to act with the knowledge that what is done matters—not just for oneself, but for everyone.

## Chapter 6 Engagement: Taking a Stand in a World That Won’t Wait

There’s a certain restlessness that comes from knowing the world is unfinished, that history is still being written with every passing day. For many, this realization is paralyzing—why bother acting when so much is uncertain, when the future is a fog? But existentialism refuses to let people off the hook. It insists that engagement—throwing oneself into the world, taking a stand, making a mark—is not just an option, but a necessity.

Picture someone sitting at a kitchen table, reading the morning news. The headlines are grim: injustice, conflict, suffering. The temptation is to turn away, to retreat into private life, to say, “It’s not my problem.” But existentialism whispers otherwise. It reminds us that to exist is to be involved, to be implicated, to be responsible for the shape of the world.

Most people don’t realize how much their silence or inaction contributes to the status quo. It’s easy to believe that only the powerful or the famous can make a difference. But existentialism levels the playing field. It says that every person, by virtue of being alive and aware, is already engaged—whether they admit it or not. The only question is how.

There’s a story of someone who, after years of feeling powerless, decides to volunteer at a local shelter. The work is hard, the results uncertain. But in the act of showing up, of choosing to care, something shifts. The world doesn’t change overnight, but the sense of helplessness begins to fade. It becomes clear that engagement is not about grand gestures, but about the willingness to act, even when the outcome is unknown.

Existentialism doesn’t promise that engagement will be easy or that it will always succeed. In fact, it warns that disappointment and frustration are inevitable. But it also insists that meaning is found not in waiting for the perfect moment, but in the act of committing oneself to a cause, a project, a relationship. The world may be uncertain, but the choice to engage is always available.

## Chapter 7 Bad Faith: The Temptation to Hide from Freedom

There’s a subtle, almost invisible way that people slip out of responsibility. It happens in the small moments—agreeing with the crowd to avoid conflict, blaming circumstances for a missed opportunity, telling oneself, “That’s just the way I am.” Existentialism has a name for this: bad faith.

Imagine someone stuck in a job they dislike, telling themselves they have no choice. The bills must be paid, the expectations met. It’s easier to believe that life is something that happens to them, rather than something they shape. But deep down, there’s a nagging sense that this isn’t the whole truth.

Bad faith is the act of pretending not to be free, of denying one’s own agency. It’s a way of hiding from the anxiety that comes with choice, of seeking comfort in roles, routines, and excuses. Most people fall into bad faith without realizing it. They become the roles they play—parent, employee, citizen—forgetting that these are choices, not destinies.

There’s a moment when someone, after years of drifting, suddenly sees the pattern. Maybe it’s triggered by a loss, a failure, or a chance encounter. The realization is unsettling: “I’ve been living someone else’s life.” The temptation is to retreat, to double down on old habits. But existentialism offers another way.

To live in good faith is to acknowledge the reality of freedom, to accept that one is always more than the sum of their roles. It means facing the discomfort of uncertainty, the risk of making mistakes, the burden of responsibility. It’s not about rejecting commitments, but about choosing them consciously, with open eyes.

Most people don’t notice how often they slip into bad faith. It’s a defense against the fear of freedom, a way to avoid the pain of regret. But existentialism insists that only by facing this fear can people begin to live authentically. The path is not easy, but it’s the only way to create a life that is truly one’s own.

## Chapter 8 Hope and Action: Building Meaning in the Face of Absurdity

There’s a quiet heroism in those who keep going when the world makes no promises. For many, the realization that life has no built-in meaning is a source of despair. But existentialism turns this on its head. It suggests that the absence of guarantees is not a curse, but an invitation—a chance to build meaning through action.

Think of someone who, after a personal loss, finds themselves adrift. The old certainties are gone, the future is a blank. It would be easy to give up, to retreat into numbness. But instead, they begin to reach out—to friends, to new experiences, to small acts of kindness. The pain doesn’t disappear, but something new begins to grow: a sense of purpose, fragile but real.

Most people wait for meaning to arrive, as if it were a gift from outside. But existentialism insists that meaning is made, not found. It’s built in the choices people make, the risks they take, the relationships they nurture. There’s no map, no guarantee of success. But in the act of trying, of caring, of creating, life becomes meaningful.

There’s a story of someone who, after years of searching for the “right” path, realizes that there is no single answer. The freedom to choose is both a burden and a gift. It becomes clear that hope is not a feeling, but a practice—a way of living in the face of uncertainty, of refusing to give in to despair.

Existentialism doesn’t offer easy comfort. It asks people to accept the absurdity of life, to face the fact that there are no final answers. But it also offers a kind of hope—the hope that, in the act of living, people can create meaning for themselves and for others. It’s a hope rooted in action, in engagement, in the willingness to keep going, even when the way is unclear.

## Chapter 9 The Humanist Promise: Dignity, Solidarity, and Becoming More Fully Human

At the heart of existentialism is a deep respect for human dignity. In a world without guarantees, every person is called to create meaning, to take responsibility, to live authentically. But this is not a solitary task. Existentialism is, at its core, a humanism—a belief in the value and potential of every individual.

Imagine a community coming together after a crisis. There’s fear, uncertainty, and grief. But there’s also a sense of solidarity, a recognition that everyone is in this together. People reach out to one another, offering support, sharing burdens, building something new from the ruins. In these moments, the promise of existentialism becomes real.

Most people think of freedom as a private affair, a matter of personal choice. But existentialism insists that freedom is always shared, always connected to the freedom of others. To act authentically is to recognize the humanity of those around us, to see their struggles and hopes as bound up with our own.

There’s a story of someone who, after years of focusing on their own goals, begins to notice the needs of others. The shift is subtle but profound. It becomes clear that to be fully human is to care, to connect, to build a world where everyone’s dignity is respected.

Existentialism doesn’t promise a perfect world. It acknowledges the reality of conflict, of suffering, of failure. But it also insists that people are capable of more—of courage, of compassion, of creativity. The humanist promise is not a guarantee, but a challenge: to become more fully human, to build lives and communities that reflect the best of what people can be.

## Chapter 10 Conclusion: Living Existentialism—A Guide for the Everyday

Existentialism is not a philosophy for the ivory tower. It’s a way of living, a call to honesty, courage, and engagement. It asks people to face the reality of their freedom, to accept the burden of responsibility, and to create meaning in a world without guarantees. It’s a demanding path, but also a hopeful one.

**Key Takeaways:**

- Every person is responsible for shaping their own existence through choices.
- Freedom is both a burden and a gift—there are no excuses, only possibilities.
- Meaning is not given; it is created through action, engagement, and care.
- Authenticity requires honesty, courage, and a willingness to face uncertainty.
- The impact of personal choices ripples outward, shaping the world for all.

If you find yourself hesitating at the edge of a decision, uncertain of the right path, remember: existentialism doesn’t offer a map, but it does offer a compass. The direction is yours to choose, and the meaning is yours to create. You are not alone in your struggle—everyone faces the same uncertainty, the same responsibility, the same hope. The world is unfinished, and your actions matter. Step forward with courage, knowing that to live authentically is to embrace both the risk and the promise of being human. In the end, it’s not about finding the perfect answer, but about living the question—fully, honestly, and together.
