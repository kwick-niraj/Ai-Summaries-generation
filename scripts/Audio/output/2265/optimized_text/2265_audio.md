 Over time, it’s become clear: white supremacy isn’t just about individual attitudes. It’s a system—a whole set of rules, policies, and expectations that decide who gets ahead and who’s left behind. And this system? It’s so deeply built in that a lot of folks barely notice it, even when they’re the ones being hurt by it. People just get used to thinking, “That’s just the way it is,” not realizing these patterns were created on purpose, and kept alive for a reason.
 

 And this persistence—it’s not an accident. Throughout history, folks in power have found ways to adapt. The language changes, but the basic structure stays the same. When one kind of discrimination gets challenged, another quietly takes its place. Laws get rewritten, but those old assumptions hang on. That’s why progress can feel so slow—every step forward seems to bring new obstacles, new reasons to keep things as they are.
 

 But here’s something important: even as these systems continue, there are always people who notice the cracks. Maybe it’s a teacher who refuses to lower expectations for any student. Or a neighbor who goes out of their way to welcome newcomers. Or a worker who calls out unfair rules at their job. These small acts of resistance—they’re the seeds of change. They’re reminders that our world isn’t set in stone, and the ideas shaping it can be questioned—and changed.
 

 So, the story of white supremacy in America isn’t just about what happened long ago. It’s about the choices people make every day—often without even realizing it. It’s about how old ideas keep shaping new realities, and how, by noticing and questioning these patterns, we really can build a different future.
