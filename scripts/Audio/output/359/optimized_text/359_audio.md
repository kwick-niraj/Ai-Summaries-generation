## Chapter 2 Unpacking the “What Happened?” Conversation—Moving Beyond Blame and Certainty

Let’s dive into Chapter 2: **Unpacking the “What Happened?” Conversation—Moving Beyond Blame and Certainty.**
 
 When you’re gearing up for a tough conversation, it’s easy to zero in on the facts. What actually happened? Who did what? Who’s responsible? But here’s the thing—this “what happened?” conversation is almost never as simple as it seems.
 
 Why is that? Well, every person brings their own set of experiences, assumptions, and interpretations to the table. What feels absolutely true to you might look completely different to someone else. And that’s not about being dishonest or stubborn—it’s just how our minds work.
 
 Think about it. Memory is selective, our attention can only focus on so much, and everyone’s got their own background shaping what stands out. Imagine two coworkers leaving a meeting—one’s convinced everyone agreed on the next steps, while the other walks away feeling unsure and unsettled. Neither one is lying, but their stories split in different directions.
 
 Here’s something to watch out for: the blame game. When things go sideways, it’s so tempting to hunt for someone to point the finger at. But chasing blame usually just ramps up defensiveness and makes things worse. Instead of figuring out what led to the problem, everyone digs in, trying to prove who’s right and who’s wrong.
 
 But what if you could shift the focus—from blaming to understanding how everyone contributed? Most conflicts aren’t about one person’s mistake. They’re a messy mix of choices, misunderstandings, and circumstances. If you explore how each person’s actions, intentions, and assumptions shaped the situation, the conversation can move from accusation to real problem solving.
 
 So, how do you actually do this? Here are a few key practices:
 
 First, recognize that every story is incomplete. Nobody has the full picture. Each perspective is colored by what was noticed, what’s remembered, and what matters to that person.
 
 Second, separate intent from impact. Most of the time, harm happens without anyone meaning for it to. Instead of guessing at each other’s motives, focus on how actions made people feel. That opens the door to empathy.
 
 And third, acknowledge that both sides usually play a part. It’s almost never just one person’s fault. If you can see—even a small way—that you contributed, it helps everyone stay open and less defensive.
 
 Let’s bring this down to earth with a simple example. Picture a family disagreement about chores. One person’s feeling overwhelmed and underappreciated, while someone else feels unfairly criticized. Instead of fighting about who’s right, what if they both explored their own roles? Maybe one expected the other to notice the mess, while the other was just swamped with work. By mapping out what each person brought to the table, the conversation moves from blame to actually solving the problem.
 
 Now, this approach doesn’t mean you ignore mistakes or let things slide. It’s about creating space for everyone’s perspective to be heard, and building solutions based on real understanding. When you move from asking “who’s at fault?” to “how did we get here together?”—the conversation stops being about winning, and starts being about learning.
