## Chapter 2 The Machinery of Segregation: Laws, Policies, and Everyday Life

**Chapter Two. The Machinery of Segregation: Laws, Policies, and Everyday Life.**
 
 You know, when most folks talk about segregation, the first thing that comes to mind is usually angry crowds or those really dramatic standoffs. But the truth? Most of the dividing happened quietly—behind closed doors, in offices filled with paperwork and policies. 
 
 The machinery of segregation wasn’t built in a single day or with a single law. It grew from countless small decisions, each one stacking on top of the last, making the whole system even harder to break.
 
 Let’s rewind to the early twentieth century. City officials all across America started passing laws that made it illegal for Black families to buy or rent homes in white neighborhoods. Some of these rules were shockingly direct—actually naming which blocks belonged to which race. 
 
 But even when the Supreme Court finally called these laws unconstitutional, the people pushing segregation didn’t just give up. Instead, they found new ways to keep neighborhoods apart.
 
 That’s when developers and homeowners’ associations started using what were called **restrictive covenants**. These were legal agreements, tucked right into property deeds, that banned selling homes to Black families—and sometimes to other minorities too. 
 
 Imagine saving for years, working hard for your own home, only to discover that some bit of fine print in the paperwork kept your dream out of reach, not just for you but for anyone who came after.
 
 And here’s something surprising: the federal government wasn’t just standing on the sidelines. In fact, it became one of the most powerful forces enforcing segregation. Agencies like the Federal Housing Administration and the Home Owners’ Loan Corporation actually mapped out cities, highlighting neighborhoods where Black families lived—or where they might move in the future. 
 
 These areas were colored red, sending a clear message to banks and lenders: don’t invest here. That practice was called **redlining**, and it changed the future of entire communities.
 
 Picture a young couple, newly married, full of hope, walking into a bank to apply for a mortgage. If their address was in a redlined area, the answer was almost always no. Even when they could find someone willing to sell, getting a loan was nearly impossible. 
 
 Meanwhile, white families in neighborhoods marked green or blue found open doors and generous terms.
 
 Public housing played a role, too. When new housing projects were built during the New Deal and the years after World War Two, they were often placed smack in the middle of already segregated neighborhoods. 
 
 In some cities, even public housing that started out integrated was split apart on purpose, with separate buildings—or even separate entrances—for Black and white residents.
 
 The impact of all these policies didn’t just stay in one place. It spread outwards. Schools, which relied on local property taxes, reflected the wealth—or the poverty—of their neighborhoods. 
 
 Businesses chased the money, opening stores and offices in white areas and leaving Black communities behind. And over time, this machinery of segregation started to run on its own, making it even harder for families to break free.
 
 Most people living through all this didn’t see the whole web. They just noticed the rules, the refusals, those subtle hints that some places weren’t meant for them. 
 
 But the real power—the laws and policies holding everything together—was almost invisible. It’s only when we look back, connecting the dots from one decision to the next, that we can really see just how massive and complicated this machinery was.
