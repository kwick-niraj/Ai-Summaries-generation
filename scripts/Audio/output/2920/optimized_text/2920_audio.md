## Chapter 1 The Critical Importance of Leadership Transitions

**Chapter 1: The Critical Importance of Leadership Transitions**
 

 Stepping into a new leadership role—it’s one of those moments that’s filled with both excitement and a bit of uncertainty, isn’t it? Those first few months aren’t just about getting comfortable; they’re a make-or-break window that can set you up for long-term success or, honestly, some pretty tough challenges down the road. 
 
 People often talk about the importance of the first 90 days. And for good reason. 
 It’s during this time that new leaders set the tone, build trust, and lay down the foundation for everything that comes next. 
 
 The pressure’s real: slip-ups early on can stick with you, but if you grab a few quick wins, that positive momentum can carry you far.

 

 Now, everyone goes through leadership transitions at some point—whether you’re moving up in the same company, joining a brand new organization, or jumping into a totally different industry. Each situation has its own twists, but the basics are always the same. 
 
 Those early days are full of unknowns. Not just for you, but for the whole team. 
 People are watching closely, forming opinions, setting expectations. 
 And as the new leader, you’re trying to figure out not just the org chart, but all those unspoken rules, the real power players, and those subtle cultural cues that aren’t in any handbook.

 
 
 Stepping into this new world is a bit like exploring uncharted territory. 
 You’re both the traveler and the guide—figuring things out, while also showing others the way forward.
 It’s not about coming in with big, sweeping changes in those first few weeks. 
 
 Instead, it’s about watching, listening, and building trust—one small step at a time.

 

 There are a few big ideas to keep in mind as you navigate this phase:
 
 First, transitions are high-stakes. 
 Get it right, and you’re off to a strong start. 
 Get it wrong, and it can be tough to recover later.
 
 Next, what you do early on matters—a lot. 
 First impressions tend to stick, and changing people’s minds later can be a real uphill battle.
 
 You’ve got to juggle learning and action. 
 Move too fast, and you’ll make mistakes. 
 Go too slow, and people might lose confidence.
 
 And remember—every organization is different. 
 Just because something worked in your last job doesn’t mean it’ll work here. 
 
 Context really does matter.

 

 Picture this: there’s a new manager stepping in after a much-loved team leader has left. 
 The team’s feeling anxious, unsure about what’s coming next, maybe even a little skeptical about change. 
 And those first conversations? They set the entire tone. 
 If the new manager barges in with a bunch of new rules without first listening, the team’s probably going to resist. 
 But if the manager takes time to listen, shows respect for the team’s history, and asks for input, trust starts to build almost right away.

 

 So, the first 90 days aren’t just about getting through—it’s about charting a course for the whole journey ahead. 
 Leaders who approach this time with intention, a bit of humility, and a real willingness to learn are the ones who set themselves up to thrive.
 
 It all starts with understanding just how critical this window is, and choosing to make the most of it.

## Chapter 2 Diagnosing the Situation and Understanding the Context

**Chapter 2: Diagnosing the Situation and Understanding the Context**
 
 Before you take any big steps as a new leader, it’s crucial to get a real sense of the world you’re stepping into. Every organization, every team, even every department—each one has its own story, its unique culture, and its set of challenges. 
 
 If you dive in and start making changes before you understand what’s really going on, it’s kind of like handing out medicine without ever checking what’s making someone sick in the first place. So, your first real job? Take your time. Assess the situation with care and a sharp eye.
 
 Here’s something to keep in mind: not all leadership transitions are the same. The context you’re walking into is going to shape everything—your priorities, your strategy, even how fast you should move. 
 
 Usually, you’ll find yourself in one of four types of situations:
 
 Maybe you’re in a **start-up**, building something brand new from scratch, often with limited resources and lots of trial and error. Or, you might be in a **turnaround**, where things just aren’t working and urgent action is needed to get back on track. There’s also **realignment**, which is about gently steering an organization that’s lost its way—often by shifting culture and direction without ruffling too many feathers. And finally, there’s **sustaining success**, where you’re taking over a high-performing team, and your challenge is to keep that momentum going, while making sure nobody gets too comfortable or complacent.
 
 Each of these situations calls for a different leadership style. In a start-up, you’ll need to be a builder and a motivator, setting the vision and putting the right pieces in place. In a turnaround, it’s all about quickly spotting what’s broken, making some tough calls, and giving people a reason to believe things can get better. If you’re realigning a group, careful listening is your superpower—challenging old assumptions, but doing it in a way that keeps people on your side. And if you’re working to sustain success, you’re really a steward—protecting what’s working, but always on the lookout for ways to innovate and improve.
 
 So, how do you actually diagnose your situation? Start by gathering data from every corner you can. Don’t just rely on formal reports—talk to people, have informal conversations, and watch what’s happening around you. Ask those probing questions: What’s the biggest challenge we’re facing? Where do the real opportunities lie? What do people secretly worry about, or quietly hope for?
 
 It’s also smart to map out both the formal and informal networks. Who really holds influence here? Where’s the resistance, and where can you find support? And don’t forget to get a feel for the culture—what behaviors get rewarded, and what stories do people love to tell about the organization’s past?
 
 Let’s say you’re stepping into a company that’s just experienced rapid growth. At first glance, everything seems fantastic—revenues are climbing, and the team keeps getting bigger. But if you look a little deeper, you might find some growing pains: maybe roles are unclear, communication isn’t as smooth as it should be, or that scrappy, entrepreneurial spark that fueled early success is starting to fade. Your job is to uncover those hidden dynamics, not just take the official story at face value.
 
 And here’s the thing—this diagnostic phase isn’t a one-and-done deal. It’s an ongoing process of learning, testing out your assumptions, and refining your understanding as you go. Leaders who skip this step are much more likely to misread the situation and make changes that just don’t stick—or worse, actually make things harder. But when you really invest the time to understand the context, you set yourself up to make smart, targeted moves that actually work.
 
 If there’s one foundation every new leader needs, it’s humility. No matter how much experience you bring, you’re still the newcomer in this role. Being willing to listen, to observe, and to keep learning—that’s what sets you up for everything that comes next.

## Chapter 3 Accelerating Learning and Building Foundational Knowledge

**Chapter 3: Accelerating Learning and Building Foundational Knowledge**
 
 Once you’ve mapped out the landscape, the next big step is to speed up your learning. The faster you can climb that learning curve, the sooner you’ll be making smart decisions and really contributing. Now, don’t worry—this isn’t about knowing every detail by heart. It’s about quickly figuring out what actually matters most, where you can make the biggest impact, and how things really work beneath the surface.
 
 Think of learning in a new role like putting together a puzzle—except lots of pieces are still missing. Some are easy to spot: organization charts, financial statements, company policies. But others? They’re hidden away. I’m talking about those unspoken norms, informal alliances, and subtle cues that tell you what’s truly valued around here. The real trick is to gather information efficiently, weed out the noise, and focus your attention on what’ll move the needle.
 
 So, how do you speed this up? There are a few strategies that really help:
 
 **First**, set clear priorities for your learning. Figure out which areas you need to understand right away—like the key products, your main customers, core processes, and the people who really drive things forward.
 
 **Next**, have structured interviews. Sit down with a range of stakeholders and ask them the same core questions. That way, you’ll start to spot patterns and pick out the outliers.
 
 Also, take the time to observe meetings and daily routines. Who speaks up? Who listens? How are decisions actually made? These little details reveal a lot.
 
 And don’t forget—look for some quick wins. Solve a small but visible problem. Not only do you learn, but you also show people you’re here to make a difference.
 
 Let’s picture this in action. Imagine a leader stepping into a manufacturing plant. The technical side is complicated, for sure, but the real challenge is figuring out why production targets keep getting missed. So, this leader spends time on the shop floor, talks with operators, and watches how things run day to day. Eventually, they uncover a bottleneck in the supply chain—something nobody had really addressed. Fixing it doesn’t just boost performance; it also shows the team that their new leader is paying attention and ready to act.
 
 But here’s something a lot of people miss: learning isn’t just about collecting facts. It’s about building relationships. Folks are a lot more likely to share their real thoughts and concerns when they feel listened to and respected. Leaders who approach conversations with genuine curiosity and openness end up creating a climate of trust—and that’s when hidden issues start to surface.
 
 There’s another thing to keep in mind. No one expects you to know it all. In fact, it’s important to recognize the limits of what you know. The goal is to learn enough to make good judgments, but always stay open to feedback and new info. That mindset of always learning? It’s not just for your first 90 days—it’s something you’ll need your whole career.
 
 So, here’s the key takeaway: accelerating your learning is all about focus, curiosity, and a healthy dose of humility. It’s the engine behind effective action—and it’s how you start building credibility from day one.

## Chapter 4 Securing Early Wins and Establishing Credibility

Let’s dive into Chapter 4: **Securing Early Wins and Establishing Credibility.**
 
 Once you’ve got a good sense of what’s going on and you’ve built a strong foundation, your next move is all about getting those early wins. 
 
 Now, when I say early wins, I’m not talking about sweeping changes or flashy heroics. 
 
 It’s about real, visible improvements that matter to your team and the organization—small steps that prove you’re not just talking, you’re actually making things better.
 
 There’s a powerful psychology behind this. 
 
 Think about it: when someone new steps into a leadership role, everyone’s a little on edge. They’re wondering, **“What’s going to change? Can I trust this person?”** 
 
 When you notch those first few wins, you’re sending a clear message—you’re capable, you keep your word, and progress is happening. 
 
 That builds trust and gets people excited to be part of what’s next.
 
 So, how do you actually nail those early wins? 
 
 Here are a few things to keep in mind:
 
 First, **focus on what matters most.** 
 Don’t just chase the problems that are interesting to you—pick the ones your team and key stakeholders care about. 
 
 Next, aim for results everyone can see and feel, even if they’re on the smaller side. 
 
 Build on strengths that are already there, instead of trying to overhaul everything from scratch. 
 
 And don’t go it alone—get others involved in spotting opportunities and putting solutions in place. 
 When people help shape the changes, they’re way more likely to support them.
 
 Let’s make this real for a second. 
 Imagine a leader who realizes the weekly team meetings are a total drag—unfocused, confusing, and frankly, a waste of time. 
 
 By teaming up with folks to revamp the agenda, clarify who does what, and set clear goals for each meeting, suddenly, things start clicking. 
 
 Meetings become productive, people leave feeling energized, and you’ve just freed up valuable time for actual work. 
 
 That’s an early win everyone can feel.
 
 These quick wins aren’t just about progress—they’re about building your credibility. 
 
 And let’s face it, credibility is the real currency of leadership. 
 
 You earn it by making smart decisions, following through, and being consistent. 
 
 Promise too much and deliver too little? Trust evaporates fast. 
 But if you set realistic goals and actually meet them, people know they can count on you.
 
 Of course, there are a few traps you’ll want to avoid along the way:
 
 Don’t try to fix everything overnight. 
 Spreading yourself too thin just waters down your efforts and makes it more likely you’ll miss the mark.
 
 Don’t ignore your team’s input. 
 If you push through changes without listening, you’ll just create resistance.
 
 And finally, remember it’s not just about technical fixes. 
 If you overlook the people side—the culture, the relationships—you might solve one problem, but create another.
 
 Securing early wins isn’t just a single event; it’s an ongoing process. 
 
 Each win builds on the last, slowly changing how people see you and what they believe is possible. 
 
 Over time, these small victories stack up, transforming not just the organization, but your own standing as a leader.
 
 So, here’s the key takeaway: 
 Be intentional. 
 Pick actions that really matter, bring people along for the ride, and celebrate every bit of progress you make. 
 
 Early wins are more than just a confidence boost—they’re the building blocks of lasting success.

## Chapter 5 Building Relationships and Creating Alliances

Let’s dive into Chapter 5: **Building Relationships and Creating Alliances.**
 
 Here’s the simple truth—no leader can make it alone. Success, especially when you’re stepping into a new role, depends on your ability to build strong connections and form genuine alliances. Think of relationships as the pathways where information moves, decisions are made, and real change takes root. Without trust and teamwork, even the smartest strategy can fall flat.
 
 It all begins with your immediate team—your direct reports, your peers, and of course, your boss. Each of these groups has its own concerns and expectations, and it’s up to you to figure out what makes each one tick. From day one, investing in trust sets the tone for everything that follows.
 
 With your direct reports, focus on open and honest communication. People want to know what’s expected, where they stand, and how they fit in. Regular one-on-one check-ins, straightforward feedback, and a willingness to really listen can do wonders for building that personal connection.
 
 Now, let’s talk about peers. They’re often overlooked, but they play a huge role. Peers control resources, influence decisions, and can either back you up or make things tough. Building these relationships takes empathy, respect, and a true spirit of collaboration. It’s about finding common ground, sharing what you know, and looking for ways to help each other win.
 
 And then, there’s your boss. This relationship is absolutely crucial. The boss sets the overall tone, provides the resources you’ll need, and shapes how others see your performance. Managing up means understanding what matters to your boss—how they like to communicate, what their priorities are, and what they expect from you. It’s also about being proactive: keep your boss in the loop, ask for feedback, and tackle concerns before they turn into real problems.
 
 But don’t stop there. Beyond your core team, there’s a whole network to consider—stakeholders, customers, and those informal influencers who can really sway the conversation. Mapping out these connections helps you spot potential allies and those who might push back. The time you spend building alliances now will pay off big when it’s time to make changes.
 
 So, what can you do in practical terms?
 
 **First**, schedule early meetings with the people who matter most. Use these chats to listen, learn, and build rapport.
 **Second**, look for quick ways to create value—help someone else reach their goals and you’ll start earning trust right away.
 **Third**, be transparent and consistent. When people see you’re reliable and open, trust follows.
 **Finally**, handle conflicts head-on, but do it diplomatically. Sweeping problems under the rug only lets them grow.
 
 Imagine stepping into a team that’s struggled with internal rivalries. By meeting with each team member one-on-one, openly acknowledging past issues, and setting a clear tone of teamwork, you can begin to shift that culture. Over time, those efforts can turn a divided team into a close-knit, highly effective group.
 
 Here’s the big takeaway: Building relationships isn’t a one-and-done task. It’s a long-term investment. The groundwork you lay in those early days is just the beginning—trust and collaboration have to be nurtured and grown. Leaders who put relationships first are the ones who can handle challenges, spot new opportunities, and create real, lasting change.

## Chapter 6 Aligning Strategy, Structure, and Team

Let’s dive into Chapter 6: **Aligning Strategy, Structure, and Team**.
 
 Think about what happens once those first relationships are taking shape and you’ve already celebrated a few early wins. The next big challenge? Making sure your team, its structure, and your broader strategy are all moving in sync. 
 
 Honestly, it’s a lot like tuning an orchestra. Every section has to play its part, but everyone needs to follow the same sheet of music. When there’s misalignment—whether it’s in goals, roles, or just the way things are done—it’s like someone playing out of tune. You get friction, confusion, and, before you know it, progress slows down and morale takes a hit.
 
 So, where do you start? It all begins with getting really clear about your organization’s strategy. And I’m not just talking about what’s written in some official document. You need to understand the real priorities—the stuff that actually drives decisions and where resources go. Sometimes, those priorities are obvious. But more often? They get tangled up in competing agendas or old projects that just won’t go away.
 
 As a leader, your job is to cut through the noise. Figure out what truly matters, then share that in a way your team really gets. 
 
 Once you’ve nailed down the strategy, it’s time to look at your structure. Are roles and responsibilities crystal clear? Do your reporting lines actually help people work together and hold each other accountable? Or are there bottlenecks and overlaps that just slow things down? Sometimes, realigning means you’ll have to shuffle teams, redefine roles, or even let go of old habits that just don’t fit anymore.
 
 Now, let’s talk about the team itself. This isn’t just about how well each person performs—it’s about how the whole group works together. Are the right people in the right seats? Is there a gap in skills or maybe a mismatch in values and culture? You might have to make some tough calls here—maybe reassigning roles, coaching someone through a transition, or, if needed, letting someone go so the team can really perform.
 
 Here are a few practical steps to guide the alignment process:
 
 First, clarify and communicate your core strategy. Keep it simple and memorable, so your team can rally around it.
 
 Next, map out everyone’s roles and responsibilities. Look for any gaps, overlaps, or places where things just aren’t clear.
 
 Then, assess your team’s strengths and weaknesses. Use honest, constructive feedback—think of it as a way to help everyone grow.
 
 And finally, when it’s time to make changes, lead with empathy. Change is tough, so support your people every step of the way.
 
 Picture this: a leader realizes that two departments are fighting for the same resources, which leads to duplicated work and a lot of frustration. By bringing both teams together, spelling out the shared goal, and redefining everyone’s roles, that leader doesn’t just solve a conflict—they unlock new ways for the teams to work together and get better results.
 
 But here’s something important to remember: alignment isn’t a one-and-done deal. As your organization grows and shifts, your strategy, structure, and team have to evolve too. Leaders who keep checking in and realigning these pieces help their organizations stay agile and focused—ready to grab new opportunities whenever they pop up.

## Chapter 7 Adapting Leadership Style to the Situation

Chapter 7: Adapting Leadership Style to the Situation
 
 You know, there’s really no one-size-fits-all when it comes to leadership. Great leaders are a bit like seasoned navigators—they’re always adjusting their approach to fit the landscape in front of them.
 
 When you step into a new role, that ability to flex and adapt becomes even more crucial. Think about it: the style that works wonders during a crisis probably won’t have the same magic with a high-performing team. And what clicks with one group? It might totally backfire with another.
 
 So, where does adapting start? It all begins with self-awareness. Every leader has a natural tendency—maybe you’re more hands-on, or maybe you prefer to collaborate. Maybe you lean toward giving clear directions, or perhaps you like to step back and let others take the lead. Knowing your default? That’s your anchor for making real, intentional changes.
 
 Next, you’ve got to read the room. Is your team confident and skilled, or are they feeling a bit lost? Do you need fast decisions right now, or is it more about building consensus? The trick is to match your style to what the moment calls for—not just what feels comfortable to you.
 
 Here are a few practical ways to flex your leadership style:
 
 First, listen before you act. Especially in new or unfamiliar settings, take time to observe and ask questions. 
 
 Then, adjust your communication. Some teams need step-by-step guidance, while others do better with just a broad direction.
 
 Remember to balance support and challenge. Sure, offer encouragement, but don’t shy away from setting high expectations.
 
 And, be upfront about your approach. Let your team know why you’re leading the way you are—it builds trust.
 
 Picture this: a leader who’s naturally decisive and action-oriented walks into a team that thrives on consensus. If they charge ahead without slowing down, they’ll probably run into resistance. But if that leader takes a moment to invite input, explains their decisions, and paces the changes, trust grows—and the team starts moving forward together.
 
 Adapting your style doesn’t mean you’re being fake. It’s really about expanding your toolkit—adding more ways to connect, guide, and inspire.
 
 Over time, leaders who flex their style become truly effective. They meet people where they are, and help guide them to where they need to go.

## Chapter 8 Avoiding Common Traps and Pitfalls

Chapter 8: Avoiding Common Traps and Pitfalls
 
 Even the most well-intentioned new leaders can find themselves slipping into familiar traps. These pitfalls aren’t always obvious—they’re often woven into old habits, assumptions, or blind spots from previous roles. Spotting and stepping around these hazards? That’s a huge part of making any leadership transition work. 
 
 So, what are some of the most common traps? 
 
 First, there’s **sticking to the old playbook**. Maybe what you did before brought great results, but every situation’s different—and what worked last time won’t always work now.
 
 Then there’s the trap of **moving too fast or too slow**. If you rush, you risk pushing people away. But wait too long, and you might lose their confidence.
 
 Another big one is **failing to build alliances**. Trying to go it alone almost never works out. Relationships—they’re really the bedrock of influence.
 
 Let’s not forget **neglecting culture**. Every team has its own unwritten rules and values. Overlooking those can quickly lead to missteps you didn’t see coming.
 
 And finally, there’s the danger of **overpromising and underdelivering**. Ambitious goals are great—they inspire people. But only if they’re truly achievable.
 
 Picture this: a leader moves from a fast-paced, results-driven environment into a team that prefers consensus and careful deliberation. If they try to run at the old tempo, frustration builds up—on both sides. But if that leader pauses to really understand the new culture, they can adjust, set realistic expectations, and sidestep unnecessary conflict.
 
 Here’s another pitfall that trips up a lot of people: failing to manage up. In other words, neglecting the relationship with your boss. Some leaders think their good work will just speak for itself—but that can leave you out of sync with your boss’s priorities, or caught off guard by shifting expectations.
 
 So, how do you steer clear of these traps? There are a few simple—but powerful—habits:
 
 First, **seek feedback early and often**. Ask for honest input, and not just from the usual voices. 
 
 Next, **reflect on past experiences**. Notice your patterns—what’s worked, what hasn’t, and why.
 
 And always, **stay humble and curious**. Assume there’s something new to learn every day.
 
 Finally, **check your assumptions**. Test your beliefs against reality, and be open to making changes.
 
 Remember, avoiding pitfalls isn’t about being perfect. It’s about staying alert, being self-aware, and having the courage to change course when you need to. The leaders who learn from mistakes—rather than denying or repeating them—end up building both resilience and credibility over time.

## Chapter 9 Creating Systems for Ongoing Growth and Feedback

Let’s talk about something that really matters for lasting leadership—**creating systems for ongoing growth and feedback**.
 
 Think of your first 90 days as just the warm-up. To truly keep moving forward, you’ll need the right systems in place—kind of like the scaffolding that supports a building as it grows and changes. These systems aren’t just nice to have; they’re what help you, your team, and your whole organization keep learning and evolving.
 
 One of the most powerful systems you can build is a habit of regular feedback. And I’m not just talking about those once-a-year performance reviews. I mean real, ongoing conversations—where you talk about what’s working, what’s not, and how you can all get better. 
 
 Feedback shouldn’t just flow from the top down. The best leaders make sure it moves in every direction—up, down, and sideways. When you show you’re open to feedback, you send a clear message: learning matters here, and mistakes? They’re just stepping stones to growth.
 
 Another system that makes a huge difference is goal-setting. Clear, measurable goals give everyone focus and a sense of accountability. They help your team figure out what to tackle first, track their progress, and celebrate wins along the way.
 
 What’s even better is when you set these goals together—with input from everyone. That way, your team feels true ownership, not just orders from above.
 
 Don’t forget about development plans. These aren’t just for folks who are struggling; they’re for everyone. By investing in your people—through coaching, training, or even special projects—you’re helping your team become stronger and more adaptable.
 
 So, what practical tools can you use for ongoing growth?
 
 First, set up regular one-on-one check-ins. These are perfect for talking about progress, sorting out challenges, and mapping out what’s next in someone’s development.
 
 Next, consider using 360-degree feedback. By gathering input from a range of people, you get a much clearer picture of how things are really going.
 
 After-action reviews are another great tool. Take time after each project to reflect together—what worked, what didn’t, and what you’ll do differently next time.
 
 And encourage personal learning plans. Make it normal for everyone—including you—to set growth goals and chase after new skills.
 
 Picture this: you’ve just wrapped up your first 90 days as a leader. Instead of settling in, you start running monthly team reviews. But these aren’t just about hitting the numbers. They’re about sharing insights, raising concerns early, and brainstorming ways to get better together. Before long, you’ve built a routine of continuous learning and honest accountability.
 
 So here’s the key takeaway. Systems for growth aren’t about adding red tape or paperwork. They’re about creating steady rhythms and routines that keep your organization healthy and always moving forward. When you invest in these systems, you’re laying down the foundation for long-term, sustainable success.

## Chapter 10 Sustaining Momentum and Leading for the Long Term

Let’s talk about something every leader faces sooner or later—how do you actually keep your momentum going, long after the excitement of the first few months has faded? 
 
 Welcome to Chapter 10: **Sustaining Momentum and Leading for the Long Term**.
 
 You probably remember how much energy and focus you had in those first 90 days. But here’s the thing—without some intentional effort, that spark can start to fade. If you want to lead for the long run, it’s not about sprinting anymore. It’s about learning to run the marathon. That means finding the right balance between acting with urgency, and making sure you’ve got enough endurance to go the distance. 
 
 So, what helps you sustain that momentum? Well, it really comes down to three things: staying attentive to your relationships, delivering on results, and making time for renewal. Let’s break that down.
 
 First off, you’ve got to keep nurturing trust with your team. Celebrate progress—no matter how small—and handle setbacks with resilience. It’s easy to get caught up reacting to the crisis of the day. But real leadership means staying anchored to your purpose and your core values—even when things get tough.
 
 There are a few habits that can make all the difference:
 
 Revisit and refresh your goals regularly. That way, your team stays focused and inspired. 
 
 Celebrate those milestones, big and small. A well-timed “great job” can really lift spirits.
 
 Don’t forget about yourself. Invest in self-care and take time to reflect. Leadership is demanding, and you can’t pour from an empty cup.
 
 And most importantly, keep reminding yourself—and your team—why the work matters. Stay connected to the broader mission.
 
 Let me give you a real-world example. Imagine a leader who started off strong, but now the team’s energy is flat. What do they do? They bring everyone together, revisit the original vision, and set some fresh challenges. Suddenly, there’s a renewed sense of excitement and commitment. That cycle—of pausing, refreshing, and re-energizing—keeps organizations vibrant and ready for whatever comes next.
 
 So, here’s the key takeaway: Sustaining success isn’t about dodging problems. It’s about facing them head-on—with courage and adaptability. If you build solid foundations in those first 90 days, you’re setting yourself up to handle all the ups and downs that come your way.
 
 Remember, the journey doesn’t end when the initial excitement fades. That’s actually where true leadership begins.
