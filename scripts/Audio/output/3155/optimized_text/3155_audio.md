## Chapter 2 The 37% Rule and the Art of Knowing When to Stop

**Chapter 2. The 37% Rule and the Art of Knowing When to Stop**
 
 Let’s face it—one of the toughest challenges we all run into is figuring out when to stop searching and just make a choice. Maybe you’re hiring someone, looking for a new place to live, or even deciding if it’s time to settle down with a partner. That nagging fear of missing out on something better? It can leave us stuck, endlessly second-guessing ourselves. 
 
 Here’s where computer science steps in with a surprisingly practical idea: **optimal stopping**. It’s a strategy designed for those moments when you have to consider your options one at a time, and once you pass something up, there’s no going back. 
 
 So, picture this: You’re in charge of hiring and you’ve got a stream of candidates coming in for interviews. The catch? You have to accept or reject each candidate on the spot—no second chances. Your mission is to pick the very best person for the job. 
 
 Mathematicians call this the “secretary problem,” and believe it or not, they’ve cracked the code. The best move? Spend the first **37 percent** of your search just observing—no hiring, no commitments, just gathering information. After that, you hire the very first person who’s better than everyone you’ve already seen. That’s the magic of the **37% Rule**. 
 
 And this isn’t just for job interviews. The 37% Rule comes in handy any time you’re dealing with options that come one after another, and you have to decide on the spot. Think about hunting for an apartment, or even dating. By looking at the first 37% of your options, you get a sense of what’s out there. Then, as soon as you find an option that beats everything you’ve seen so far, you go for it.
 
 Here’s the simple formula:
 
 **First, review and pass on the first 37% of your choices—just to set your standard.**
 
 **Then, pick the very next option that’s better than everything before it.**
 
 This approach is all about balance. If you stop too early, you might miss out on something amazing. But if you wait too long, the best option might slip away. The 37% Rule doesn’t guarantee you’ll find perfection, but it gives you the best possible shot, given the situation.
 
 Let’s make this real. Imagine you’re searching for a new apartment. You spend the first third of your time just looking, not making any offers. After that, the first place that feels better than anything you’ve seen? That’s the one you grab. Or in dating, after you’ve spent some time meeting people, the next person who truly stands out becomes your focus.
 
 This way of thinking also sheds light on why regret is such a natural part of making decisions. No strategy can promise you won’t miss out entirely—but having a clear rule to follow can really cut down on anxiety and second-guessing. When you accept that some uncertainty is just part of the deal, you can move forward with a little more confidence—and a lot more peace of mind.
 
 So, the 37% Rule is a great example of how computer science can actually make everyday choices clearer. Even our most personal decisions can benefit from a little structure—turning what feels like guesswork into a process that makes sense.

## Chapter 3 Balancing Exploration and Exploitation in Uncertain Worlds

**Chapter 3: Balancing Exploration and Exploitation in Uncertain Worlds**
 
 Life really is a constant dance, isn’t it? We’re always deciding between trying something new or sticking with what we already know and love. Ever found yourself wondering if you should check out that new restaurant down the street or just go back to your favorite spot? Or maybe you’ve debated whether it’s time to search for a new job, or if you should just settle into the one you have? 
 
 This back-and-forth is what computer scientists call the "explore versus exploit tradeoff." It’s all about balancing two things: searching for new opportunities—exploration—and making the most out of what you already know—exploitation.
 
 Let’s picture it with a classic example: imagine you’re in a casino, staring at a long row of slot machines. Each one could have a different payout, but you don’t know which is best. You’ve only got a limited number of pulls. Do you try all the machines to see what’s possible, or do you keep playing the one that’s treated you well so far?
 
 That’s the heart of the explore/exploit tradeoff.
 
 So, what do these terms really mean for us?
 
 **Exploration** means trying new options—gathering information, discovering new possibilities. **Exploitation** is about focusing on the best choice you’ve found so far—maximizing your immediate rewards.
 
 The tricky part? The right balance between exploring and exploiting depends on where you are. Early on—like when you’ve just moved to a new city, or you’re starting out in a new career—it makes a lot of sense to explore. There’s so much out there, and the cost of missing out on something better is pretty high. Plus, you’ve got time to enjoy any new discoveries.
 
 As time goes by, though, exploring everything just isn’t as valuable. You start to know what works for you, and it feels smarter to double down on those favorites.
 
 Think about someone who just moved to a new place. At first, they might spend months trying out different neighborhoods, restaurants, and meeting new people. But as they settle in, they find their go-to spots and close friends—and that’s where their energy goes.
 
 The same thing happens in business. A new company often tries out a bunch of products or services. But once they see what’s really working, they pour their efforts into refining and improving those winners.
 
 And here’s something you might recognize: sometimes, when we stick to what we know for too long, life starts to feel a little stale. We get bored, or wonder if we’re missing out. But if we only chase new things, we never get to enjoy what’s already good. Finding that balance—between novelty and comfort—can help us make choices we actually feel good about.
 
 So, how do we handle this tradeoff in real life? Computer science gives us a few clever strategies. One way is to set aside a certain amount of time or resources for exploring new things, especially at the beginning. As we learn more, we gradually focus more on what works. 
 
 There’s also a smart method called "upper confidence bounds." This one helps you favor not just the options that look good, but also the ones you’re still a bit unsure about—just in case there’s a hidden gem waiting to be found.
 
 Here are a few takeaways you can use:
 
 Early on, lean into exploration—try new things and gather information. As you figure out what works, shift your focus toward making the most out of those choices. And every now and then, don’t forget to revisit the options you haven’t tried yet. You never know what you might discover.
 
 When you understand the explore/exploit tradeoff, it gets a lot easier to make decisions with confidence—whether you’re chasing something new or enjoying what you already have. It’s not just a matter of taste; it’s actually a principle at the heart of smart decision-making, and it comes straight from the logic of algorithms.

## Chapter 4 Organizing Life with Sorting, Filtering, and Prioritization

**Chapter 4: Organizing Life with Sorting, Filtering, and Prioritization**
 
 Let's be honest—life throws a ton of information our way every single day. Think about it: emails, appointments, endless to-do lists, and those random ideas that pop into your head at the worst possible moments. 
 
 The real challenge isn’t just to keep up... it’s to organize all that chaos so you actually feel clear and less stressed. 
 
 And believe it or not, computer science has some pretty amazing tools for this—thanks to decades of research into sorting, filtering, and prioritizing.
 
 So, what is sorting all about? Well, at its heart, sorting means arranging things in a way that makes sense to you. Maybe it’s alphabetizing your contacts, lining up tasks by urgency, or just putting files in order by date. 
 
 When computer scientists talk about efficient sorting—like quicksort or mergesort—they’re really just showing us how the right approach can save you a ton of time and effort when you’re trying to wrangle a mess into something manageable.
 
 Now, let’s talk about filtering. Imagine you’re staring at a jam-packed inbox or a mile-long list of search results. Filtering helps you zoom in on what actually matters. 
 
 Online, filters can clean up your email, your social feeds, or even your shopping results. In everyday life, filtering might mean cutting out distractions, focusing on your top priorities, or just saying “no” to things that don’t serve you.
 
 Then there’s prioritization—the art of deciding what gets your attention first. In computer science, this is called a scheduling problem. 
 
 Imagine you’ve got a pile of tasks, all with different deadlines and importance. How do you decide what comes first? Well, there are clever rules—like “shortest processing time first” or “earliest due date first”—that can help you tackle your list in a way that boosts productivity and keeps stress in check.
 
 Here are a few key principles you can use to organize your own information:
 
 - Use sorting to bring order and clarity to your lists and schedules.
 
 - Apply filters to cut through the noise and focus on what actually matters.
 
 - Prioritize your tasks based on urgency, importance, or the impact they’ll have.
 
 Let’s bring this down to earth with some examples. Sorting your emails by sender or by subject can help you spot what’s important right away. 
 
 Filtering out low-priority notifications on your phone reduces distractions and gives your mind some breathing room. And if you prioritize your tasks by deadline, you’ll avoid that last-minute scramble and make sure nothing critical slips through the cracks.
 
 But here’s something to pay attention to: computer science also teaches us that perfect organization isn’t always possible. No matter how good your system is, a little bit of chaos will always sneak in. 
 
 And sometimes, trying to keep everything perfectly ordered actually takes more effort than it’s worth. The real trick? Find a balance between structure and flexibility. Use these algorithms as guides, not as unbreakable rules.
 
 Another powerful idea is “satisficing.” It means going for solutions that are good enough, instead of chasing perfection every time. 
 
 Honestly, the time and energy you’d spend trying to make everything flawless could be used somewhere more important. By letting go of the need for absolute order and zeroing in on what really matters, you’ll find it’s much easier to handle information overload—and you’ll feel a lot less stressed.
 
 So, what does “satisficing” look like when it comes to organization?
 
 - Aim for solutions that work well enough, rather than stressing about perfection.
 
 - Accept that a bit of disorder is totally normal—and sometimes, it’s not even a bad thing.
 
 The cool part is, these ideas go way beyond your personal to-do list. They shape how companies run projects, how cities design transit systems, even how societies decide where resources go. 
 
 When you apply sorting, filtering, and prioritization at every level, you can build systems that aren’t just efficient—they’re actually more human, too.

## Chapter 5 Memory, Forgetting, and the Art of Letting Go

**Chapter Five: Memory, Forgetting, and the Art of Letting Go**
 
 Memory can feel like a double-edged sword, can’t it? On one hand, being able to remember facts, faces, and all those little commitments makes life feel manageable. But sometimes, the sheer amount we try to keep track of turns into a heavy load. 
 
 Now, what if you could borrow a trick or two from computer science to lighten that mental burden?
 
 Think about how computers handle their memory. They have strict limits, so they have to be smart about what information they keep close and what they let go. This is where “caching” comes in—a way of holding onto the most important or frequently used data, while moving less relevant stuff to the background, or even deleting it altogether.
 
 So, how does this translate to real life? Well, imagine focusing your memory on the things you use most. Like remembering the names of people you see every day, or keeping your essential passwords handy. For the rest—those less critical details—you can jot them down, or use digital tools to help you out. 
 
 Here’s the thing: forgetting isn’t a failure—it’s actually a smart way to handle complexity. Just like a computer clears out old files to make room for new ones, we need to let go of outdated commitments, irrelevant details, and even the regrets that weigh us down. Sure, it can be tough emotionally, but it’s key for a clear mind and healthy outlook.
 
 There’s another strategy from computer science I want to share: it’s called the “least recently used” method, or LRU for short. Basically, the oldest or least-used info gets tossed first. If you apply that to your own life, it might mean sorting through old files, emails, or even obligations—and letting go of the ones that haven’t mattered for a long time.
 
 And this idea doesn’t just apply to information. It can shape how you think about relationships, your possessions, and even your beliefs. By releasing what’s no longer relevant, you make room for what really matters.
 
 Here’s another insight: externalizing memory. Just as computers use external drives to save space, you can write things down, use calendars, or set reminders. Offloading some of that mental clutter frees up your mind for creativity, problem-solving, and genuine connection with others.
 
 So, by adopting these simple ideas—caching what’s important, regularly letting go of what you no longer need, and using tools to help you remember—you’re not just reducing stress. You’re actually creating more space in your life for growth, learning, and joy.

## Chapter 6 Scheduling, Queues, and the Hidden Logic of Waiting

**Chapter 6: Scheduling, Queues, and the Hidden Logic of Waiting**
 
 Have you ever noticed how much of your life is spent waiting? Maybe it’s standing in line at the grocery store, waiting for an email reply, or just holding out for the right moment to take action. 
 
 Those moments can feel pretty frustrating, can’t they? Like you’re wasting time. But what if I told you that waiting isn’t just a random nuisance—there’s an actual structure to it, and even some clever solutions? 
 
 Computer science digs deep into this problem, and it turns out, the way we schedule things and handle queues can make waiting a lot less painful.
 
 At its heart, scheduling is all about figuring out the best order to get things done—whether you want to save time, be fair, or just get everything running smoothly. Computers deal with this every second, juggling tons of tasks with limited resources. 
 
 But this logic isn’t just for machines. Think about your own to-do list, or the errands you need to run. The big question is: what should you tackle first?
 
 There are a few classic strategies that pop up, both in tech and in daily life:
 
 **First-Come, First-Served:** You handle things in the order they show up. It’s simple and feels fair, but if something takes a long time, everyone else just has to wait.
 
 **Shortest Job First:** Here, you go for the quick wins—finish the fastest tasks first. This cuts down on the average wait, but the big stuff might get stuck waiting forever.
 
 **Round Robin:** You give every task a turn, moving through them in a loop. Nobody hogs all the resources, and everything makes progress.
 
 Picture a doctor’s office for a second. If every patient is seen in the exact order they arrive, someone with a simple question could end up waiting behind a patient with a complicated case. But if the staff triages—meaning, they spot the quick or urgent cases and handle those first—they can actually help more people in less time. 
 
 And if you’re working through your own to-do list, knocking out a few quick tasks early on can really build momentum and clear your head for the bigger stuff.
 
 Now, let’s talk about queues—those familiar waiting lines. Computer science gives us some neat insights here too. The length and speed of any queue comes down to two things: how quickly new tasks show up, and how fast you can handle them. 
 
 If more people arrive than you can help, the line just gets longer—like when the grocery store is slammed and there’s only one cashier. That’s why stores open extra registers during rush hour, or why traffic jams form at those annoying bottlenecks.
 
 So, how can you manage queues more effectively? Here are a couple of tried-and-true tricks:
 
 First, boost your capacity when things get busy. Bring in more help, or open extra lanes, to cut down on wait times.
 
 Second, split up your queues—think express lanes for folks with just a few items, or different lines for different types of tasks. It’s all about matching the flow to the need.
 
 Here’s the thing worth remembering: understanding how scheduling and queues work can actually make waiting feel less frustrating. Most delays aren’t your fault—they’re just part of how systems work. 
 
 Once you start to notice these patterns, you can plan ahead, set more realistic expectations, and maybe even find ways to use that waiting time—so it feels less wasted, and maybe even a little bit productive.

## Chapter 7 The Power and Pitfalls of Randomness

Chapter 7: The Power and Pitfalls of Randomness

 

 Have you ever thought about how randomness shapes our world? Most of the time, we think of randomness as pure chaos—something that gets in the way of order and predictability. But here’s the twist: in computer science, randomness can actually be a secret weapon. It helps us solve tough problems, break out of deadlocks, and sometimes, even make better choices. By embracing a bit of randomness, instead of always trying to control everything, we might just find some unexpected advantages—both in technology and in everyday life.

 

 Let’s start with computers. Imagine you’re searching for the best solution to a complicated problem, and you keep running into the same old patterns, going in circles, or getting stuck. What if you could just shake things up a little? That’s exactly what algorithms like simulated annealing do—they introduce random jumps, letting the computer break free from ruts and explore totally new paths. It’s like taking a different route home just to see if you discover something interesting.

 

 So, when does it make sense to use randomness? Well, here are a few moments when it really shines:
 
 When you’re facing a tie or a deadlock and all options look equally good, a random choice can help break the stalemate.
 
 If you’re stuck repeating the same old thing, randomness opens up new possibilities.
 
 And when it comes to sharing resources or attention fairly—like picking who does what chore—random selection can keep things balanced.

 

 Now, let’s bring this back to daily life. Ever been torn between two restaurants and just can’t decide? Flipping a coin might sound silly, but it actually moves things forward when you’re stuck. And if you’ve ever drawn names out of a hat to decide who gets the last slice of pizza, you’ve already used randomness to keep things fair.

 

 Randomness isn’t just about making decisions or solving problems—it’s also a spark for creativity. Think about those “aha!” moments that seem to come out of nowhere, or the chance encounters that lead to great ideas. By leaving a little room for the unplanned, you open the door to discoveries you’d never make if you stuck to a strict plan.

 

 But let’s be real—too much randomness isn’t always a good thing. If everything’s left to chance, things can quickly get messy, effort gets wasted, and you might even miss out on improving what’s already working. The real trick is using randomness on purpose, as a way to break free when you’re stuck, or to explore new options, but always coming back to structure when it’s time to move forward.

 

 So, how do you use randomness strategically?
 
 Shake things up with a random choice when you’re stuck, but don’t forget to return to a plan once you’re moving again.
 
 And consider leaving space in your schedule for something unexpected—you never know what kind of creative connection or new idea might pop up.

 

 Here’s the real takeaway: when you understand how randomness works, you can actually use it to your advantage—without letting things spiral out of control. Striking that balance between order and chance is at the heart of both computer science... and a well-lived life.

## Chapter 8 Overfitting, Generalization, and the Wisdom of Simplicity

**Chapter 8: Overfitting, Generalization, and the Wisdom of Simplicity**
 
 You know, as we try to make sense of the world, it's only natural to look for patterns and explanations. But here’s the twist—computer science actually gives us a warning about this instinct. There’s a hidden trap called **overfitting**. 
 
 So, what’s overfitting all about? Imagine building a model, a rule, or even just a habit that’s been shaped way too closely by past data. It starts picking up on every little detail—even the random noise—rather than the real underlying signal. 
 
 The problem? That system will look brilliant on familiar situations, but the moment something new comes along, it just can’t keep up. 
 
 And honestly, overfitting doesn’t just happen with computers—it pops up in everyday life too. Think about a student who memorizes every detail from last year’s exams. Sure, they might ace the same questions, but throw in a new twist, and suddenly they’re lost. Or maybe a business that makes every decision based on last year’s trends—they could easily miss out on fresh opportunities or be blindsided by new threats.
 
 So, how do you spot overfitting in your own life or work? There are a couple of telltale signs:
 
 You might notice rules or habits that work perfectly in one specific situation but fall apart anywhere else. Or maybe you’re so caught up in the small details, you lose sight of the bigger picture.
 
 The real solution here is something called **generalization**. It’s all about hunting for simple, sturdy rules that capture the heart of a problem—without getting distracted by every little exception.
 
 In computer science, this means keeping models less complex and always testing them with fresh, unseen data. In everyday life, it’s about choosing habits and principles that are flexible, not rigid routines that only work in one spot.
 
 So, what can you actually do to generalize better? Here are a few strategies:
 
 Look for patterns that seem to hold up across different situations, not just the familiar ones. Try out new rules or habits in places you haven’t been before—see if they still work. And maybe most importantly, don’t be afraid of simplicity. Sometimes, a simple rule that’s “good enough” is better than a complicated one that tries to cover every possible detail.
 
 Here’s a metaphor that might help: think of the difference between a map and the actual territory. If a map tries to include every single detail, it just becomes impossible to read. A good map highlights what matters most—sure, it leaves out some things, but that’s what makes it useful. Your systems, your beliefs, even your routines, work the same way. The best ones are simple enough to use, and broad enough to adapt when things change.
 
 So, next time you’re building something—a plan, a model, or just a way of doing things—watch out for the trap of overfitting. Embrace generalization. Favor simplicity. That’s how you create systems, both technical and personal, that can really stand up to change and keep working, no matter what the world throws your way.

## Chapter 9 Game Theory and the Social Side of Algorithms

**Chapter 9: Game Theory and the Social Side of Algorithms**
 
 Have you ever noticed how some of the trickiest decisions in life don’t really happen in a vacuum? Whether you’re figuring out how to split chores with your roommate, negotiating a salary, or even eyeing that last slice of pizza, what you do often hinges on what someone else decides. 
 
 That’s where game theory and computer science come in—they give us a way to make sense of these social puzzles, and maybe even make better choices together.
 
 Game theory is all about scenarios where your outcome depends not just on what you do, but on what others do too. Classic examples pop up everywhere—think of the prisoner’s dilemma, where you have to choose between cooperating or competing, or the tragedy of the commons, where everyone’s looking out for themselves, and the whole group ends up worse off.
 
 Let’s break down a few of the big ideas from game theory that show up in everyday life.
 
 First, there’s the **Nash Equilibrium**: that’s a situation where nobody can do better just by changing their own strategy—unless someone else changes theirs too. Imagine two drivers stuck at a four-way stop, each waiting for the other to go. If both keep waiting, nobody gets anywhere, but if they both go at once, it’s chaos. The Nash Equilibrium is that sweet spot where everyone’s choices line up, and nobody has a reason to change.
 
 Then there are **zero-sum games**, where one person’s win is another’s loss—like a game of chess or splitting a fixed pie. But not everything’s a zero-sum situation. In **non-zero-sum games**, working together can make things better for everyone. Think about a group project where sharing ideas leads to a result way better than what any one person could do alone.
 
 These ideas help explain why conflicts sometimes drag on, or why it’s tough to get everyone on board with cleaning up a shared kitchen. If each person’s waiting for the other to make a move, nothing gets done. But once you understand the incentives at play, you can start to find smarter ways forward—like taking turns, or setting up a simple chore rotation.
 
 Game theory also shows just how powerful communication and trust can be. Sometimes, just talking honestly about what everyone wants can turn a tense standoff into real teamwork. And when you need a tie-breaker, tools like voting, auctions, or even random draws can make decisions feel more fair for everyone.
 
 So, how can you actually use game theory in your daily life? Start by figuring out what everyone cares about, and what’s holding them back. Look for ways to line up individual goals with what’s best for the group. And when things get tricky, lean on fair decision-making methods to keep things moving.
 
 When you see social challenges as a kind of algorithm—a puzzle with incentives, strategies, and outcomes—you’ll find you can handle tough interactions with more empathy and confidence. 
 
 And maybe, just maybe, you’ll find it easier to keep the peace over that last slice of pizza, too.
