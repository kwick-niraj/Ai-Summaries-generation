## Chapter 1 Rethinking Management—Why Old Rules Fall Short

**Chapter 1: Rethinking Management—Why Old Rules Fall Short**
 
 When most people take on a management role, they tend to carry with them a suitcase full of old beliefs. Things like, "treat everyone the same," "focus on fixing weaknesses," or "trust experience and training above all else." 
 
 On the surface, these ideas sound pretty reasonable, right? But if you look closely at teams where people are really thriving—where they’re doing their best work and enjoying it—you’ll notice something surprising: the most effective managers aren’t following those old rules. In fact, they quietly break them. Instead, they zero in on what makes each person unique.
 
 Here’s the foundation of this new style of management, and it might seem simple, but it’s powerful: people aren’t blank slates. Every person brings their own mix of talents, motivations, and ways of thinking to the table. If you try to shape everyone into the same mold, or push them to improve in areas where they just don’t have a natural spark, you end up with frustration—and a whole lot of mediocrity.
 
 The best managers flip the script. Rather than asking, "How can I fix this person’s weaknesses?" they start by wondering, "What is this person naturally good at?" Then, they build roles and responsibilities around those strengths.
 
 And this isn’t just a trendy idea—it’s backed by years of research. When thousands of managers and employees were studied, a clear pattern showed up. Teams led by managers who focused on strengths didn’t just do a little better—they consistently outperformed the ones led by managers stuck on fixing weaknesses. We’re talking about higher productivity, better employee retention, and more satisfaction all around.
 
 Let’s put this into a real-life example. Imagine you’re tending a garden. Traditional management would have you water every plant the same amount, no matter if it’s a cactus or a fern. But great managers? They pay attention to the differences. They don’t waste time trying to turn a cactus into a fern. Instead, they help each plant thrive in its own way.
 
 This approach also turns the spotlight on another old belief: that experience and training are the main ingredients for success. Sure, both are valuable. But they can’t replace raw talent. Some folks are just naturally drawn to certain kinds of work, and all the training in the world can’t make someone love a role that doesn’t fit them. The best managers know this. They spend more time selecting someone who’s right for the job, rather than trying to force a square peg into a round hole.
 
 One of the biggest takeaways from this research is something you might not expect: the relationship between an employee and their direct manager is the single most important factor in whether people feel engaged and decide to stick around. People don’t really leave companies—they leave managers. When someone feels truly seen, valued, and supported in their strengths, they’re much more likely to stay and give their best.
 
 Now, embracing this new mindset about management can feel a bit uncomfortable at first. It means letting go of the idea that there’s just one right way to do things, and instead, accepting the real, sometimes messy individuality of people. It means admitting that not everyone can—or should—be great at everything. And that it’s way more effective to build on what’s already strong, instead of focusing all your energy on fixing what’s weak.
 
 So, here’s the key takeaway from this first step toward being a great manager: don’t just accept the old rules at face value. Question them. Look for what actually works. Focus on strengths, select for talent, and build strong, genuine relationships. From here, it’s all about putting these principles into practice, one step at a time.

 So what does this look like in practice?
 
 First, there’s **hiring for talent**. Instead of just relying on interviews or past jobs, smart managers use targeted questions—maybe even scenarios—to uncover someone’s natural strengths. They pay close attention to what’s always come easily for a candidate, especially when facing challenges.
 
 Next, they focus on **building on strengths**. Once someone’s in the right spot, it’s all about helping them do even more of what they do best. Maybe you give a detail-oriented person more responsibility for quality control, or you let a creative thinker lead the next big brainstorm.
 
 And, when it comes to weaknesses? Rather than trying to fix every flaw, top managers find ways to work around them. That might mean pairing teammates with complementary strengths, or simply redesigning tasks so everyone’s playing to their natural abilities.
 
 Picture a sports team for a second. A great coach doesn’t try to turn the goalkeeper into a striker. They help each player master their natural position. The same goes for the workplace: when people focus on what they do best, the whole team wins.
 
 Focusing on talent also changes how we think about development. Instead of rolling out generic training for everyone, it becomes a personal journey for each team member. The real question shifts from “How can this person fix their weaknesses?” to “How can they use their strengths even more effectively?” That’s where you see real engagement, faster growth, and much better results.
 
 And here’s something to remember: great managers turn into talent scouts. They learn to spot those subtle signs of potential and build environments where people can experiment, learn, and grow. This takes curiosity, patience, and letting go of the idea that one solution fits all.
 
 In the end, the real power of talent is how it unlocks energy and motivation. When people get to do work that matches their natural abilities, they’re more engaged, more productive, and just plain happier. That’s the bedrock of every great team.
