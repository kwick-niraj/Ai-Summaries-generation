## Chapter 2 The Difference Between Racist, Assimilationist, and Antiracist Thinking

Chapter 2: The Difference Between Racist, Assimilationist, and Antiracist Thinking.
 
 When you’re growing up, you probably hear things like, “Why can’t everyone just get along?” or “We should all be the same.” At first, those ideas sound kind and simple, right? But if you look a little closer, you’ll find there’s a lot more going on beneath the surface.
 
 Whenever people talk about race, you’ll usually come across three main ways of thinking. Some folks believe that differences between groups mean some people are better or worse—that’s what we call racist thinking. Then there’s another group that says everyone should just blend in, act the same, and not make a fuss—that’s assimilationist thinking. And finally, there are those who believe our differences should be respected, and that we should call out unfairness when we see it. That’s antiracist thinking.
 
 Picture this: a teenager walks into a new school for the first time. At lunch, they notice students from different backgrounds tend to sit with people who look and talk like them. Later, a teacher says, “Let’s all just be American. Don’t worry about where you’re from.” It sounds simple, but the student can’t help but wonder—should they try to fit in? Or is it okay to stand out? See, even small moments like this are shaped by those bigger ideas.
 
 So let’s break it down. Racist thinking is when someone believes that some groups are born better, smarter, or more deserving than others. It’s the idea that skin color, culture, or background somehow make people “more” or “less.” It’s been used to excuse a whole lot of harm—from slavery to unfair laws—and it doesn’t always show up as shouting or anger. Sometimes, it’s just a quiet assumption that certain people are “trouble” or “lazy,” without any real reason.
 
 Assimilationist thinking is a little sneakier. It says, “Sure, everyone’s equal—as long as you act like us.” In real life, that often means folks from minority backgrounds are expected to drop their language, traditions, or even their names just to fit in. The message is, “Hey, you can belong... but only if you change.” And for a lot of people, that’s like being asked to give up a piece of who they are.
 
 Then there’s antiracist thinking. This approach doesn’t pretend differences don’t exist. Instead, it sees those differences as real—and valuable. Antiracist thinkers ask tough questions: Why are some people treated unfairly? What can we do to fix it? This isn’t always comfortable. It means having hard conversations and sometimes, standing up to people in charge.
 
 If you look back in history, you’ll see these three approaches shaping big decisions. Take the civil rights movement, for instance. Some leaders told Black Americans to prove they were “just as good” as whites by dressing or talking a certain way. But others argued—loudly—that the real problem wasn’t with Black people at all. It was with the unfair rules and beliefs holding them back.
 
 It’s easy to think that just not being racist is enough. But here’s something to pay attention to: if we ignore unfairness, it’s only going to stick around. Real change? That happens when people are willing to see the problem—and actually do something about it. That’s what sets antiracist thinking apart. It’s not just about what you believe; it’s about taking action.
