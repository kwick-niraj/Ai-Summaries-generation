## Chapter 1 Rethinking Negotiation: Moving Beyond Positions

Chapter 1. Rethinking Negotiation: Moving Beyond Positions
 
 Let’s face it—negotiation is everywhere. Whether you’re sorting out who’s doing the dishes at home, working through a disagreement with a coworker, or trying to finalize a business deal, you’re negotiating. But, if you’re like most people, you probably see negotiation as a tug-of-war—a contest where each side picks a position, stands their ground, and then bargains until someone gives in. Or, even worse, both sides end up settling for less than they really wanted.
 
 That’s what we call positional bargaining. It’s familiar, sure, but more often than not, it leads to frustration, damaged relationships, and outcomes that just don’t feel satisfying.
 
 Picture this: two people trying to agree on the price of a used car. One throws out a high number, the other counters low, and they slowly inch their way toward the middle. Pretty soon, each person is more focused on defending their number than actually solving the problem. The whole thing can get tense, even a little hostile. Winning the argument becomes the goal, not finding a good solution.
 
 Now, this approach might get you through a simple, one-time deal. But what happens when the stakes are higher, the issues get complicated, or you really care about your relationship with the other side? That’s where positional bargaining tends to fall apart.
 
 Here’s why: when people dig in their heels and stick to their positions, they stop listening and start defending. It turns into a battle of egos. Creative solutions? They’re often left behind. And even if an agreement is reached, both sides might walk away feeling like they’ve lost something important.
 
 So, what if you didn’t have to choose between being tough and being fair? What if negotiation wasn’t about winning or losing?
 
 There’s a better way, and it starts by shifting the focus from positions to principles. Instead of arguing over fixed demands, both sides can work together to understand the real interests behind those demands. This approach—principled negotiation—rests on four simple but powerful ideas:
 
 **First**, separate the people from the problem. In other words, treat your relationship and the substance of the negotiation as two distinct things.
 
 **Second**, focus on interests, not positions. Dig a little deeper—what are the real needs and motivations behind each demand?
 
 **Third**, invent options for mutual gain. Work together to find creative solutions that benefit both sides.
 
 And **fourth**, insist on using objective criteria. Make decisions based on fair standards, not just who can shout louder or apply more pressure.
 
 Here’s the thing: principled negotiation isn’t about being soft or giving in. It’s about being firm on the issues, while staying open and respectful with the people involved. You can stand your ground without being aggressive—and cooperate without being weak.
 
 Let’s make this real. Imagine two departments at work, both fighting over a limited budget. If each one just demands a bigger slice, it quickly turns adversarial. But if they step back and discuss why they need the funds—maybe one needs new equipment while the other’s facing a staffing crunch—they can start to look for ways to help each other out. Maybe they share resources or adjust timelines so everyone’s needs are met.
 
 At its core, principled negotiation isn’t just a set of tactics—it’s a mindset. It invites you to see negotiation as a chance for joint problem-solving, not a contest. And that shift in perspective? It opens the door to agreements that last longer, spark more creativity, and genuinely satisfy everyone involved.

Suddenly, the real issue—the project timeline—gets buried under a pile of personal frustrations.

 

 So, how do you keep things on track? The key is to separate the people from the problem. Treat the relationship as its own thing, separate from what you’re actually negotiating. When you do that, you can tackle both without letting one mess up the other.

 

 There are three big areas where people issues usually pop up:

 

 First, **perceptions**. Everyone brings their own viewpoint—shaped by their own experiences, assumptions, and expectations. It’s so easy to misunderstand someone’s intentions or motives and jump to the wrong conclusion.

 

 Second, **emotions**. Let’s be honest, negotiations can stir up some strong feelings—anger, anxiety, even resentment. If these emotions aren’t recognized and managed, they can cloud your judgment and make things escalate pretty quickly.

 

 And finally, **communication**. Sometimes, it’s the way we talk—or don’t talk—that causes trouble. Unclear language, constant interruptions, or just not really listening can all lead to confusion and mistrust.

 

 So, what can you actually do to manage these challenges? Here are some practical steps:

 

 Try putting yourself in the other person’s shoes. See the situation from their perspective. You don’t have to agree with them, but understanding where they’re coming from can go a long way.

 

 Then, talk openly about perceptions. Bring assumptions out into the open and make sure everyone’s on the same page about intentions.

 

 Don’t forget to acknowledge emotions—yours and theirs. Sometimes, just naming what you’re feeling can take the sting out of it.

 

 Listen actively. Give your full attention, reflect back what you hear, and double-check that you understand. It shows respect and builds trust.

 

 When you talk about the issue, speak about yourself—use “I” statements to share how you feel and what you need, instead of blaming or accusing.

 

 And most importantly, focus on building a working relationship. Treat the negotiation like a team effort, not a fight. Even small gestures of trust and respect can make a big difference.

 

 Think about a family trying to pick a spot for their next vacation. Maybe someone feels left out of the decision. By recognizing that and inviting them to share their thoughts, the group can move past any resentment and focus on finding a destination that works for everyone.

 

 Separating people from the problem doesn’t mean you ignore feelings or pretend relationships don’t matter. It means you face them head-on, respectfully, so they don’t derail the conversation. And here’s the thing—when people feel truly heard and understood, they’re way more likely to work together and find a good solution.

 Here’s something to keep in mind—your BATNA isn’t a tool for threats. It’s your private yardstick, helping you decide what’s actually good for you. It keeps you from saying yes to a deal that’s worse than just walking away.
