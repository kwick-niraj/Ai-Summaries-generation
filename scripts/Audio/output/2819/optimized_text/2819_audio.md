## Chapter 3 Myths and Misconceptions: Why the Truth Gets Lost

Chapter 3: Myths and Misconceptions—Why the Truth Gets Lost
 
 You know, when people talk about abusive relationships, there are a few stories that get tossed around all the time. Maybe you’ve heard them, too. Things like—he must’ve had a rough childhood, or maybe he just drinks too much, or he can’t control his temper. 
 
 These explanations feel comforting, don’t they? They sort of promise that with enough love or patience—or maybe therapy—things will eventually get better.
 
 But for anyone actually living through it, those stories rarely line up with real life. Imagine sitting in a counselor’s office, listening as your partner talks about his stressful job or his tough childhood. The counselor nods, suggests anger management, and for a little while, it seems like things might change. 
 
 But then, at home—it’s the same old pattern. The apologies come, sure, but so do the outbursts. Nothing really shifts.
 
 One of the biggest myths out there is that abuse happens because of anger issues. But here’s something to pay attention to: most controlling partners can manage their anger just fine—when it suits them. They don’t blow up at their boss or their friends. Those outbursts? They’re saved for the people closest to them, where the risk is lowest.
 
 There’s another belief that does a lot of damage—the idea that victims “provoke” the abuse, or that both people are equally responsible for the conflict. This idea sticks with people, leaving them feeling isolated and ashamed, like if they just tried harder, things would get better.
 
 But the truth is, no one can make someone else be abusive. The responsibility always lies with the person who chooses to control, belittle, or hurt.
 
 Alcohol and drugs get blamed a lot, too. And while substance use can definitely make things worse, it doesn’t plant the attitudes that drive abuse in the first place. Many controlling partners are just as manipulative when they’re sober. Sure, alcohol might lower their inhibitions, but it doesn’t create the need for power.
 
 Even saying that abusers are simply “sick” or “broken” misses the real issue. Most are able to function perfectly well in other parts of their lives. They hold down jobs, keep up friendships, and can even be loving parents—when it serves their interests. 
 
 The problem isn’t that they can’t be kind. It’s that they choose not to be, especially when kindness gets in the way of control.
 
 Why do these myths stick around? Well, they give us easy answers to something incredibly complicated. They let friends, family, and even professionals sidestep the uncomfortable truth: abuse is a choice. It’s rooted in entitlement, not in uncontrollable emotions or outside circumstances.
 
 For anyone trying to make sense of their own experience, letting go of these myths can be tough—but it’s also freeing. It means facing that real change probably won’t happen unless the controlling partner is willing to challenge his own beliefs. 
 
 And most importantly, it means recognizing that the abuse was never your fault. That responsibility never belonged to you.
 
 Finding that kind of clarity? It can be the very first step toward taking back your sense of self.
