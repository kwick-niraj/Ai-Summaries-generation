## Chapter 1 How Children First Notice Race and Difference

**Chapter One: How Children First Notice Race and Difference**
 
 Picture this—you’re walking through a grocery store with your little one, and out of nowhere, that small, curious voice asks, “Why is her skin a different color than mine?” Or maybe your preschooler points out that their friend’s hair is “so curly.” Those moments can really catch you off guard, can’t they? 
 
 For a lot of adults, especially those who grew up hearing “don’t talk about it,” there’s this rush of discomfort. The instinct might be to hush the child, change the subject, or give a quick, vague answer—just hoping the topic will quietly disappear.
 
 But here’s the thing—kids notice everything. Even before they’re talking, they’re picking up on who lives in their neighborhood, who their parents spend time with, and who shows up in their bedtime stories. By the time they get to school, they’ve already soaked up a world of silent messages about who fits in, who gets celebrated, and who gets ignored. 
 
 And for a lot of kids, their very first lesson about race doesn’t come from a book—it comes from how adults react to those innocent questions.
 
 Take a kindergarten classroom, for example. A teacher overhears a group of kids sorting themselves out by who can “get tan” and who “just gets sunburned.” At first, it sounds like just a game, but really, it’s a peek into how kids try to make sense of what they see. 
 
 For some children—especially those from families of color—these conversations start early and stick around. Maybe they notice the way someone watches their parents in a store, or how people reach out to touch their hair without even asking. There’s a feeling of being noticed, sometimes in ways that feel uncomfortable or unfair.
 
 Now, for white children, race often stays invisible. Their families might not talk about it much, and they might mostly be surrounded by people who look like them. When race does come up, it’s sometimes in the form of, “we don’t see color.” That sounds nice, but for kids, it can be confusing. They pick up on the tension, the way adults’ voices get quiet, and learn—maybe without anyone saying it out loud—that some things just aren’t okay to talk about.
 
 But here’s something important: staying silent doesn’t actually protect anyone. Instead, kids end up filling in the blanks with whatever they see on TV, hear from friends, or catch in passing comments. If a child hears a joke about a certain group, or notices that certain classmates always seem to get in trouble, those little moments start to add up. And without some honest guidance, those ideas can turn into stereotypes.
 
 In families where race is front and center every day, the conversations happen sooner—and they matter more. A Black parent might need to explain to their child why they have to be extra careful in some situations, or why a teacher might expect less from them. These aren’t just nice-to-have talks—they’re about safety, about dignity, and about helping a child understand their place in the world.
 
 As kids get older, their understanding of race grows. They start to notice who gets praised, who gets in trouble, and who gets left out. A Latina student might wonder why her history class barely mentions people who look like her. An Asian American boy might find himself labeled “the smart one,” as if that’s the only thing anyone sees in him. And these experiences shape not just how they see others, but how they see themselves.
 
 Most adults really underestimate how early kids start picking up on all of this. They hope that by not talking about race, they’re shielding their children from prejudice. But the truth is, the world isn’t silent—and neither are kids’ questions.
 
 So, here’s the key: it’s not about whether children notice race—they absolutely do. The real question is, will the adults in their lives help them make sense of what they see? Will they do it with honesty, compassion, and the kind of guidance that empowers kids to understand and celebrate differences, rather than fear them?

 Adolescence is a time of searching, and race is just one piece of the puzzle every young person is trying to put together. The cafeteria table is just one example of how students navigate these waters, looking for places where they feel seen and understood. Ultimately, the real challenge isn’t about forcing everyone to mix together. It’s about building environments where every student feels safe to explore who they are—without fear, and without shame.
