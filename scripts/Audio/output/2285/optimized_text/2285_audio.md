## Chapter 3 How We Interpret Motives—Our Own and Others’

**Chapter 3: How We Interpret Motives—Our Own and Others’**
 
 Let’s dive into one of the biggest puzzles in social life: figuring out why people act the way they do.
 
 Social psychology shows us that making sense of motives isn’t always as straightforward as we’d like to think. 
 We’re all natural mind readers—constantly guessing why someone did what they did. But here’s the tricky part: we’re working with limited info, and our own biases and assumptions are always at play.
 
 Think about this for a second—have you ever noticed how easy it is to justify your own actions, yet be suspicious of someone else’s? 
 
 That’s what’s called the actor-observer bias. For example, if you show up late to a meeting, you might blame it on traffic or a last-minute emergency. 
 But if someone else walks in late? It’s tempting to chalk it up to carelessness or poor planning.
 
 It’s a double standard we all fall into, and it can lead to all kinds of misunderstandings and even conflict.
 
 There’s more: we all rely on mental shortcuts—what psychologists call heuristics—to make quick judgments about others. 
 These shortcuts help us get through the day, but they can also lead us astray.
 
 Take the availability heuristic, for example. If a dramatic news story comes to mind easily, we’ll probably overestimate how likely that event is to happen. 
 Or consider the confirmation bias—where we naturally notice information that backs up what we already believe, while ignoring anything that doesn’t fit.
 
 Another layer to all this? Impression management. We know others are watching and forming opinions, so we sometimes highlight certain motives and hide others.
 
 The result? It gets even tougher to tell what’s genuine and what’s just strategic.
 
 So, how do we navigate these challenges and really get better at understanding each other?
 
 Here are a few practical ideas grounded in social psychology:
 
 First, try to catch yourself judging others more harshly than you’d judge yourself. Next time someone does something that bugs you, pause and ask—could there be a reasonable explanation you haven’t thought of yet?
 
 And remember, we’re not actually mind readers. Instead of assuming you know why someone acted a certain way, try asking them directly or look for more information.
 
 Also, keep an eye out for those mental shortcuts. If you feel yourself jumping to a conclusion—especially in a heated moment—take a breath and reflect before reacting.
 
 Using these strategies can really make a difference. They help reduce misunderstandings, build empathy, and remind us all to stay humble.
 
 After all, everyone makes mistakes, and understanding each other is an ongoing journey.
 
 By becoming more mindful of how we interpret motives—both our own and others’—we can strengthen our connections, and respond to the people around us with a little more patience and understanding.
