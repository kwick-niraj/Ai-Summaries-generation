## Chapter 3 The Four Laws of Behavior Change—Making Habits Obvious, Attractive, Easy, and Satisfying

Let’s dive into Chapter 3: The Four Laws of Behavior Change—making habits obvious, attractive, easy, and satisfying.
 
 Building new habits, or breaking old ones, can feel overwhelming sometimes, right? But when you break the process down into four simple laws, it suddenly feels doable. Think of these laws as a roadmap—a way to design behaviors that stick and make lasting change more manageable.
 

 **First up: Make it obvious.**
 
 Here’s the thing—most habits don’t fail because you lack willpower. They fail because the cue, or the trigger, isn’t clear. If you want a habit to take hold, you’ve got to shine a spotlight on it.
 
 So, how do you do that? Try setting an **implementation intention**. That’s just a fancy way of saying, “Decide exactly when and where this habit will happen.” Like, “After breakfast, I’ll meditate for five minutes in the living room.” 
 
 Or, use **habit stacking**: link your new habit to one you already do. For example, “After I brush my teeth, I’ll read one page of a book.” 
 
 By anchoring your new habit to something that’s already automatic, you make it way more obvious—and way easier to remember.

 
 **Second: Make it attractive.**
 
 Let’s be honest, we’re all wired to chase what feels good and avoid what’s uncomfortable. If a habit is appealing, you’re just more likely to stick with it.
 
 Here’s a simple trick: **temptation bundling**. Pair your habit with something you love—like only listening to your favorite podcast while you exercise. Suddenly, the workout is something you look forward to.
 
 And don’t underestimate the power of **social influence**. If you surround yourself with people who already have the habit you want, it’ll feel much more normal—and a lot more attractive—to do it too.

 
 **Third: Make it easy.**
 
 Let’s keep it real—if something’s complicated or takes a lot of effort, chances are, it’ll fall by the wayside. The easier you make a habit, the more automatic it becomes.
 
 Start by **reducing friction**. If you want to work out in the morning, lay out your clothes the night before. That way, you’re not scrambling around half-awake, looking for your sneakers.
 
 And here’s a game-changer: the **two-minute rule**. Scale the habit down to something that takes two minutes or less. Instead of reading a whole chapter, just read a single paragraph. The key is to make starting so easy you can’t say no.

 
 **And finally: Make it satisfying.**
 
 Our brains love immediate rewards. When a habit feels good right away, you’ll want to do it again.
 
 Try tracking your progress. Use a calendar or a journal, and mark down every time you stick with your habit. Watching those streaks grow is surprisingly rewarding.
 
 Or, reward yourself with a small treat or an enjoyable activity after you finish. The more satisfying the habit feels, the more likely you’ll repeat it.

 
 Here’s a neat twist: you can flip these four laws to break a bad habit, too. 
 
 Want to cut down on screen time? Make the cue **invisible**—keep your devices out of sight. Make the habit **unattractive**—remind yourself of the downsides of too much screen time. Make it **difficult**—add extra steps before you can scroll. And finally, make it **unsatisfying**—track the time wasted and see how it adds up.
 

 By thoughtfully using these four laws, you can design your environment and routines so that positive change feels natural. 
 
 And that’s the real secret: when you make good habits obvious, attractive, easy, and satisfying, you set yourself up to succeed—almost on autopilot.
