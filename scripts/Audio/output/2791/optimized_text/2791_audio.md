## Chapter 2 Building Trust and Reducing Resistance with Simple Phrases

**Chapter 2: Building Trust and Reducing Resistance with Simple Phrases**
 
 Let’s dive into something that sits at the heart of every real connection—trust. Think about it: without trust, even the strongest argument can feel like it’s hitting a brick wall. 
 
 So, how do you actually build trust and gently lower people’s resistance, especially when you’re hoping for agreement or buy-in?
 
 Well, people are naturally cautious when they sense someone’s trying to persuade them. If it feels like they’re being pushed, their guard goes up—fast. The trick is to talk in a way that feels safe, genuine, and respectful, so trust can grow on its own.
 
 One of the simplest and most powerful ways to do this? Use words that show you truly get where the other person is coming from. Just a small phrase like, “I understand this might not be the right time for you,” can work wonders. It tells someone, “Hey, I respect your situation,” and it takes all the pressure off needing to say yes right away.
 
 Here’s another tip: use language that makes hesitation or uncertainty feel normal. When people realize their doubts are totally valid—and that others have felt the same way—they relax. Try saying, “Many people feel this way at first,” or, “It’s completely normal to have questions.” It instantly helps someone feel like they belong, instead of feeling singled out or defensive.
 
 Now, let’s talk about honesty. It’s tempting to make big promises, but absolute statements can backfire if they sound too good to be true. If you want real credibility, be straightforward. Instead of, “This will definitely work for you,” try, “This has worked well for others in similar situations.” See the difference? You’re setting realistic expectations, and people can sense that you’re not trying to oversell.
 
 Sometimes, you know resistance is coming. Here’s where a little “pre-framing” goes a long way. Bring up possible concerns before the other person does, and do it in a way that feels open, not confrontational. For example, “You might be wondering if this is really worth your time.” It opens the door for honest conversation, and shows you’re really tuned in to what’s on their mind.
 
 Let’s make this practical. Here are a few phrases you can use right away:
 
 Acknowledge concerns: “I can see why you might feel that way.”
 
 Normalize doubts: “Others have had similar questions.”
 
 Set realistic expectations: “This may not be for everyone, but it could be a good fit for you.”
 
 And invite honest feedback: “If you have any reservations, I’d love to hear them.”
 
 These phrases work because they shift the conversation from a tug-of-war to a partnership. When someone feels heard and respected, they’re way more likely to open up and have a real, productive conversation with you.
 
 But here’s something you can’t forget: trust isn’t built overnight. It grows with every genuine word and action you take. The right language opens the door—but it’s your sincerity and follow-through that keep it open. People pick up on insincerity in a heartbeat, so use these techniques only if you truly care about the person in front of you.
 
 So, here’s the key takeaway: building trust and reducing resistance isn’t just about choosing the right words. It’s about showing empathy, being honest, and working together. Use simple, thoughtful phrases that show you understand the other person’s point of view, and you’ll create the kind of trust that leads to real influence—and relationships that last.

 Framing isn’t just about making things sound good—it can also create a gentle sense of urgency or importance, but without using any high-pressure tactics. For instance, saying, “Many people are taking advantage of this opportunity right now,” taps into our natural tendency to follow what others are doing, making the action feel timely and valued.

 And finally, emphasize positive outcomes: “Imagine how things could improve if…”

 So, here’s the key takeaway: Framing is a powerful tool for making your messages more compelling and easier to accept.
