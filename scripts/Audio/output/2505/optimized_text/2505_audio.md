## Chapter 2 The Five Core Skills of Drawing: Foundations for Seeing

Let’s dive into Chapter 2: **“The Five Core Skills of Drawing: Foundations for Seeing.”**
 
 Now that we’ve reimagined what drawing can be, it’s time to get practical. There are five fundamental skills that, together, make up the backbone of this method. They aren’t just techniques—they’re new ways of seeing, and like any skill, you can strengthen them with practice.
 
 
 **First up, perceiving edges—lines and contours.**
 This is all about noticing where one shape ends and another begins. Sometimes, it’s a clear, bold line, but often it’s just a subtle shift in tone, color, or texture. Learning to follow these edges with your eyes—and then with your pencil—is the gateway to drawing what you really see, not just what you think is there.
 
 Here’s a classic exercise: try outlining your hand or an object without looking down at your paper. It sounds tricky, but it’s a powerful way to connect your observation and your hand—skipping that part of your brain that wants to label everything.
 
 
 **Next, perceiving spaces—or, more specifically, negative space.**
 This skill asks you to look at the spaces around and between objects, not just the objects themselves. Those gaps and backgrounds are just as important as the subjects you’re drawing. For example, if you focus on the shapes between your fingers instead of the fingers, you’ll often get a more accurate result. This shift in focus can help you see proportions and placements much more clearly.
 
 
 **The third core skill is perceiving relationships—proportion and perspective.**
 Here, you’re comparing the sizes, angles, and positions of different parts of what you’re drawing. Think about how a chair’s legs relate to its seat, or how a tree’s branches angle away from its trunk. Many artists use a pencil or even their thumb to measure and judge these distances and angles. This isn’t about guessing—it’s about really seeing how everything fits together.
 
 
 **Fourth, perceiving lights and shadows—value.**
 This is where your drawing starts to feel three-dimensional. Instead of seeing just flat outlines, you begin to notice how light creates highlights, mid-tones, and deep shadows. When you train yourself to spot the darkest darks and the brightest lights—and everything in between—you’re able to build depth and form that makes your drawing come alive.
 
 
 **And finally, perceiving the whole—the Gestalt.**
 This is about seeing your subject as a unified whole, not just as a bunch of separate parts. Imagine stepping back and taking in the overall shape, the balance, and even the mood of what you’re drawing. This holistic approach is subtle but powerful. It’s what gives your drawing its energy and coherence—its sense of being alive.
 
 
 These five skills work together, each one supporting and strengthening the others. As you get better at seeing edges, for instance, you’ll also start to notice negative spaces more easily. Progress isn’t always a straight line; with each drawing, you’ll find yourself circling back, refining these ways of seeing, and discovering new insights.
 
 To help you on this journey, you’ll find exercises designed not just to boost your technical skills, but to train your mind to see in new ways. The real secret? It’s all about observation, patience, and the courage to look at the world with fresh eyes.
