## Introduction

Welcome to a journey that’s anything but ordinary. Imagine stepping into the bustling streets of Southeast Asia, where hope and despair are woven together, and every corner holds a new story waiting to unfold. 
 We’re about to dive into the heart of Laos, as painted by Walt Gleeson in his gripping work, **Lost in Yaba: Down and Out in Laos**. Together, we’ll wander through Vientiane’s vibrant chaos—a city alive with wanderers, expats, and locals, each carrying their own mix of burdens and dreams. 
 But there’s a shadow that lingers here—the haze of yaba, a potent methamphetamine, quietly shaping the lives of those on the margins. 
 As we walk these neon-lit nights and drift beside the lazy riverbanks, you’ll find stories of survival, addiction, and the fragile connections that form between unlikely companions. It’s a world that’s both alluring and unforgiving—where the line between adventure and disaster can disappear in an instant. 
 What drives someone to seek escape? Is it the past, pain, or something even deeper within? Through raw honesty and unflinching observation, Gleeson’s narrative pulls us right into the heart of addiction, poverty, and the universal search for connection. 
 Along the way, we’ll meet hustlers, addicts, and lost souls—people whose resilience and vulnerability show us just how strong, and how fragile, the human spirit can be. 
 So, as we set out together on this unforgettable trek through Laos, let’s keep our eyes open and our hearts curious. Because this isn’t just a story about a place—it’s an exploration of what it means to survive, to adapt, and to find meaning even when the future is uncertain.

## Chapter 1 Arriving in Laos: Searching for Escape and Belonging

**Chapter 1: Arriving in Laos—Searching for Escape and Belonging**
 
 Landing in a new country... there’s always that mix of hope and uncertainty, isn’t there? Those first few days in a foreign place—everything feels new, maybe even a little overwhelming, but there’s also this quiet belief bubbling beneath the surface that, just maybe, life could finally turn a corner.
 
 That’s the feeling stepping into Vientiane, the capital of Laos. On the outside, it really does seem to promise a slower pace, a gentler rhythm, and, for a lot of people, a real chance to start fresh.
 
 The city itself? It’s a patchwork of contradictions. You’ll see French colonial buildings right next to beautiful Buddhist temples, and the slow-moving Mekong River drifting by it all. The air is thick—not just with humidity, but with the scents of street food, and the traffic moves in this laid-back, almost chaotic way. For someone arriving from a life that’s felt rushed or maybe even a bit worn out, Vientiane’s calm can actually feel like a soothing balm.
 
 But dig a little deeper, and you’ll notice the city keeps its own secrets. The expat community is small, but it’s tight—a mix of teachers, drifters, and people who, for one reason or another, have ended up far from home. Some are running from relationships that fell apart, others from careers that just never took off, and then there are a few who are simply chasing that thrill of being somewhere completely new. There’s this camaraderie among outsiders... but also a quiet sort of loneliness that never quite disappears.
 
 One thing you notice pretty quickly? How easy it is to slip into new routines—habits you’d never imagine back home. Days blur into nights, and those clear lines between work and leisure start to vanish. Teaching English is a common gig for foreigners. It pays just enough to get by, but, honestly, not enough to really build anything lasting. The work turns repetitive, the students are polite but distant, and that sense of purpose you might have felt at first... well, it fades pretty fast.
 
 So, what do you do when emptiness creeps in? Some people turn to the city’s bars. Cheap beer, familiar faces, and the comfort of other expats—it all offers a way to escape, at least for a little while. Others find solace in daily rituals, like grabbing a coffee by the river in the morning or wandering through the night market in the evening. But for a few, the search for relief takes them down a darker road.
 
 The local drug scene is never really out of sight. Yaba—a mix of methamphetamine and caffeine—is cheap and pretty easy to find. Those little, brightly colored pills promise energy, euphoria, and a break from the routine. For anyone feeling adrift, it’s a tough temptation to resist. Usually, the first encounter comes through a friend or an acquaintance—someone who seems to have found a shortcut to happiness. The pill gets offered casually, almost like it’s no big deal. And, honestly, in that moment, the decision to try it feels pretty insignificant.
 
 But the effects hit hard and fast. Suddenly, the world sharpens, worries slip away, and—for a few hours—everything feels possible. It’s not hard to see why people fall into the habit. Yaba offers control, a way to push back against those feelings of isolation and disappointment that can come with living as a foreigner in a strange place.
 
 Most folks don’t realize how quickly the line between occasional use and real dependence can blur. What starts as a once-in-a-while escape soon becomes something you need. The city’s gentle pace, which once felt comforting, now starts to feel stifling. Those routines that used to offer comfort? They become tedious. And suddenly, searching for the next high takes on this new urgency.
 
 In the early days, it’s easy to believe you’ve still got things under control. The consequences seem far off, and the sense of community among fellow users creates this illusion of safety. But underneath it all, the seeds of addiction are already taking root. The city—with all its beauty and contradictions—becomes both a refuge... and a trap.

## Chapter 2 Slipping into Addiction: The Pull of Yaba and the Expat Margins

**Chapter Two: Slipping into Addiction—the Pull of Yaba and the Expat Margins**
 

 You know, there’s this moment when the excitement of a new city just... fades. At first, every street in Vientiane feels like adventure, every face a mystery. But after a while, routine sets in, and that routine? It slowly turns into restlessness.
 
 For a lot of folks living on the edges of the expat world, that’s when the real struggle starts. The city’s slow, gentle rhythm—once comforting—can suddenly feel like a cage. Days begin to drag, each one blending into the next, and you start searching for something—anything—to bring back that spark of meaning.

 

 Enter Yaba. With promises of energy and escape, it sneaks its way into this world almost effortlessly. It’s everywhere—easy to buy in back alleys, handed out at parties, or just passed between friends with a subtle nod. The first few times you try it, it almost feels like you’re in on a secret. Suddenly, those impossible tasks seem doable, and that heavy feeling of being alone? It lifts, just for a moment, replaced by this rush of connection and purpose.

 

 But here’s the thing—the high never lasts. When the effects fade, the emptiness creeps back in, sharper and deeper than before. And the cycle starts: use, come down, regret... and then, before you know it, that craving for another escape. Pretty soon, it’s clear—Yaba isn’t actually fixing anything. It just makes the tough moments easier to ignore, at least for a little while.

 

 The expat community feels close, but it’s kind of a fragile support system. Friendships are often built around habits rather than real connection. There’s this quiet competition—who can party harder, who can push the boundaries further. Most don’t talk about the consequences, not out loud. There’s this silent agreement to look the other way and pretend everything’s fine.

 

 As the habit grows, your world gets smaller. Your days start to revolve around finding the next pill, and everything else—work, friendships, even taking care of yourself—just slips away. Money becomes a daily worry. Teaching jobs barely cover the basics, let alone an addiction. Some start borrowing, others get desperate and turn to hustling or even petty theft. Shame builds, but so does the need.

 

 You can’t ignore what it does to your body. Sleep gets harder, appetite disappears, and you start to feel yourself falling apart. Paranoia sets in, and suddenly, the city that once felt open and safe seems threatening. And then there’s the ever-present fear of the police—foreigners caught with drugs face steep penalties, so every deal comes with a pulse of anxiety.

 

 Yet, despite the danger, the pull of Yaba stays strong. Even when you know things are slipping out of control, those moments of clarity are quickly swallowed by the next high. It becomes a pattern—hope, relapse, repeat.

 

 Still, even in the middle of all this chaos, you find these little sparks of kindness. Maybe a fellow teacher brings you a meal, or a local friend shares a story, or a stranger stops to help when your bike breaks down. These small acts—they shine bright in the darkness, little reminders that, even when things are rough, there’s still a bit of humanity holding on.

 

 But it’s tough to escape the reality. Addiction isolates. And the longer it goes on, the harder it is to see any way out. The city that once seemed so full of promise starts to feel like a maze with no exit. Out on the margins—where poverty, addiction, and desperation all meet—you start to realize there are different rules. And breaking free? That takes more than just wanting it... it takes something deeper.

## Chapter 3 Life on the Edge: Surviving in Vientiane’s Underbelly

**Chapter 3. Life on the Edge: Surviving in Vientiane’s Underbelly**
 
 Imagine waking up each day and facing a new set of choices—a tug-of-war between hunger and craving, fear and necessity, hope and resignation. That’s what life becomes when addiction tightens its grip. Suddenly, the hidden corners of Vientiane—the cheap guesthouses, those shadowy bars, the maze of backstreet markets—aren’t just places you pass by. They become your whole world.
 
 In these places, the rules are completely different. Money swaps hands with barely a word, favors get traded in whispers, and everyone’s got their own angle. You learn quickly: trust is a rare commodity, and betrayal is an everyday risk. Most folks here aren’t looking for trouble—they’re just doing whatever they can to get by.
 
 For anyone caught in that endless loop of addiction, every day is a hustle. Maybe you once had a steady teaching job, but showing up on time gets harder and harder. So you pick up whatever work you can—tutoring, translating, running the odd errand for another expat. Some drift even deeper into the shadows, taking chances they never would have dreamed of before. The craving for yaba takes over, pushing everything else aside.
 
 Living like this takes a toll that’s hard to describe. Nights without sleep blend into endless days, and your body just feels drained. Paranoia creeps in, making every conversation feel risky. And then there’s that constant fear—the worry that the police might show up, or that you’ll end up like the foreigners you hear about in stories: gone after a raid, or stuck in a jail nobody wants to think about.
 
 But even in the middle of all this chaos, there are still moments that cut through the haze. Maybe it’s a sudden memory—a family dinner, an old friend, a song you used to love. Or maybe it’s something small, like the kindness of a stranger or a child’s laughter ringing out in the street. These moments don’t last long, but they remind you that a different life is possible, if only for a second.
 
 The expat community, which once felt like a lifeline, can start to feel like a trap. Gossip travels fast, and your reputation? It’s fragile. If you fall too far, people pull away, and suddenly you’re on your own. That sense of isolation only grows, and the city—once so full of promise—starts to lose its shine.
 
 Still, even on the edge, there are small wins you hold onto. Maybe it’s a single day without using. Maybe it’s a meal you share with a friend, or a job that pays just enough to cover the next night’s bed. These tiny victories might not change everything, but they matter. Sometimes, just surviving feels like an achievement.
 
 What most people don’t see is just how easy it is to fall through the cracks. The systems that should help—embassies, charities, even local friends—are stretched thin, or sometimes just look the other way. The city moves on, barely noticing the struggles playing out on its edges.
 
 And yet, hope sticks around. No matter how tough things get, there’s always that stubborn belief that change is possible, that redemption isn’t out of reach. Over time, you realize survival is about more than just getting by. It’s about holding onto the possibility of something better, even when it feels so far away.

## Chapter 4 Cultural Gaps and Misunderstandings: Navigating Life as an Outsider

Chapter 4. Cultural Gaps and Misunderstandings: Navigating Life as an Outsider
 
 
 Living in a foreign country always comes with its own set of challenges, but in Laos, those cultural gaps can run even deeper than most people expect. The city moves to a rhythm shaped by centuries of tradition and a blend of influences that can feel almost impossible to fully grasp, especially if you’re new to it all.
 

 The very first barrier you’ll hit? Language. Even the simplest conversation can turn into a real exercise in patience, as words and meanings just seem to slip through your fingers. Most expats end up cobbling together a mix of broken Lao, English, and hand gestures to get through the day. Misunderstandings happen all the time, and honestly, it’s easy to feel a bit foolish or out of place.
 

 But it’s not just about what you say—or try to say. The differences dig deeper, into social norms and values that might clash with everything you’ve grown up with. In Laos, there’s a deep respect for elders, a strong deference to authority, and a real preference for harmony over confrontation. If you’re used to speaking your mind or challenging the rules, you might find yourself feeling confused or even isolated.
 

 Then there’s the expat community itself. As diverse as it is, it can sometimes turn into a bit of an echo chamber. Stories about awkward run-ins with the police, misunderstandings with landlords, or little conflicts at work start to circulate. And before you know it, these stories shape how you see the place—making that sense of being on the outside feel even stronger.
 

 Of course, it’s not all obstacles and awkward moments. There are these beautiful flashes of real connection—a shopkeeper who remembers your favorite snack, a neighbor who invites you to their family’s celebration, a student who opens up about their dreams. Those moments are gold. They remind you that the barriers aren’t permanent; they can be crossed, even if just for a moment.
 

 Still, cultural dislocation can make personal struggles feel even heavier. That constant sense of not really belonging makes it harder to ask for help or show vulnerability. Pride and shame get in the way, and the temptation to retreat into old comforts—maybe drinking, using drugs, or just isolating yourself—becomes much harder to resist.
 

 Most of us don’t realize just how much we lean on the routines and comforts of home until we’re suddenly without them. Missing familiar foods, holidays, even the weather can make small problems feel huge. And that city, which once seemed so enchanting and full of promise, can suddenly feel distant and unreachable.
 

 But here’s what’s important: these challenges can teach us something. Patience, humility, and a genuine openness can become your lifelines. If you’re willing to listen, to observe, and to adapt, you’ll find yourself bridging those gaps—maybe not every day, but sometimes. And those moments of connection? They make it all worth it.

## Chapter 5 Hitting Bottom: Despair, Reflection, and the Flicker of Hope

Chapter 5: Hitting Bottom—Despair, Reflection, and the Flicker of Hope.
 
 There comes a time when the weight of addiction, that feeling of being cut off from the world, and the ache of being far from home... it all just becomes too much. The routines that once felt safe? Suddenly, they're more like shackles. Even the city, with its gentle rhythm, starts to feel suffocating.
 
 For so many people, this is the turning point—the moment when just getting by gives way to a deep sense of despair.
 
 The warning signs are hard to ignore. Money dries up. Friends slowly disappear. Your body, once resilient, starts to give in. And that fear—the fear of getting caught, of getting sick, or even facing something worse—it’s always there, whispering in the background. Nights become a blur, spent in cheap guesthouses or sometimes just out on the street. The future? It feels impossibly far away.
 
 But in those darkest hours, something happens. Self-reflection, whether you want it or not, creeps in. Memories from another life—family gatherings, old friends, dreams you once held close—they surface. Sometimes they offer a bit of comfort. Other times, they sting. Realizing you've gone too far can be terrifying, yet there’s a strange freedom in admitting it. Most people don’t reach this place without putting up a fight, but once you’re here, there’s no turning back.
 
 Despair can feel like it’s going to swallow you whole. But here’s something to remember: it can also be the spark that pushes you to change. Recognizing that you just can’t go on like this cracks the door open, even if it’s just a sliver. For some, that means picking up the phone to call a family member, reaching out to a local charity, or simply sharing what’s going on with a trusted friend. For others, it’s about the small wins—eating a real meal, getting a little sleep, or just making it through one day without using.
 
 The road to recovery isn’t a straight line. Relapses happen. The urge to throw in the towel? It’s always lurking. But those little victories—they’re huge. A single day without drugs, a conversation that makes you smile, a moment of genuine laughter... these are the building blocks of something new.
 
 Most folks don’t realize just how much strength it takes to start over. The process is slow, and yes, setbacks happen. But with every step, no matter how small, the sense of possibility grows. The city, once a place you wanted to escape, starts to feel like home again. The beauty you couldn’t see before, hidden behind all that pain, begins to shine through. And you start searching for meaning once more.

## Chapter 6 The First Steps Toward Recovery: Facing the Mirror

Chapter 6: The First Steps Toward Recovery — Facing the Mirror.
 
 Picture this. You wake up after a long, exhausting night, and somehow, the world just feels different. There’s this heaviness in the air, almost as if everything’s shifted. For most people, that first real step toward recovery isn’t some big, dramatic moment. Instead, it’s quiet—a private moment of honesty, standing alone in front of a mirror, staring at a face that feels unfamiliar.
 
 You might notice that the person looking back at you seems older, more worn out. But look closely—there’s a tiny spark of determination in those tired eyes.
 
 Admitting you need help? That’s tough. Pride and shame have a way of hanging around, whispering that it’s not really so bad, that you can handle it on your own. But your body? It tells another story. Maybe your hands won’t stop shaking, your cheeks look a bit hollow, or your heart is racing for no reason at all. And your mind? It’s busy, restless—chasing regrets and what-ifs that just won’t let go.
 
 One afternoon, you find yourself sitting in a faded café by the river. And there, in that ordinary moment, you make a decision. It isn’t bold or dramatic. It’s just a quiet promise to yourself to try something different this time. Maybe you reach out to a friend you haven’t seen in months—or you walk into a local support group, not really sure what you’ll find.
 
 Those first conversations? They’re awkward. There’s a lot of silence, and it’s hard to find the right words. But even so, there’s relief in simply being heard. You realize you’re not alone—others have walked this path, too.
 
 A teacher who always seemed distant suddenly offers a listening ear. A local social worker sits down with you, patiently explaining how withdrawal and recovery actually work. The process feels huge—there are no shortcuts, no easy way out. Cravings come in waves, sometimes so strong it’s hard to breathe, and that old temptation to give in is always lurking nearby. But every day you make it without using? That’s a real win. It’s proof that change is possible.
 
 Most people don’t see how much courage it takes just to ask for help. The world keeps moving, barely noticing your struggle. But inside, something’s changing. The routines that once revolved around chasing the next high are slowly replaced with new habits—simple things, like morning walks, cooking a basic meal, or winding down with a book in the evening. The city, which used to feel like a maze filled with temptation, starts to reveal its gentler side.
 
 Of course, there are setbacks. Some days, the urge to escape is just too much, and you might fall back into old habits. But now, the difference is, you’re willing to start again. You forgive yourself for slipping, and you keep moving forward. The support from friends, counselors, even people you barely know, becomes a lifeline—a reminder that you don’t have to do this alone.
 
 And here’s what really matters: recovery isn’t about trying to erase your past. The memories—both good and bad—are still there. But they don’t have the power to control your present anymore. Every single day is another chance to build something new, to rediscover the parts of life that addiction tried to steal away.

## Chapter 7 Rebuilding Trust: Mending Relationships and Finding Community

Chapter 7: Rebuilding Trust – Mending Relationships and Finding Community
 
 Trust... it’s such a delicate thing, isn’t it? So easy to break, and yet, it takes what feels like forever to really build it back up. After addiction, the hardest work often isn’t just about staying sober—it’s about patching up the relationships that got strained or even snapped along the way.
 
 Maybe you’ve noticed that friends who used to check in have started to drift away. They’ve heard too many promises that didn’t stick, too many missed calls. And family—sometimes they’re far off, worrying quietly, not sure what’s real anymore.
 
 Reaching out? That’s tough. There’s always that fear—you might get rejected, or maybe you’ll run into anger or disappointment. But here’s the thing: sometimes, just sending a simple message—a text, an email, even a short letter—can crack open the door to forgiveness. The first conversations are awkward, maybe a little shaky, full of apologies and explanations. There’s no promise you’ll be welcomed back, but just taking that first step... it’s a kind of healing all its own.
 
 Imagine this: a former colleague, someone you used to grab drinks with, suddenly becomes a cheerleader for your recovery. Or a friend who watched you struggle from a distance—one day they decide to offer you another shot. Even the expat community, with all its quirks and flaws, can surprise you. Maybe someone invites you to a meal, or points you toward a job, or offers you a couch for a few nights when you need it most.
 
 And it’s not just about fixing old friendships. Recovery has a way of creating new connections, too. Support groups—whether they’re official meetings or just a handful of people grabbing coffee—turn into lifelines. When you sit with others and trade stories, you start to realize: everyone’s carrying something heavy. The details change, but those feelings—shame, fear, hope—those are the same for all of us.
 
 There’s a real comfort in seeing that you can fit in again. The city, which once felt like a place you were just passing through, slowly starts to feel more like home. Maybe a neighbor waves, or the shopkeeper remembers your usual order, or someone at work asks for your advice. These little things—they start weaving a new sense of belonging, thread by thread.
 
 Of course, not every relationship can be saved. Some friends may move on, and some family ties might stay a bit distant or tense. And while those losses hurt, they’re also reminders—of what addiction can cost, and of how much the connections you rebuild are truly worth.
 
 Most people don’t realize just how much strength it takes to trust again. Not just trusting others, but learning to trust yourself, too. The journey is slow, full of setbacks and doubts. But every single step matters. Bit by bit, those old walls built by addiction start to crumble. And in their place, something new and stronger takes shape—a sense of community, of being seen and valued, imperfections and all.

## Chapter 8 Redemption and Purpose: Finding Meaning Beyond Survival

**Chapter Eight: Redemption and Purpose—Finding Meaning Beyond Survival.**
 
 After months of just trying to get by, the big question naturally changes.
 
 Instead of asking, “How do I survive?” you start to wonder, “What comes next?”
 
 The routines of recovery—daily meetings, healthier meals, quiet nights—bring a sense of steadiness. But honestly, they can also spark a new kind of restlessness. There’s this longing for purpose, for something meaningful to fill the space addiction once took up.
 
 For a lot of people, that means finding their way back to old passions. Imagine dusting off a battered guitar that’s been ignored for years, finally letting your fingers find the strings again in the hush of a rented room. Or flipping open an old sketchbook and filling page after page with drawings—the city’s temples at sunrise, kids laughing by the river, or the maze of the night market after dark. That creative spark, dulled for so long, starts to flicker back to life—maybe not all at once, but in little, hopeful bursts.
 
 Others discover purpose in helping those who are still struggling. Sometimes it’s a chance meeting—a young teacher, a lost traveler, a local kid at a crossroads. Suddenly, you’re in the position to offer support. The hard lessons learned in your darkest moments become a well of wisdom, something you can share to help someone else through their own storm.
 
 And then there’s work. What once felt like just a way to pay the bills—say, teaching English—starts to matter so much more. You find yourself putting care into each lesson, celebrating when your students succeed, and realizing the classroom isn’t just a job anymore. It’s a place for connection and growth. That missing sense of purpose finally begins to return.
 
 Of course, it isn’t all smooth sailing. There are days when the old emptiness sneaks back in, and the urge to escape feels almost overwhelming. But now? You’ve got tools—friends who’ll pick up the phone, routines that ground you, and the powerful memory of how far you’ve come. The city around you, with all its contradictions, turns into a source of inspiration instead of something that drags you down.
 
 Most people overlook just how much meaning hides in the small things—a shared laugh, a student’s proud moment, a peaceful evening with friends. The search for redemption? It’s not about huge, dramatic changes. It’s about finding worth in the everyday, and slowly building a life that feels truly worth living.
 
 And maybe the biggest realization is this: the journey out of addiction is really a journey toward accepting yourself. You can’t change the past, but you can understand it. You can even forgive it. The scars remain, but they don’t get to tell your whole story anymore. Each new day is a blank page—a chance to write a new chapter, and to find purpose in simply moving forward.

## Chapter 9 The Ongoing Struggle: Temptation, Relapse, and Resilience

Chapter 9: The Ongoing Struggle—Temptation, Relapse, and Resilience.
 
 Recovery isn’t a straight line, is it? Even after months—or years—of progress, those old habits can sneak back up on you when you least expect it. Maybe it’s a rough day at work. Or you bump into someone from your past. Sometimes, it’s just a wave of loneliness that hits out of nowhere.
 
 Living in the city doesn’t make it any easier. There’s always easy access, and the culture of secrecy keeps that danger lurking right around the corner.
 
 Picture this: It’s Friday night. You’ve survived a tough week, and suddenly, temptation feels almost unbearable. Your mind starts spinning with excuses—just this once, just to take the edge off. You pick up your phone, dial that old, familiar number... then, at the very last second, you hang up. The urge fades. Relief washes over you, and you feel this quiet pride for choosing to stay strong.
 
 But let’s be honest—sometimes, that line gets crossed. Maybe you slip. Relapse hits, fast and heavy, and the guilt feels crushing. The fear of losing everything comes roaring back. But now, things are different. You’ve got tools you didn’t have before. You call a friend. You show up at a meeting. You talk to your counselor. You face the setback head-on, but it doesn’t erase all the progress you’ve made.
 
 Most people have no idea how common relapse really is—or how much courage it takes to start over. Shame can feel overwhelming, but it’s not the end of your story. Each time you choose recovery again, it gets just a little bit easier. The path gets clearer.
 
 And you know, the city itself becomes a strange mix of challenge and support. The same streets that once led you into trouble now offer reminders of how far you’ve come. Maybe it’s a favorite café where you feel safe, or a friendly face on your walk, or a quiet park where you can breathe. The routines that used to feel like a burden? Now they give you structure and a sense of comfort.
 
 As you keep going, you start to notice your own triggers and patterns. You get better at spotting the moments that put you at risk. So you come up with strategies—avoiding certain places, reaching out for help, practicing a bit of mindfulness when things get rough. Recovery isn’t a one-time thing. It’s a daily decision to stay on course.
 
 And here’s what truly matters: Recovery isn’t about getting it perfect. It’s about resilience. It’s about showing up for yourself, again and again, no matter how many times you wander off the path. The journey’s never really over, but every single day brings a new chance for growth—and for healing.
