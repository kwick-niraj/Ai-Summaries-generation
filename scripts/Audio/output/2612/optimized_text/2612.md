## Introduction

Introduction

In the shadow of unimaginable violence, the Rwandan genocide stands as a haunting testament to the depths of human cruelty and the resilience of those who survive. We Wish to Inform You That Tomorrow We Will Be Killed with Our Families by Philip Gourevitch immerses itself in the aftermath of this tragedy, tracing the stories of survivors, perpetrators, and bystanders as they navigate the fractured landscape left behind. The narrative unfolds through personal testimonies and historical context, revealing the complex interplay of memory, justice, and reconciliation in a society struggling to rebuild itself from the ashes of atrocity.

As the layers of Rwanda’s recent history are peeled back, the intricate web of political, social, and personal forces that fueled the genocide comes into focus. The journey through these accounts is both harrowing and illuminating, offering a window into the enduring scars and the fragile hope that persist in the wake of mass violence. Through Gourevitch’s lens, the search for meaning, accountability, and healing becomes a universal quest, inviting reflection on the capacity for both evil and redemption within communities and individuals alike.

## Chapter 1 A Country on the Edge: Everyday Life Before the Storm



Chapter 2 The Descent into Violence: How Genocide Unfolded

When the violence finally erupted, it was both shocking and, in a way, unsurprising. The assassination of the president was the spark, but the kindling had been laid for years. In the hours that followed, the country seemed to unravel at the seams. Roadblocks sprang up overnight, manned by men with machetes and lists of names. The radio, once a source of comfort, became a tool of terror, urging listeners to do their “duty” and turn on their neighbors.

In one neighborhood, a mother tried to calm her children as shouts echoed down the street. She had heard stories of what was happening elsewhere, but it was only when the violence reached her own door that the reality set in. The people she had known for years—people she had shared meals with, whose children had played with hers—now looked at her with suspicion, their faces hard and unfamiliar.

The speed and scale of the killings were almost unimaginable. In the space of a few weeks, hundreds of thousands were murdered—men, women, and children, often by people they knew. The tools of death were simple: machetes, clubs, and sometimes guns. The killers were not faceless monsters but ordinary people—farmers, teachers, shopkeepers—driven by fear, hatred, or the simple pressure to conform.

Some tried to resist. A priest opened his church to those seeking refuge, believing that the sanctity of the place would protect them. For a time, it did. But eventually, the killers came, and the walls that had once offered safety became a trap. Survivors later spoke of the silence that followed, a silence filled with the weight of loss and betrayal.

Elsewhere, a young man hid in a neighbor’s attic, listening as the search parties passed below. He counted the footsteps, the shouts, the sudden silences. Each day was a test of endurance, a battle against hunger, fear, and the urge to give up. He survived, but the cost was more than he could have imagined.

The machinery of genocide was both organized and chaotic. Lists were drawn up, names checked off, and bodies disposed of with chilling efficiency. Yet, in the midst of the horror, there were moments of confusion and hesitation. Some refused to kill, risking their own lives to protect others. A few managed to smuggle friends to safety, hiding them in cellars or leading them through the hills under cover of darkness.

For most, though, the choice was stark: participate or perish. The pressure to conform was immense, and the consequences of refusal were often fatal. The violence fed on itself, growing more brutal as the days passed. The world, watching from a distance, struggled to comprehend the scale of the atrocity. News reports spoke of “tribal conflict” and “civil war,” missing the reality of a planned, systematic campaign to wipe out an entire people.

In the aftermath, survivors emerged from hiding, blinking in the harsh light of a world forever changed. The streets were littered with the evidence of what had happened—shattered homes, abandoned belongings, and the unspoken grief of those who remained. The question that lingered was simple but impossible to answer: how could this have happened?

## Chapter 5 Bearing Witness: The Power and Limits of Storytelling



Chapter 6 The Long Road to Justice: Trials, Gacaca, and the Weight of Truth

After the dust settled, Rwanda faced a question that would haunt any society: how do you seek justice when the crime is so vast, and the wounds so fresh? The country’s jails overflowed with suspects—tens of thousands accused of participating in the genocide. The formal courts, battered and understaffed, could barely process a fraction of the cases. It was as if the entire nation was on trial, and the world was watching.

In the capital, a judge sat behind a battered desk, files stacked high, each one a story of loss and accusation. He listened as survivors recounted what they had seen, their voices sometimes trembling, sometimes fierce. The accused, often neighbors or former friends, sat in silence, eyes fixed on the floor. The process was slow, and the backlog grew with each passing month.

But in the villages, a different kind of justice was taking shape. The government revived the old tradition of gacaca—community courts where ordinary people would gather under the open sky to hear confessions, accusations, and pleas for forgiveness. It was a system born of necessity, but also of hope: that truth-telling could help heal what had been broken.

One afternoon, in a small clearing, villagers gathered on wooden benches. A woman stood to speak, her hands clasped tightly. She named the men who had come to her house, who had taken her husband. The accused, sitting nearby, did not look up. The air was thick with tension, but also with something else—a sense that, for the first time, the truth was being spoken aloud.

Some confessed, their voices barely above a whisper. They spoke of fear, of pressure, of choices made in the heat of terror. Others denied everything, insisting they had only watched, or had tried to help. The community listened, weighed the words, and decided on punishment or forgiveness. It was not perfect—there were mistakes, false accusations, and old grudges—but for many, it was better than silence.

For survivors, the process was both cathartic and painful. To see the faces of those who had harmed them, to hear apologies or denials, was to relive the trauma. But it also offered a chance to reclaim some measure of agency, to be heard in a world that had so often ignored their suffering.

The international community watched with skepticism. Some argued that gacaca was too rough, too prone to error. Others saw it as a bold experiment in restorative justice, a way to rebuild trust in a society torn apart. For Rwanda, it was a path forward—imperfect, but necessary.

Over time, the gacaca courts closed, their work done. Some wounds had begun to heal, others remained raw. The search for justice was never complete, but the act of seeking it had changed the country. It became clear that justice, in the aftermath of such horror, was not just about punishment, but about truth, memory, and the possibility of forgiveness.
