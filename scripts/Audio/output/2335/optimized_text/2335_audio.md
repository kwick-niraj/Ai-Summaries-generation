## Chapter 7 Point of View—The Lens of the Story

**Chapter Seven: Point of View—The Lens of the Story**
 
 Let’s take a moment to think about how every story you’ve ever loved was shaped by the point of view it was told from.
 
 Point of view is like the lens of a camera—it decides what you see, how you feel about it, and even what remains out of focus.
 
 When you’re learning to write, figuring out point of view is a bit like adjusting that camera’s focus. Each setting reveals something new, and sometimes, shifts the whole mood of the story.
 
 There are a few familiar points of view that pop up again and again, each bringing its own flavor to a story.
 
 First, there’s **first person**. Here, a character tells the tale using “I.” It’s intimate and immediate—you’re right there, inside their head and heart. But, you only get what they know and feel, nothing more.
 
 Then, we have **third person limited**. The narrator sticks close to just one character, letting you peek into their thoughts and experiences, but still keeping a little distance—like you’re looking over their shoulder.
 
 With **third person omniscient**, the narrator knows everything—every character’s secrets, every twist and turn. This point of view lets the story stretch out, giving you a bird’s-eye view of the world and everyone in it.
 
 And sometimes, you’ll come across **second person**. The story talks directly to you, calling you “you,” and suddenly, you’re in the middle of the action, as if you’re living the events yourself.
 
 If you pay close attention as a reader, you’ll start to notice how point of view shapes your whole experience. You might find yourself asking: Whose eyes am I looking through? What am I allowed to see, and what’s hidden from me? And maybe, how does the narrator’s voice make me feel about what’s happening?
 
 There are a few handy strategies to help you dig deeper into point of view as you read or write.
 
 Try tracking when the narrative shifts—does the focus jump from one character to another? How does that change the way the story feels?
 
 Think about reliability, too. Is the narrator someone you can trust, or is their perspective a little skewed? Sometimes, the fun is in not knowing for sure.
 
 And take a moment to notice how close or distant the narration feels. Are you drawn right into a character’s mind, or kept at arm’s length? That distance can totally change your emotional connection to the story.
 
 Writers love to experiment with point of view—trying out different narrators, playing with perspective, and seeing which approach really brings the story to life. The more stories you read, the more you’ll notice how point of view creates intimacy, builds suspense, or delivers a surprise when you least expect it.
 
 So here’s the heart of it: Point of view isn’t just a technical detail. It’s a core part of what makes a story work. When you learn to read and write with an awareness of perspective, you unlock the power to shape your stories in ways that truly move your readers.
